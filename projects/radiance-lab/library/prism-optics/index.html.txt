These scenes separate the two.</p><section><h2>Why can one object appear many times?</h2><p>At each glass–air interface, Fresnel reflection creates a reflected route alongside the transmitted route. From inside glass, angles above the critical angle produce total internal reflection: θc(λ) = asin(1/n(λ)), about 41° for n = 1.52 in air. Several planar faces can therefore show repeated virtual images of room edges and emitters.</p><p>Unfolding each reflection into a mirrored copy of the room makes the paths straight. A rectangular mirror room produces a lattice of virtual rooms; a prism produces a pattern determined by its face angles. Glass also transmits light, so it is only mirror-like for some directions. Finite bounce limits truncate this pattern.</p><p>The grid in the supplied screenshots is not, by itself, proof of this effect: editor lines and geometry mismatches must also be excluded. The original rotated-box scene had enlarged analytical half-extents even though rotation was already applied separately; this mismatch with the visible mesh is corrected. Disable overlays when interpreting the rendered image. These geometric-optics lines are not diffraction or holographic fringes.</p><p><a href="https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-3/pages/2-1-images-formed-by-plane-mirrors">Plane mirrors and virtual images · OpenStax</a></p></section><section><h2>Four controlled scenes</h2><p><strong>129 · Inside glass:</strong> start inside a large glass block, with asymmetric colored markers outside. Rotate to compare transmitted views and internally reflected images. This is an optically immersed camera probe; an air cavity around a real camera is not modeled.</p><p><strong>130 · Mirror room:</strong> six reflective walls and asymmetric luminous markers reveal repeated rooms. Reflectance is 0.96, so long paths fade. A 32-surface-bounce budget keeps the experiment finite.</p><p><strong>131 · Prism bench:</strong> a narrow broadband white source illuminates a triangular glass extrusion and a white screen. The three side faces refract light; opaque end plates close the fixture. Camera-connection light tracing follows light through glass onto the wall.</p><p><strong>132 · Prism bench with mist:</strong> the same setup adds dilute homogeneous scattering. The fog reveals the paths; it does not create the wavelength separation.</p></section><section><h2>A laser is not automatically a rainbow source</h2><p>A monochromatic laser remains one color. Here the source is a narrow <em>white-light beam</em>. Wavelength-dependent refractive index separates its colors at nonparallel faces. A parallel-sided glass slab generally leaves the emerging direction parallel to the incident direction, with wavelength-dependent lateral displacement instead.</p><p>The original scene uses a rectangular prism and exaggerated dispersion (spread 12). The new bench uses a triangular cross-section and spread 1. They answer different questions: interesting reflected views versus a simple spectrum on a screen. A very broad source, excessive angular spread or a short screen distance can make the colors overlap again.</p><p><a href="https://www.physicsclassroom.com/class/refrn/lesson-4/dispersion-of-light-by-prisms">Dispersion by prisms · The Physics Classroom</a></p></section><section><h2>Implementation and interpretation</h2><p>Naive and camera-connection light tracing now sample a cone source directly within its angular support, using p(ω) = 1/[2π(1 − cos α)]. Throughput retains the surface cosine and divides by this density. Previously, a 0.2° cone accepted only about 0.0012% of cosine-weighted launch directions. This change improves sampling efficiency; it does not widen the source or invent extra light. BDPT retains its existing launch proposal and density evaluation.</p><p>The physical-dispersion wall spot is deliberately small. Walk closer to the screen to inspect it; the scene is not an exaggerated full-wall rainbow. The fog scene exposes the incident and outgoing beam paths.
