      <p class="sec-label">05d — When does an estimator work well</p>
      <h2>The variance has exactly three sources</h2>
      <p class="lead">"Planes work when they face you" is true and useless. The systematic version is a closed-form Monte Carlo of every primitive's weight over the crossings a camera actually sees — 22 estimators, four parameter families, every number cited to a run.</p>
      <p>Each analytic primitive samples some photon dimensions with a density <code>p</code>, sweeps two more into a sheet, and meets the camera ray at a crossing with weight</p>
    </div>
    <div class="formula">
      <div class="f">W = f / ( p · J ),   J = |(∂x/∂α × ∂x/∂β) · d|</div>
      <div class="n">and W = 0 when the sheet misses the ray. The per-pixel relative variance then comes from three places, and every finding in the study is one of them.</div>
    </div>
    <div class="narrow">
      <ul class="clean">
        <li><b>Miss mass.</b> With crossing probability <code>P</code>, the zero point-mass alone contributes <code>(1 − P)/P</code>. Measured as the <em>whole</em> story for the UV quad (rel std = 1.05–1.16 × √(1/P − 1) on 90% of pixels) and as the exponential wall of the photon sphere in dense fog.</li>
        <li><b>How J vanishes.</b> Constant across a pixel: a grazing band <em>between</em> pixels, no in-pixel tail. Linearly with the sampled coordinate (the planes): an integrable tail, mean converges everywhere. As the square root — a <strong>fold</strong>, two quadric roots merging (sphere, cone, and every hourglass): <code>W ~ ε^{−1/2}</code>, tail index 2, and <code>E[W²]</code> is <em>log-divergent</em>. The sample variance of a fold member never converges (the sphere's grows 5.2 → 20.1 from 10³ to 1.6·10⁷ samples) and must never be read as a convergence gauge. A Cauchy fan (the disk): bounded, but silent on two exact rays.</li>
        <li><b>Whether p tracks f.</b> Phase function, transmittance and emitter cosine are common multipliers — <em>unless</em> the phase peak sits on a member's singular locus. The same <code>g = 0.8</code> divides the sphere's variance by 23 and multiplies the disk's by 11 on identical Jacobian histograms.</li>
      </ul>
    </div>

      <h3>The hourglass verdict, by light size</h3>
      <p>The efficiency question between the two seeds has an answer, and it is not the Jacobian. The vertex members are <strong>light-trace-limited</strong>: at the baseline waist their light half succeeds on 1–2% of roots, and that is the whole 30–90× gap in coverage below. Their Jacobian term is actually 2–3× <em>smaller</em> than 20's, with no axial pathology.</p>

      <p class="note"><b>What the numbers say to do next.</b> Not MIS between the seeds — their densities live in different measures and the vertex members rarely have a root where 20 does. Importance-sample the light half instead: aim <code>θ</code> at the emitter and the 1–2% success rate is the entire gap. The medium, meanwhile, is irrelevant to the whole hourglass family (coverage 0.738 at every σ<sub>t</sub>).</p>

    <div class="narrow">
      <p>Six of the fifteen rules, each with its measurement in <code>docs/variance/README.md</code>: a single swept plane degrades like <code>sin(γ)^{−3}</code> in its angle to the camera; the three-plane mixture caps the worst pixel at 1.5 rel std in every viewing direction; never use the random-orientation plane when the continuous mixture exists (same samples, 1.6–2.2× better); the cone has an exact on/off criterion for bounded variance; the medium density matters only to members that sample a distance from it; and epsilon guards are irrelevant to variance — acceptance geometry and mixtures are what keep it finite.</p>
      <p>The study also records where its own Monte Carlo means did not match quadrature — a −0.7% cone clamp bias, the beam's kernel bias at the axial caustic, and a fold-tailed running mean sitting 31% high at 4·10⁵ samples before settling — so nobody has to rediscover them.</p>
