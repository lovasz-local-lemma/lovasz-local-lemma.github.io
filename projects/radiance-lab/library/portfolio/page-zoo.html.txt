      <p class="sec-label">01 — What is actually in the box</p>
      <h2>Ten modes, one enum, seven enable flags</h2>
      <p class="lead">Mode selection is a single integer, <span class="mono">m_ui.integratorMode</span>, dispatched at <span class="mono">EngineRender.cpp:2005-2265</span>. Modes 0-2 always run the GPU path tracer. Modes 3-9 each carry a second boolean — <span class="mono">vcmEnabled</span>, <span class="mono">mltEnabled</span>, <span class="mono">pssEnabled</span>, <span class="mono">upbpEnabled</span>, <span class="mono">pgEnabled</span>, <span class="mono">meEnabled</span>, <span class="mono">ppEnabled</span> — and <b>every one of them is declared false</b> (<span class="mono">UIManager.h:379-478</span>). What ticks them is a separate control one panel above: <b>Auto-enable on switch</b>, which defaults <em>on</em>, so completing a mode switch turns that mode's integrator on for you. An earlier draft of this page said selecting mode 4 without ticking its box silently falls back to the GPU tracer. The declaration is right and the conclusion was wrong: at defaults you get MLT. Turn auto-enable off and the fallback is real — and it is not quite silent either, because the Integrators HUD writes "(Tracer for viewing)" after the mode's name — in the HUD's own one-based numbering, which calls this mode 5. That HUD also defaults off, so the honest sentence is that <b>the mode number is a request, and whether anything honours it depends on a checkbox in a different panel</b>.</p>
      <p>That is the first thing to know about this zoo: the mode number is a request, not a state. The second is that the seven CPU integrators share exactly three things — the scene, the camera, and <span class="mono">intersectScene</span>. They do not share a film, an RNG, a light-sampling routine, a BSDF evaluator or a tone-mapper. Each one carries its own copy, written at a different time, and the copies have drifted.</p>

    <div class="narrow">
      <p class="sec-label">02 — The BDPT pyramid</p>
      <h2>A grid you can click a cell out of and watch the image lose exactly one kind of light</h2>
      <p class="lead">Bidirectional path tracing is not one estimator. It is a family of them, indexed by how many vertices come from the light and how many from the camera, all summed with MIS weights. That family is usually invisible: you get the combined image and take the decomposition on faith. Here it is a draggable panel with a clickable cell per technique, sitting over a real-time BDPT that implements all six (s,t) families — one of very few open real-time codebases that do — and two clicks reproduce Veach's figure inside one running render. It is the explanation of multiple importance sampling that no diagram can give: not a picture of the decomposition, the decomposition itself, live.</p>
      <p>Light strategy 5 turns it on. The overlay lives at <span class="mono">EngineRenderViz.cpp:1859 drawBDPTPyramidOverlay</span>; the filter it writes, <span class="mono">bdptFilterS</span> / <span class="mono">bdptFilterT</span>, reaches the shader as <span class="mono">uBDPTFilterS/T</span> and is tested by two short functions in <span class="mono">shaders/modules/pt_bdpt.glsl</span> — <span class="mono">bdptPass(s,t)</span> at <span class="mono">:53</span> for ordinary contributions and <span class="mono">bdptSplatPass(s,t,px)</span> at <span class="mono">:68</span> for light-tracer splats. Between them they are called at <b>22 sites</b> in <span class="mono">pathTrace.comp</span> — thirteen and nine — and those 22 are every place in the megakernel where a contribution is added: emitter hits on the eye path, both NEE branches, the light-to-camera splats, the light-to-camera connects, and the interior-connection loop. Isolating a technique is therefore exact rather than approximate; the other 21 sites return early.</p>

    <div class="paircap"><b>The grid, in the panel's own orientation</b><span>Columns are <span class="mono">l</span>, rows are <span class="mono">e</span> — <span class="mono">bdptTileS = px.x / tileW</span>, <span class="mono">bdptTileT = px.y / tileH</span> at <span class="mono">pathTrace.comp:3639-3640</span>, and the overlay draws the same way, "<span class="mono">l:</span>" across the top at <span class="mono">EngineRenderViz.cpp:2084</span> and "<span class="mono">e0…e5</span>" down the side at <span class="mono">:2107</span>. Every cell on one anti-diagonal builds a path of the same length by a different route, so those are the techniques MIS has to weight against each other. The <span class="mono">e=1</span> row is where caustics arrive; the <span class="mono">l=0</span> column is what an ordinary path tracer finds on its own.</span></div>

    <div class="narrow">
      <h3>Three ways to look at one grid</h3>
      <p>The pyramid has a <b>tile mode</b> with three settings, chosen by the <span class="mono">[T]</span> button on its own title bar. <b>Off</b> gives one full-screen image filtered to a single cell. <b>Tiled</b> divides the film into an <span class="mono">(l,e)</span> grid and lets each cell accept only its own technique, so the scene is cut into a technique checkerboard. <b>Shrunken</b> remaps the camera UV per tile (<span class="mono">pathTrace.comp:3649-3657</span>) so every tile is the <i>whole</i> scene rendered by one technique — a contact sheet of the decomposition. Light-tracer splats are remapped into the matching tile by eight lines inside <span class="mono">bdptSplatPass</span> (<span class="mono">pt_bdpt.glsl:75-82</span>), which is what stops a caustic splat from landing in the wrong cell.</p>
      <p>Two affordances make the panel teach rather than merely filter. Hovering a cell highlights <b>every cell on the same anti-diagonal</b>, in the panel and in the tiled viewport at once (<span class="mono">EngineRenderViz.cpp:2153</span> and <span class="mono">:2314</span>) — the UI draws "MIS mixes across a diagonal" for you instead of asserting it. And each cell carries prose written in the renderer's own voice, assembled at <span class="mono">:1918-1975</span>: <i>"Useless for small/occluded lights"</i>, <i>"Nearly useless unless aperture is huge"</i>, <i>"Excellent for caustics (L-&gt;S*-&gt;D-&gt;E)"</i>.</p>
      <p><b>One correction to this project's own shooting notes.</b> <span class="mono">docs/CAPTURE-SHOOTING-LIST.md:564</span> calls shrunken mode "expensive — it renders the scene once per cell". It does not. Every pixel still traces its full eye subpath and its light subpaths and then <i>discards</i> every contribution that does not match its tile, so a shrunken frame costs about what an ordinary BDPT frame costs. What you lose is samples per technique — one forty-ninth of the film at the default 7×7 grid. The constraint is convergence time, not frame rate, which is why the recipes below ask for a lot of passes and not for patience with the viewport.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="narrow">
      <h3>Two cells, one anti-diagonal, and the reason MIS exists</h3>
      <p>Preset 40 is a purpose-built Veach rig — four metal plates at roughness <span class="mono">.02 / .07 / .18 / .40</span> in a staircase, five emissive spheres from radius <span class="mono">.90</span> down to <span class="mono">.04</span> at matched power, each plate's normal computed as the half-vector between the camera position and the light row. The direct reflection off a plate is a three-vertex path, and exactly two cells of the pyramid can build it: <b>(l=0, e=3)</b>, where the eye path bounces off the plate and happens to hit an emitter, which is BSDF sampling; and <b>(l=1, e=2)</b>, where the eye path stops on the plate and sends a shadow ray, which is next-event estimation. Same anti-diagonal, <span class="mono">l+e = 3</span>, circled in the figure above.</p>
      <p>The complementarity is designed into the rig and it is visible without measuring anything. Cell (0,3) is clean on the sharp plate under the big light and catastrophic on the rough plate under the tiny one, because BSDF sampling has to hit a small emitter by luck. Cell (1,2) is noisy in exactly the opposite places, because a shadow ray to a large light is a poor match for a nearly specular lobe. Neither is usable alone. Their weighted sum is clean across all four plates. That is the whole of multiple importance sampling on one screen, and here you produce it by clicking two cells of one running render — no change of integrator, no change of scene, and accumulation reset on each click so the noise you are looking at is that technique's own.</p>
    </div>

      <h3>And the half almost no renderer shows: the weights themselves</h3>
      <p>The decomposition above answers "why two techniques". It does not answer "why <i>weight</i> them rather than average them", and that is the part the pyramid can actually display. The <span class="mono">[M]</span> button cycles the display through <b>raw → weighted → weight-only</b> (<span class="mono">EngineRenderViz.cpp:2004-2013</span>). In a per-strategy view, <span class="mono">applyBDPTMIS</span> returns the contribution, or the contribution times its weight, or <span class="mono">vec3(w)</span> — the weight, rendered as an image (<span class="mono">pt_bdpt.glsl:119-123</span>). Method B honours the same three modes through <span class="mono">applyBDPTMISW</span> (<span class="mono">:130-138</span>), whose own comment reads "weight only (inspect the weight!)".</p>
      <p>So the figure is a pair, one setting apart. Under <b>Flat 1/N</b> the weight image is a flat grey per anti-diagonal, because <span class="mono">countActiveStrategies</span> depends only on <span class="mono">s+t</span> and the uniforms — the structure you see is the technique's <i>support</i>, not its weighting. Under <b>B balance</b> the same image acquires structure: bright where this technique is the one that sampled the path well, dark where another technique wins. One is a picture of a constant; the other is a picture of a decision.</p>
      <p>One qualification on reading it. Weight-only mode accumulates <span class="mono">w</span> at every site that actually fired, so what lands on the film is the weight <i>masked by where the technique contributes at all</i>, not a weight field defined everywhere. A dark region can mean "this technique is down-weighted here" or "this technique never fires here"; the raw view next to it disambiguates.</p>

      <p class="sec-label">03 — The density-estimation branch</p>
      <h2>Photon mapper, VCM and UPBP: three implementations, one shared shortcut</h2>
      <p class="lead">Connection-based estimators fail when the connecting segment has zero density — through glass, at a specular vertex. Density estimation is the standard answer: store what the light path did, and reuse a stored vertex when a camera vertex lands near it. All three of this renderer's density estimators work, and all three take the same shortcut in the same place.</p>

      <h3>Mode 2 — the photon mapper</h3>
      <p>Genuinely split across the two processors, and that split is the interesting part. Photons are emitted and bounced on the CPU into two maps, <b>global</b> and <b>caustic</b> (a photon is filed as caustic when a specular chain precedes its first diffuse hit, <span class="mono">PhotonMapper.cpp:337-347</span>). Each map builds a KD-tree, which is packed and uploaded as three SSBOs. Then a compute shader traces the camera rays and does a stack-based KD-tree radius search per pixel, with an Epanechnikov kernel and a <b>per-photon</b> radius so progressive batches with different radii can coexist in one tree.</p>
      <p>Progressive photon mapping is here too, with both the PPM radius formula and a plain multiplier, plus an outer loop that restarts the sequence and averages across restarts. It is a real implementation. Three things it is not:</p>
      <ul class="clean">
        <li><b>It is surface-only.</b> <span class="mono">opts.skipVolumes = true</span> at <span class="mono">PhotonMapper.cpp:56</span>, with the comment "Photon mapper only traces surface geometry". There is a <span class="mono">m_volumeMap</span>; nothing anywhere in the tree pushes a photon into it. Its count is structurally zero.</li>
        <li><b>The volume-method control has no implementation under it.</b> The UI offers "points / beams / primitives". The beams branch is an empty block: <span class="mono">if (config.enableVolume &amp;&amp; config.volumeMethod == 1) { /* comment only */ }</span> at <span class="mono">PhotonMapper.cpp:378-382</span>. Not a partial implementation — a comment and two braces.</li>
        <li><b>"Final Gather" is a menu item, not a mode.</b> Both gather shaders branch on <span class="mono">uGatherMode == 0</span> and fall through everything else to the kNN path, so option 2 has always been option 1. Since 2026-09-04 the entry is named for what it does — "Final Gather (not built - renders kNN)" — and its tooltip says NOT IMPLEMENTED (<span class="mono">UIManagerRender.cpp:5396-5402</span>).</li>
      </ul>
      <p>And the result is not combined with the path tracer by MIS. It is <span class="mono">mix(tracerResult, photonIrradiance, uOverlayBlend)</span> — a slider. That makes mode 2 an excellent teaching instrument and not an estimator you would quote a number from.</p>

      <h3>Its panel is the laboratory, and it has one control with two different matchers behind it</h3>
      <p>The photon tab offers <b>two progressive shrink formulas</b>, side by side, which is the useful shape for a teaching instrument: Hachisuka's <span class="mono">R &larr; R&middot;&radic;((N + &alpha;M)/(N + M))</span>, and a plain <span class="mono">R &larr; R&middot;&alpha;</span> the tooltip calls fast and dramatic. The precise thing to say about the first is where its <span class="mono">N</span> and <span class="mono">M</span> come from. In the published formulation they are per-measurement-point photon counts and every pixel shrinks at its own rate; here they are the <em>global map's</em> photon totals before and after the batch, applied to <b>one scene-wide radius</b>, so the whole image shrinks in lockstep. It is PPM's schedule without PPM's per-pixel statistics — and the PPM branch also skips the shrink entirely on any batch that added no photons, where the multiplier would have shrunk anyway.</p>
      <p>Beside it, one small piece of interface design worth more than its size: <b>Max Iter sits behind a Confirm button.</b> The slider writes a pending value and the engine reads a separate confirmed one, so dragging across the range cannot launch fifty photon passes on the way to the number you wanted. Two variables where one would do, because one would have made the control dangerous.</p>
      <p>The path-expression box is the one to be careful about. It is <b>read by two different matchers</b> that do not implement the same language. Feeding the gather, it is compiled to five integers — a mode, plus a prefix and suffix as bit-masks of "specular here or not" with their lengths — and everything that is not <span class="mono">L</span>, <span class="mono">S</span>, <span class="mono">D</span> or the first <span class="mono">*</span> is dropped without comment, with any pattern not beginning in <span class="mono">L</span> discarded entirely. Feeding the on-screen photon-path overlay, the same string is handed to <span class="mono">std::regex</span> and matched case-insensitively against a built path notation, so it really is a regular expression there. The field is named <span class="mono">pmFilterRegex</span>, and in exactly one of its two readers that name is true.</p>

w = clamp(w, 0.05f, 0.95f);<br>

color = directContrib + (connectionContrib + mergeContrib) * <span class="hl">0.5f</span>;</div>

It is also, like MLT and path guiding, surface-only: <span class="mono">skipVolumes = true</span> at <span class="mono">VCMIntegrator.cpp:47</span>.</p>

      <div class="note"><b>"Fix Bias" is the one row where this page and the project's own catalogue disagree, and the source settles it.</b> The catalogue grades the VCM <span class="mono">fixBias</span> checkbox <span class="st st-b">seen</span> and describes it as toggling the substitute complementary weights. It reaches <span class="mono">VCMConfig::fixBias</span> from the UI, and then the string <span class="mono">fixBias</span> <b>does not occur once in <span class="mono">VCMIntegrator.cpp</span></b>. The field's own declaration says why — "always enabled; kept for UI compat" — so the weights described above are what you get in both positions and the box changes nothing. That matters more than an ordinary dead knob, because the greyed MIS-mode selector directly beside it carries a tooltip pointing at this control as the live one: "'Fix Bias' below is the MIS control that does change the image." One inert control is now vouched for by another.<br><br>And the weights it claims to fix are not complementary in the first place, for two reasons that compound. The clamp to <span class="mono">[0.05, 0.95]</span> breaks the sum wherever the density is extreme — which is exactly where a weight matters. And the two halves are not computed from the same count: the connection uses every neighbour the kNN search returned, while the merge uses only those that survived a bounce-0 rejection and a normal-compatibility test, so the merge's density is the smaller number and the pair sums to less than one whenever a single photon is rejected. The comment above each says "complementary". Grade carried from the catalogue; the reading is this page's.</div>

      <h3>Mode 6 — UPBP, which is the more complete of the two</h3>
      <p>Counter to the naming, the volumetric integrator in this renderer is UPBP, not VCM. It intersects volume shells when media are on (<span class="mono">:397</span>), delta-tracks through heterogeneous density, scatters in the medium, stores volume vertices with a null normal so the estimator falls back to isotropic, and does point merging <b>at volume scatter events</b> with a doubled search radius. That is P-P3D — the volumetric photon-point estimator — and it works.</p>
      <p>What is missing is the part the acronym is named for. UPBP is <i>unifying points, beams and paths</i>; the beams are built and never used:</p>
      <ul class="clean">
        <li>Beam segments are constructed and pushed into <span class="mono">m_allBeams</span> at <span class="mono">UPBPIntegrator.cpp:537</span> and <span class="mono">:581</span>, and packed into a GPU SSBO at <span class="mono">:1163</span> for the viewport overlay.</li>
        <li><span class="mono">enableBeamMerge</span> — "Beam-beam estimation", <span class="mono">UPBPIntegrator.h:39</span> — is declared and <b>read nowhere in the .cpp</b>. There is no BB1D, no PB2D, no beam query of any kind in the radiance estimate. Since 2026-09-04 the header says so flag by flag in an audit comment above the declarations, and the panel's Beam-Beam Merge toggle is greyed <span class="mono">[inert]</span>.</li>
        <li><span class="mono">m_beamEvents</span>, the visualisation record, is cleared twice and never filled.</li>
        <li><span class="mono">enableBeams</span> does do something, but not what it says: it enables a <i>global homogeneous fallback medium</i> when no volume entity is found (<span class="mono">:506</span>, <span class="mono">:723</span>), and it gates the analytic transmittance on connections at <span class="mono">:974</span>.</li>
      </ul>
      <p>UPBP also inherits VCM's <span class="mono">1 − density</span> / <span class="mono">density</span> weights and the flat 0.5 (<span class="mono">:996</span>), because the surface half was transcribed from it. And it is the one CPU integrator with headless output: <span class="mono">pp.dumpCpuAt</span> writes its film in the same PGR1 format the PP and GPU dumps use, so it is directly subtractable from them.</p>
    </div>

<section id="chains" style="--secc:var(--l540)" data-nav="Markov chains">
  <div class="wrap">
    <div class="narrow">
      <p class="sec-label">04 — The Markov-chain branch</p>
      <h2>Three modes, one algorithm, and a bias fix that is a blur</h2>
      <p class="lead">Metropolis light transport is not an estimator in the sense the rest of this page uses the word. It is a <i>sampler</i>: it takes some existing way of turning random numbers into a path contribution and explores the high-contribution regions by mutating the random numbers instead of redrawing them. That distinction decides the superset question later, so it is worth being exact about here.</p>
      <p>Modes 4, 5 and 8 are all primary-sample-space Metropolis over the same underlying path tracer. They differ in bookkeeping, not in kind.</p>
      <ul class="clean">
        <li><b>Mode 4, "MLT."</b> Bootstraps <span class="mono">seedPaths</span> candidates, takes the average luminance as the normalisation constant <span class="mono">b</span>, importance-samples a start state, then mutates a <b>fixed-length vector of random numbers</b> and replays <span class="mono">tracePath</span> on it (<span class="mono">MLTIntegrator.cpp:210-225</span>). Large step = redraw everything; small step = wrapped Gaussian per coordinate. Veach's dual splat: both the current and the proposed state are splatted, weighted <span class="mono">(1−a)/L</span> and <span class="mono">a/L</span>. This is Kelemen's algorithm, not Veach's path-space MLT — there is no lens perturbation, no caustic perturbation, no bidirectional mutation. A <span class="mono">kelemenMLT</span> flag exists in its config, commented "true=primary sample space, false=path space", defaults false, and is read nowhere. It is primary sample space either way.</li>
        <li><b>Mode 5, "PSSMLT."</b> The same algorithm done properly: primary samples are created lazily on demand and each carries a <span class="mono">lastModIter</span> so a coordinate the path never asked for is never mutated, plus explicit backup/restore on rejection (<span class="mono">:415-461</span>). This is the Kelemen implementation; mode 4 is a simpler sibling that happens to be named after the more famous algorithm.</li>
        <li><b>Mode 8, "Manifold exploration."</b> Manifold exploration is Jakob and Marschner's method: a Newton solve that walks a specular chain back onto the manifold where the half-vector constraints hold, letting a chain be perturbed without breaking. This is not that. It is mode 4's mutation loop with one change — a random number that fed a bounce flagged specular gets <span class="mono">sigma × 0.1</span> instead of <span class="mono">sigma</span> (<span class="mono">ManifoldIntegrator.cpp:234</span>). <span class="mono">newtonIterations</span> and <span class="mono">newtonStepSize</span> are written into its config from the UI at <span class="mono">EngineRender.cpp:2277</span> and appear in the .cpp exactly once, as <span class="mono">cv.newtonSteps = 0</span> in a visualisation record. A smaller step near a specular vertex is a reasonable heuristic. It is not a manifold walk, and the name promises the thing the code does not do.</li>
      </ul>

      <h3>The border blur</h3>
      <p>PSSMLT carries a checkbox called "fix bias". MCMC renderers do have a real start-up bias problem, and there are real fixes for it. This is not one:</p>
    </div>

    <div class="formula">
      <div class="f">Vec3 c = m_film[i] * scale * m_exposure;<br>
c = c / (c + Vec3(1.0f));&nbsp;&nbsp;<span class="hl">// Reinhard</span><br>
c = pow(max(c, 0), 1/2.2);&nbsp;&nbsp;<span class="hl">// gamma</span><br>
…<br>
if (m_fixBias) { <span class="hl">3×3 box blur, border pixels only</span> }</div>
      <div class="n"><span class="mono">MLTIntegrator.cpp:815-847</span>. It runs after tone mapping and gamma, on the display texture, on pixels within two of the film edge, and its stated purpose is "redistribute energy to under-sampled borders". It is a cosmetic pass over a symptom. Calling it a bias fix is the misleading part; the blur itself is harmless and does what it says.</div>
    </div>

    <div class="narrow">
      <p>All three chains are surface-only (<span class="mono">skipVolumes = true</span> at <span class="mono">MLTIntegrator.cpp:36</span> and <span class="mono">:395</span>, <span class="mono">ManifoldIntegrator.cpp:28</span>), all three hold a 12 ms per-frame mutation budget so the viewport stays interactive, and all three expose acceptance-rate and chain-length statistics that the viewport can draw. The visualisers are the reason to keep them: watching the acceptance rate collapse when <span class="mono">smallStepSigma</span> is raised is a better explanation of Metropolis than a paragraph is.</p>

<section id="caches" style="--secc:var(--l580)" data-nav="Caches &amp; reservoirs">
  <div class="wrap">
    <div class="narrow">
      <p class="sec-label">05 — Learned and resampled</p>
      <h2>Two ways of remembering where the light was last time</h2>
      <p class="lead">Path guiding and ReSTIR both attack the same weakness — a sampler that has no idea which direction is worth trying — and they attack it from opposite ends. Guiding builds a persistent spatial cache and samples from it. ReSTIR keeps a one-sample reservoir per pixel and improves it by borrowing from neighbours in space and from the previous frame in time.</p>

      <h3>Mode 7 — path guiding</h3>
      <p>A uniform 3D grid over the scene bounds, resolution configurable, one <span class="mono">GuidingCell</span> per voxel holding a directional histogram. After <span class="mono">trainingPasses</span> passes of unguided tracing, each diffuse bounce draws from the local histogram with probability <span class="mono">guidingStrength</span>, and the throughput divides by a one-sample-MIS mixture of the guided pdf and the cosine pdf (<span class="mono">PathGuidingIntegrator.cpp:332</span>). The training signal is the path's luminance splatted back along its bounces with a learning rate, spread over a 3×3 angular neighbourhood so the histogram smooths rather than spikes.</p>
      <p>That is a fair, simplified version of practical path guiding. Two things separate it from Müller's: there is no adaptive subdivision — no SD-tree, just a fixed grid — and the histogram is not fitted, so it cannot represent a lobe more sharply than one bin. And there is a mismatch in the bin arithmetic worth stating precisely, because it was invisible at the default and the panel has since been pinned to it:</p>
    </div>

    <div class="formula">
      <div class="f">// addSample / sampleDirection<br>
int tBin = clamp((int)(theta / π * <span class="hl">16.0f</span>), 0, 15);<br>
int idx  = t * <span class="hl">16</span> + p;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;// dirWeights is std::array&lt;float, 256&gt;<br><br>
// thetaPhi — the decoder<br>
int tBin = bin / <span class="hl">bins</span>;&nbsp;&nbsp;int pBin = bin % <span class="hl">bins</span>;&nbsp;&nbsp;<span class="hl">// bins = config.dirBins</span></div>
      <div class="n">The histogram is hard-coded 16×16 in <span class="mono">GuidingCell::addSample</span> and <span class="mono">::sampleDirection</span> (<span class="mono">PathGuidingIntegrator.cpp:12-56</span>), but the decoder and the bin solid angle both use <span class="mono">config.dirBins</span>, which the UI used to expose as a slider from 4 to 32. At 16 they agree. At any other value the sampled bin index was decoded into the wrong direction <i>and</i> normalised by the wrong solid angle — silently, because guiding is MIS-weighted against the cosine lobe. On 2026-09-04 the slider was pinned to 16 and greyed, with a tooltip that says why (<span class="mono">UIManagerRender.cpp:7391-7400</span>). That closes the trap at the panel; the encode and decode sides still read two different constants, so it is closed rather than removed.</div>
    </div>

    <div class="narrow">
      <p>One more note on this mode: <span class="mono">addSample</span> ends with a per-bin cap at 15% of the cell's total weight, redistributing the excess by discarding it. That is a variance clamp on the learned distribution. It makes the guide better behaved and it is not part of any published formulation.</p>
      <div class="note"><b>And one correction that runs the other way, since this page keeps score in both directions.</b> This project's own capture notes carry a confirmed bug for this panel — "4.1: the Path Guiding tab's enable toggle is wired to the wrong variable", binding the enable switch to <span class="mono">poisEnabled</span> (Poisson gradient editing) so that revealing the field visualiser also ran a gradient filter over the frame. <b>It is fixed.</b> <span class="mono">UIManagerRender.cpp:7386-7391</span> now binds <span class="mono">pgEnabled</span>, and carries the comment "<span class="mono">pgEnabled, NOT poisEnabled — the latter is Poisson Gradient Editing</span>". The shooting note is the stale document, not the code. The <span class="mono">Dir Bins</span> trap two paragraphs up has since been pinned shut the same way.</div>

      <h3>ReSTIR — the two passes that are not in the mode enum</h3>
      <p>ReSTIR DI is the full construction — resampled importance sampling into per-pixel reservoirs, temporal reuse against the previous frame, spatial reuse from neighbours — not a checkbox that blurs the direct term, and it is easy to miss because it is not a mode. It runs as a post-pass over the path tracer's G-buffer (<span class="mono">EngineRender.cpp:2666-2682</span>): <span class="mono">restir.comp</span> generates candidates and does temporal reuse against the previous frame's reservoirs, <span class="mono">restirSpatial.comp</span> combines each pixel's reservoir with its neighbours', and the result replaces the direct-lighting term while specular pixels pass the tracer's own value through. 907 lines of GLSL across the two passes.</p>
      <p><b>ReSTIR PT is not the same thing</b>, and its own file header says so: "screen-space multi-scatter reservoir reuse … uses the tracer's accumulated HDR radiance as the candidate pool … still an approximation of ReSTIR GI/PT rather than a full sub-path backend" (<span class="mono">restirPT.comp:1-7</span>). It reuses <i>radiance</i> between geometrically similar pixels. It does not resample paths, so it is not ReSTIR PT in Lin et al.'s sense; the UI labels it Experimental and the header labels it an approximation, which between them is an accurate description.</p>

      <h3>The debug surface underneath both, which nobody has switched on</h3>
      <p>Both ReSTIR passes carry an instrument panel that is larger than the algorithm's own controls, and it is the place a survey expects to find dead knobs. There are none. Every control below reaches a uniform and every uniform is read in the shader — the seven-way visualisation combo goes into <em>both</em> compute passes and each consumes all six of its non-zero modes; the BRDF overrides pack into one integer that both passes branch on; all nine multi-scatter damping controls arrive as nine separate uniforms and appear in the arithmetic. What is worth stating is not deadness but three details a reader would otherwise have to grep for.</p>
      <p><b>The two BRDF overrides look independent and are not.</b> They are drawn as two separate toggles and encoded as one integer 0&ndash;3: All Lambertian claims 1 or 2 depending on Keep Color, and Ignore Dielectric is reached only by the <span class="mono">else</span>. Tick both and you get All Lambertian; the second toggle stays lit and stops meaning anything. <b>The visualisation combo is a replacement, not an overlay</b> — any non-zero mode zeroes the path-traced colour first, so what reaches the film is the debug channel alone, so a visualisation frame is a picture of the reservoir field and not a tinted render. And <b>three of the damping controls ship in the position that does nothing</b>: reuse blend defaults to the top of its range, the radiance clamp defaults to the top of its range, and outlier rejection defaults to zero. Each of the three can only damp from there, and none of them is damping until you move it.</p>
      <p>Which explains the button beside them. "Reset Legacy Profile" promises to restore "the older, stronger multi-bounce reuse profile before the newer damping controls", and the nine values it writes are <b>byte-identical to the nine declared defaults</b>. On a fresh session it is a no-op. That is not a defect — the older profile is deliberately what ships, so the damping is opt-in and the button is an escape hatch back from your own experiment — but the label describes a state you have to leave before the button can return you to it.</p>

<section id="superset" style="--secc:var(--l600)" data-nav="The superset claim">
  <div class="wrap">
    <div class="narrow">
      <p class="sec-label">06 — The claim, scoped</p>
      <h2>Photon primitives are a superset along one axis and a sibling along the other</h2>
      <p class="lead">The tempting sentence is "photon primitives generalise the other estimators". Part of that is defensible and worth defending precisely; the rest is a category error a reviewer would find in about a minute. The two parts are cleanly separable, so this section separates them, states the construction as an arithmetic rather than an analogy, and then says what the arithmetic does not reach.</p>

      <h3>The construction, as a dimension count</h3>
      <p>A light subpath is generated by scalar decisions and nothing else: two for a point on an area emitter (three for a solid one), two for each direction, one for each free-flight distance. Sampling a scalar commits it to a number. <b>Sweeping</b> a scalar refuses to commit and integrates it analytically instead. Sweep <span class="mono">k</span> of them and the photon's final vertex stops being a point and becomes a <span class="mono">k</span>-manifold in space. The camera-side query is a ray — a 1-manifold. Everything else follows from one subtraction.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="formula">
      <div class="f">dim( photon manifold ∩ camera ray ) = <span class="hl">k + 1 − 3</span></div>
      <div class="n">Negative: no generic intersection, so a blur kernel of dimension <span class="mono">3−(k+1)</span> and a bias that goes with its radius. Zero: isolated crossings, admissible against the delta, weight <span class="mono">f / |det[ ∂S/∂u₁ , ∂S/∂u₂ , −ω_cam ]|</span> — no kernel anywhere. Positive: the crossing is a segment, still kernel-free, but a 1-D integral survives and has to be sampled or quadratured. Stated this way in <span class="mono">docs/2026-06-10-photon-primitives-research.md §1.1</span>, which is the repository's own reading of the published theory.</div>

      <h3>The roster is the same split, chosen differently</h3>
      <p>Two things make this an arithmetic and not a story. First, the direction-sweep trio is <i>exhaustive</i>: strategies 7, 8 and 9 are the three ways to choose two of the three scalars <span class="mono">{θ, φ, t}</span> at one vertex — sweep θ and φ and you get the sphere of radius <span class="mono">t</span>, sweep t and φ and you get a cone, sweep t and θ and you get a meridian half-plane. There is no fourth. Second, the crossings really are closed form and the Jacobians really are the ones the count predicts. The photon sphere's is <span class="mono">t²·|ω·d|</span> (<span class="mono">PPIntegrator.cpp</span>); the plane's is Cramer's rule on <span class="mono">[d | −e | −ω]</span> with determinant <span class="mono">(e×ω)·d</span> (<span class="mono">:2762</span>); the photon volume's chord is a genuine one-dimensional integral with a genuine extra random number, <span class="mono">s = sIn + L·u</span> (<span class="mono">:2364</span>, <span class="mono">:2411</span>) — the only per-sample draw in the family that exists because <span class="mono">k</span> went to three.</p>

      <p class="note"><b>On anchors.</b> File references on this page name the file, not a line. The integrator is under active development and every line number in an earlier draft of this page had already drifted by the time it was checked — one of them by twenty-seven lines, in a document that had been correct when written. Grep the symbol.</p>

      <h3>Whose family this is</h3>
      <p>The lineage the section above is arguing with, named, because a page that claims to generalise a
      published family and cites nobody from it reads as a bluff. Photon <b>beams</b> are Jarosz and
      colleagues, 2011: the light path's free-flight distance is swept rather than sampled, and the camera
      ray integrates against the resulting segment. <b>Beyond points and beams</b>, Bitterli and Jarosz,
      2017, is where the higher-dimensional members come from — sweeping two or three consecutive
      distances gives the photon plane and the photon volume, and their own implementation is vendored in
      this repository under <span class="mono">REF/correlated/</span>, which is how the plane row in the
      table below was checked and corrected. <b>Photon surfaces</b>, Deng and colleagues, 2019, is the
      zero-blur two-dimensional case. Transient extensions by Marco and colleagues in 2019 and by Liu and
      colleagues in 2022 slice the same primitives temporally, and the non-exponential Tungsten fork those
      build on is also in <span class="mono">REF/</span>.</p>
      <p class="note">Venues and years here are given from memory of the literature rather than from a
      fetched bibliography, so treat the attributions as author-and-idea; the ideas are what the argument
      rests on. What this project adds to that lineage is the dielectric members — a swept manifold that
      passes through a refracting sphere and stays analytic — and a balance-heuristic mixture over a
      deliberately diverse set, which is a different contribution from adding another dimension to the
      sweep.</p>

      <div class="note"><b>A correction, since this page is where it would have done the damage.</b> An earlier draft of this section called next-event estimation "the degenerate member — sweep nothing" and said it "lives in the same dispatch as the rest". Both halves are wrong. NEE closes the same three-dimensional constraint by a <b>connection</b>: sample the light point, sample the scatter point on the camera ray, and the photon direction becomes deterministic, paid for with the <span class="mono">1/r²</span> geometry factor. A sweep closes it by <b>intersection</b>: sample less, and let the camera ray decide the position, paid for with the co-area Jacobian. Sweeping and aiming are different operations and the dimension count does not apply to the second, because NEE does not generate the two subpaths independently. And in this renderer NEE is not in the swept dispatch at all — strategies 1 and 2 are a full multi-bounce camera walk with phase-MIS, and the single surface connection is gated to exactly those two strategies (<span class="mono">PPIntegrator.cpp-4760</span>). It shares the light samplers with the swept strategies and nothing else.</div>

      <p>What <i>is</i> true, and is sharper than the thing it replaces: <b>the photon sphere is NEE with the two segments' roles exchanged.</b> NEE samples the camera distance and solves for the photon distance; strategy 7 samples the photon distance and solves for the camera distance. Same paths, same integrand, opposite assignment of which length is drawn and which is found. The renderer already carries the proof: strategy 7's density along the camera ray is <span class="mono">σ_t·e^(−σ_t·t)·|ω·d|</span> (<span class="mono">ppDirPrimPdf</span>, <span class="mono">:1677</span>) — the free-flight distance pdf, pushed forward to camera arclength by the tangency factor. That is a density in the same measure as NEE's own, so the two <i>could</i> be MIS-combined, and the ingredient for doing it already exists. <b>Nobody has done it.</b> No strategy in the roster MIS-combines a swept primitive with the camera walk. The renderer does <i>compose</i> the two — comp mode runs a swept primary alongside the path-tracing backup — but by a hard 0/1 partition of declared path classes, not by a weight; <span class="mono">ppCompWeight</span> returns a float only so that a weighted seam can replace its body later without touching anything else in the file.</p>

      <h3>Photon <i>mapping</i> is the correlated member — and only partly</h3>
      <p>Sampling a fresh primitive per camera sample is one choice; drawing a batch once per pass with no reference to any camera ray and letting every pixel sum the same batch is the other, and the second one is photon mapping's move. That mode is built and validated (<span class="mono">pp.correlated</span>, <span class="mono">pp.primCount</span>, <span class="mono">pp.freezePool</span>): twelve films byte-identical with the mode off, and correlated films reaching the uncorrelated energy across two presets and eight strategies. The normalisation is the whole risk, and it is the same rule the reference photon mapper follows — divide by primitives <b>drawn</b>, never by primitives that crossed the ray. The wrong version was built on purpose, measured at <b>2.1× to 6.8×</b> too bright on frame energy and <b>6.6× to 14.2×</b> per lit pixel, found to be flat to 1.5% over a 16× range in passes, and then deleted. It converges cleanly to the wrong answer and nothing in the image says so.</p>
      <p>But the honest scope of that is narrower than "photon mapping". Three things are still missing, and two of them are missing on purpose:</p>
      <ul class="clean">
        <li><b>No radius reduction, and for most members the idea does not apply.</b> A progressive radius schedule exists to drive a kernel's bias to zero while keeping variance finite. Strategies 3–17, 20, 22–24, 26 and 27 have no kernel: nothing in them converges by shrinking a radius, and a progressive shrink would inject bias where there is none. <b>Six slots carry one</b> — 18 and 19, the tracer beam and the tracer point; since the one-dimensional group shipped, the ring at 28, the arc at 29 and their mixture with the beam at 30; and the hourglass pair at 21, whose beam member carries it at weight O(r) on the caustic and at one everywhere else, and whose energy moves with it (+0.9 % at r = 0.15) — and their radius is a single fixed slider, <span class="mono">pp.beamRadius</span>, rather than a schedule. Sharing one width is load-bearing rather than incidental in 30: with the same <span class="mono">r</span> across its three members the <span class="mono">2r</span> cancels identically in the balance weights. So the family does not contain progressive photon mapping — it removes the problem PPM solves for the analytic members, and does not solve it for the five that need it. What it does instead is publish the ladder rather than shrink it: five radii from 0.30 down to 0.019 at 3,200 passes each, every rung carrying its own error bar, on <a href="photon-hourglass.html#onedim" style="color:var(--l460)">Caustics without a kernel</a>.</li>
          <li><b>No surface gather.</b> Photon mapping's headline is a density estimate on surfaces, and the family has none: no kd-tree, no kNN, no surface photons. Every strategy is single fog scatter, and the only surface term is one Lambertian connection with no continuation.</li>
        <li><b>No multi-bounce photon storage.</b> The frozen batch stores <i>primitives</i>, not paths. One traced light path can yield several beam segments, but no stored vertex is ever reused by a different estimator, and the light-side chain terminates at the first diffuse surface. And freezing the batch across passes converges to <i>that batch's realisation</i>, not to L — the UI labels it as not converging, because it is the artifact mode, not a render mode.</li>
      </ul>
      <p>So the correct sentence is: correlation makes the family a superset of <b>volumetric photon mapping's density-estimation core</b> — the reuse structure, and the point and beam kernels — and not of progressive photon mapping, and not of surface photon mapping at all.</p>

    <div class="narrow">
      <h3>What the construction does not reach, and which axis each one varies</h3>
      <p>Every item below fails to be contained for a structural reason, not for want of work. The test is the same each time: name the axis it varies, and check whether the sweep construction touches that axis at all.</p>
      <ul class="clean">
        <li><b>Bidirectional path tracing varies the join index.</b> BDPT's content is the <span class="mono">(l,e)</span> grid — which light-subpath vertex is connected to which eye-subpath vertex — and the megakernel here implements every cell of it, interior connections included. The sweep construction fixes that index and never moves it: light → manifold → camera ray, with a straight camera leg. The renderer states this as a machine-checkable declaration rather than a claim. Its strategy table gives every photon-primitive row <span class="mono">q = 0</span> (no non-scatter vertex between the eye and the fog scatter) and <span class="mono">nV ∈ [1,1]</span> (exactly one fog scatter), and no row is ever allowed a diffuse vertex in the light-side chain (<span class="mono">kPPClass[25]</span>, <span class="mono">PPIntegrator.cpp-1981</span>). A family with one join cannot contain a family indexed by every join.</li>
        <li><b>Vertex connection and merging varies how the join is made</b> — connection or merge — and then MISes the two across the whole pyramid. That is two moves the family does not have. A merge reuses a <i>stored</i> vertex from a <i>different</i> subpath under a kernel; a sweep integrates one fresh photon analytically. Correlation supplies storage, but not merging, because a kernel-free sheet has no radius to merge inside. And VCM's actual payoff is the MIS <i>between</i> merging and every connection technique — which needs a pyramid to MIS against.</li>
        <li><b>Metropolis varies the sequence of samples, not the estimator.</b> This is the one that would be embarrassing to get wrong. MLT is a sampler over path space: it mutates the random numbers some estimator consumes, and the estimator underneath is unchanged. In this renderer, modes 4 and 5 mutate the primary sample vector feeding the same CPU path tracer, and mode 8 does the same with a scaled-down mutation on the randoms that fed a specular bounce. You can run Metropolis <i>on</i> a photon-primitive estimator — nothing stops a chain from mutating the primitive's random vector — but you cannot write Metropolis as a strategy number, because it is not a way of forming a path. It is a way of choosing which path to form next.</li>
        <li><b>Manifold exploration varies how a specular chain is solved.</b> It runs a Newton solve on the half-vector constraint to walk from one valid specular path to a nearby one. The hourglass looks adjacent — both are about chains through glass — but it is the opposite move: it never solves for a path, it parameterises the entire family of them in closed form, because rotating the whole refraction chain about the light-to-centre axis is a rigid motion. Exact where it applies, and it applies only to a sphere with a point-sampled origin. Manifold exploration applies to arbitrary geometry. Neither contains the other. (This renderer's mode 8 is <i>labelled</i> Manifold and implements no Newton solve at all — see the ledger.)</li>
        <li><b>Path guiding varies the proposal density.</b> It learns a directional distribution and draws from it. That is orthogonal by construction: a swept strategy could draw its <i>non-swept</i> scalars from a learned density and not one line of the derivation would change. The family fixes what is swept; guiding fixes where the remaining draws come from. Nothing in this renderer composes them, and that is a gap rather than a boundary.</li>
      </ul>

      <h3>The paragraph, if you read only one</h3>
      <p class="lead">The photon-primitive family is a <b>superset of photon density estimation</b> — photon points, beams, planes and volumes are one estimator at zero, one, two and three swept dimensions, and here they are consecutive blocks of one function under one MIS ledger that prices them by their kernel's order in the radius. It is a <b>sibling of the connection strategies</b> — BDPT, VCM and light tracing vary which two vertices are joined, and every photon primitive joins the same two, so neither family contains the other and their product is larger than either. It is <b>orthogonal to the samplers</b> — Metropolis mutates the numbers an estimator consumes and guiding changes the density they come from; both compose with a swept primitive and neither can be written as one. And it is itself <b>a special case</b>: single scatter in a medium, a straight camera leg, and a light-side chain that may be specular but never diffuse — a restriction this renderer does not merely accept but declares, one row per strategy, in a table a second integrator is checked against.</p>
      <p>That is less sweeping than "photon primitives generalise the other estimators", and it is the version that survives contact with the source. It is also the more interesting claim, because the boundary it draws is exactly where the next piece of work is: the megakernel's own comment already sketches photon primitives as a third axis on the <span class="mono">(l,e)</span> grid, and strategies 22, 23 and 24 have taken one step onto it.</p>
    </div>

      <h2>The megakernel wrote down the meeting point before the estimator existed</h2>

    <div class="formula">
      <div class="f">// - 0-bounce connection: shadow ray (standard BDPT, what we have now)<br>
// - 1-bounce connection: photon hits a surface, connects to eye vertex<br>
//   (equivalent to VCM/UPS — vertex connection + merging)<br>

The last line is the part that holds, and it is exactly right: BDPT indexes techniques by <i>where</i> the two subpaths are joined, and the photon-primitive family indexes them by <i>how much of the photon is left free</i> at the join.     <div class="narrow">
      <p>The renderer has already taken one step onto that third axis. Strategies 22, 23 and 24 — the vertex hourglass members — are seeded <b>on the glass</b> rather than at the light, and their light side is <b>traced</b> back to an emissive entity rather than sampled from <span class="mono">scene.lights[]</span>. The light endpoint of a photon primitive is therefore a subpath vertex, which is the beginning of a light-vertex index on a swept estimator. It is not a pyramid yet; it is one column of one.</p>
      <p>The measurements say the step is sound. Energy ratio 22/20 = <b>1.007</b> and <b>1.020</b> on presets 47 and 52; 23/20 = <b>1.0001</b> on both at 48k passes; 24 against the stochastic beam reference restricted to the reflection branch = <b>0.9982</b>, and <b>0.9996</b> with the top twenty pixels removed from each side. And the other half, stated with the same precision: <b>none of the three has ever been looked at on screen.</b> They pass headless numeric checks — the ratios above, one of them to four digits — and no human has seen a frame — built, validated, and never once looked at. In this project's grading that is a different status from "works", and the triage reference records it as such.</p>

      <h3>What the swept family has that none of the others do</h3>
      <p>One capability is genuinely absent from every other mode in this renderer, and it is the reason the family exists rather than being a curiosity. A smooth dielectric is a delta BSDF: fix the incoming direction and Snell fixes the outgoing one, so a connection <i>through</i> glass has zero density. NEE cannot do it. BDPT's interior connections cannot do it — the pyramid's own cell description says so, "Diffuse-only (implemented). Specular needs photon primitives or merge (VCM)". Merging can do it, blurred by a radius. Manifold exploration is the published answer and this renderer does not have one.</p>
      <p>The photon hourglass does it analytically. Rotating the whole refraction chain about the light-to-centre axis is a rigid motion, so the swept manifold is exact, the crossing is closed-form, and the caustic has no kernel radius in it at all. Paired with the stochastic beam as strategy 21, at equal passes (200, preset 48) the median relative error on the isolated caustic is about 1.9× lower than the beam's alone, and the converged pair matches the converged beam at a median ratio of 1.0000.</p>

    <div class="narrow">
      <h3>Rendered, and validated, are different words</h3>
      <p>It would be tidy to say the CPU integrators have never been rendered on purpose, and it would be false. This project keeps a capture inventory with explicit status codes — <span class="mono">OK</span> = verified working, <span class="mono">UNSEEN</span> = built and headless-checked but never viewed by a human — and modes 2 through 8 are all marked <span class="mono">OK</span> there, with per-mode recipes and viewport visualisers that someone clearly drove (<span class="mono">docs/CAPTURE-SHOOTING-LIST.md §4</span>). They have been looked at. What is marked <span class="mono">UNSEEN</span> is a shorter list: the MLT chain and acceptance visualiser, showcase presets 49-52, the split A/B compare, clean view itself — and, from this page, <b>the BDPT pyramid, which has no capture anywhere in this repository</b>, and photon-primitive strategies 22, 23 and 24.</p>

      <h3>What this page is not claiming</h3>
      <p>None of the seven CPU integrators has ever been validated against a reference. The photon-primitive family has, extensively, and the GPU path tracer has against it — <b>median 1.0005</b> on the one transport class both express identically, with directly-seen emitter pixels masked because the two disagree there by a factor of 2.96 and it is unresolved which convention is intended. But there is no number anywhere in this repository for "is VCM right", and the reason is plain: nobody built the gate. Modes 2 through 8 are teaching instruments and research scaffolding that happen to render; they are not evidence, and this page does not lean on them as any.</p>
      <p>Nor is a stub a criticism. A stub in a research renderer is a decision about where the time went, and this project's went into the swept estimators and the machinery to prove they are right. The point of listing them is that a reader can tell which is which without reading 20,000 lines, and that the four labels which promised more than they delivered — method A, final gather, manifold exploration, beam merging — are named here rather than discovered later. Three of the four were relabelled in the panel on 2026-09-04; manifold exploration still carries its name.</p>
