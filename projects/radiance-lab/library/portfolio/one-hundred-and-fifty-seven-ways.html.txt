      <p class="sec-label">01 &mdash; What is actually there</p>
      <h2>Not a filter pack. A second renderer that happens to run on the first one's output</h2>
      <p class="lead">The landing page carries one row for all of this: <em>"157 stackable image operators &mdash; over 21 non-photorealistic modes &mdash; Ricci flow and persistent homology share a combo with halftone."</em> That row is the only place on this site the bank exists. Everything below is what is behind it.</p>
      <p>The claim is not "we have effects". Every renderer has effects. What is unusual here is the <em>company these operators keep</em>: a lattice U(1) gauge field with Wilson-loop holonomy is nineteen rows below Risograph misregistration, in the same scrolling panel, and both are one click. And what is unusual in the other direction is that fifty-five of them read the path tracer's G-buffer, so they are not image filters at all &mdash; run them on a photograph and they do nothing.</p>
      <p>The bank is genuinely wired. That was the first thing to check, because a list of 157 named toggles is exactly the sort of thing that turns out to be a menu with no engine behind it. It is not.</p>

    <div class="narrow">
      <h3>Ricci flow, in full, because it is the one people will check first</h3>
      <p>The tooltip reads: <em>"Flow the metric by &minus;2&middot;Ric(g). (Perelman's tool for Poincar&eacute; conjecture.) Curvature-weighted smoothing."</em> Here is what is right and what is not, in order.</p>
      <p><b>Right:</b> the shader's own header states the correct reduction &mdash; in two dimensions a conformal metric <span class="mono">g = e<sup>2u</sup>&delta;</span> turns the flow into an equation on the single scalar <em>u</em>. And the curvature overlay really is the conformal-metric Gauss curvature rather than an edge detector wearing its name. It really does run explicit Euler on a 5-point Laplacian for 4&ndash;20 iterations, not a blur pretending to be one.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="formula">
      <div class="f">&part;u/&part;t = <span class="hl">e<sup>&minus;2u</sup></span> &Delta;u<span style="opacity:.35">&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&larr; 2-D Ricci flow</span></div>
      <div class="f">&part;u/&part;t = &Delta;u<span style="opacity:.35">&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&larr; what <span class="mono">ricciFlow.frag</span> integrates</span></div>
      <div class="f">K = &minus; e<sup>&minus;2u</sup> &Delta;u<span style="opacity:.35">&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&larr; Gauss curvature of <span class="mono">g = e<sup>2u</sup>&delta;</span></span></div>
      <div class="f">K = &minus; &Delta;u / ( <span class="hl">2</span> e<sup>2u</sup> )<span style="opacity:.35">&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&larr; <span class="mono">ricciFlow.frag:96</span> &mdash; half of it</span></div>
      <div class="n">The dropped factor in the flow is the whole nonlinearity. What runs is the linear heat equation on image luminance.</div>
    </div>

    <div class="narrow">
      <p><b>A correction to an earlier version of this section</b>, which printed the shader's curvature line and called it <em>exact</em>. It is not: it is off by a factor of two, and the reason is more interesting than the size. The same identity has two standard forms. Writing the metric as <span class="mono">g = e<sup>2u</sup>&delta;</span> gives <span class="mono">K = &minus;e<sup>&minus;2u</sup>&Delta;u</span>; writing it as <span class="mono">g = &rho;&delta;</span> gives <span class="mono">K = &minus;(1/2&rho;)&Delta;log&rho;</span>. They agree, because <span class="mono">log&rho; = 2u</span> and the 2 cancels. <span class="mono">ricciFlow.frag</span> takes the <span class="mono">1/(2&rho;)</span> prefactor from the second form and the <span class="mono">&Delta;u</span> from the first, so the 2 never cancels and the overlay shows <span class="mono">K/2</span>. Nothing visible depends on it &mdash; the value is immediately normalised by <span class="mono">clamp(K&middot;8.0, &minus;1, 1)</span>, so the factor is absorbed into a display gain &mdash; but this was the one claim the page made in the operator's favour, on a page whose whole thesis is that a name has to be checked against the code. It did not survive its own standard.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="narrow">
      <p><b>Not right, in three parts.</b> First, the missing <span class="mono">e<sup>&minus;2u</sup></span>: real 2-D Ricci flow is a nonlinear fast-diffusion equation, and the shader integrates its linearisation. Second, <em>u</em> is image luminance, not a metric on any surface &mdash; there is no manifold here, and no depth or normal is read, so this operator is running on the picture, not on the geometry the renderer knows. Third, the iteration does not close: the comment concedes that the ring neighbours are "updated with their own simpler one-step estimates", so past the first step the stencil is inconsistent and the scheme is not the explicit Euler iterate it claims to be.</p>
      <p>And one line in the shader is worth quoting because it is the exact species of argument this page exists to catch: <em>"Cap iterations to keep worst-case cost reasonable; mathematically Ricci flow converges in 2D regardless of specific step count."</em> That is a true statement about the continuous flow being used to license a truncation of a discrete scheme, which it does not license. The truncation is probably fine &mdash; with <span class="mono">dt &le; 0.05</span> in texel units the explicit scheme is comfortably inside its stability bound &mdash; but it is fine for a different reason than the one given.</p>
      <p class="note"><b>The fair summary.</b> "Ricci Flow" here is <em>curvature-coloured heat-equation smoothing of luminance with a gradient warp</em>. That is a real and slightly unusual image operator, and the overlay genuinely is the conformal-metric Gauss curvature, up to the stray factor of two above. It is not Perelman's tool, and nothing in this program flows a metric. The name would be earned by a rename &mdash; "Conformal Curvature Flow" &mdash; and by keeping the <span class="mono">e<sup>&minus;2u</sup></span>, which is one multiply.</p>
    </div>

      <p class="lead">The renderer spends enormous effort producing a linear HDR film: unbiased estimators, byte-identity contracts, energy ratios to four digits.       <p>The reason this belongs on a rendering site rather than in a footnote is that it is the same class of modelling as everything else here. A thin-lens camera is a model of an input device. Both are physical-process emulation; only one of them is usually treated as serious.</p>

      <p><b>The pre-filter is not boilerplate; it is the concession to running on a path tracer.</b> Its own tooltip says why it exists: both transforms <em>"vote ONLY from clean binary edges&hellip; this prevents a few bright HDR regions from dominating the accumulator"</em>. A Hough transform written for an 8-bit photograph can vote by gradient magnitude and be fine. Vote by gradient magnitude on a linear HDR film and a specular highlight three stops above everything else wins every bin it touches. So there is a tone-map multiplier <em>ahead of</em> the edge detector and a hard binary threshold after it, and the denoise combo exists because the tracer's own sampling noise is impulse noise &mdash; which is why <em>median 3&times;3</em> is in a list that would otherwise be Gaussian and bilateral. That is a computer-vision operator adapted to a renderer, and it is the one thing in this pair worth a paragraph.</p>
      <p><b>One defect, in the circle pass.</b> The NMS shader's header says it finds peaks that are a <em>"strict local max in 3x3x3"</em>, and the test it runs is <span class="mono">if (n &gt; v) isMax = false</span> &mdash; which passes when a neighbour <em>equals</em> the centre. Ties are not suppressed. Every cell of a flat plateau in the accumulator is emitted as its own peak, and because the accumulator holds integer vote counts on a downscaled grid (the Accum Downscale slider goes to 16), plateaus are the normal case at low vote counts rather than a pathological one. The visible consequence would be several concentric or near-coincident rings drawn for one circle, and the 256-peak cap being reached by duplicates. <b>I have not run it</b> &mdash; this is read from <span class="mono">houghCirclesNMS.comp:42-59</span>, where the test itself is line 53, and the shot below is the test.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="slot">Screenshot — The Hough pair, and the tie question. A family is the tie behaviour described above, and it would be the first observation of it.</div>

      <p>After the 157 comes a short, unreorderable sequence that every frame passes through: <span class="mono">applyCVFilter</span>, then depth-of-field, then the in-engine photo tap, then data moshing, then the node projector's reproject mode, then glow, then the final temporal blur, then the selection overlay. Four of its rows have never been photographed and one of them is not a filter at all.</p>
    </div>

Both are on screen at once if both headers are open. The second is the one that ghosts a camera move; the first is the one that makes an animated volume converge faster and quietly biases it.</p>
      <p><b>And the second one is not last.</b> The catalogue calls it <em>"a second, independent EMA at the very end of the chain"</em>, and the source disagrees: <span class="mono">applySelectionOverlay</span> runs after it. The consequence is small and specific &mdash; the selection outline is composited on top of the blur and is therefore the one element in the frame that does not ghost when you move the camera &mdash; and it is invisible under Clean View, which is where every shot on this page is taken. The correction is here rather than in the catalogue because the source is what settles it.</p>

      <h3>The bilateral-grid preview is a diagram of an algorithm, drawn from your slider values</h3>
      <p>Turn on the bilateral filter and a <em>Show Grid Preview</em> toggle appears. What it draws is not your image. It builds a hard-coded one-dimensional test signal &mdash; flat at 0.3, a linear ramp between x = 0.45 and 0.55, flat at 0.8 &mdash; and splats it into a grid whose two axes are <em>spatial x</em> and <em>range</em>, with the cell counts derived live from your two sigma sliders: the x cell count from the spatial sigma, the range cell count as <span class="mono">1/(&sigma;<sub>r</sub> + 0.01)</span>, both clamped. Occupied cells are tinted by their mean value, the signal curve is drawn over the top, and hovering the canvas draws the filter's <b>support ellipse</b> at the cursor with semi-axes proportional to &sigma;<sub>s</sub> and &sigma;<sub>r</sub> &mdash; so you can see, directly, that raising the range sigma is what lets the filter reach across the step (<span class="mono">UIManagerRender.cpp:1694-1785</span>).</p>
      <p>That is a teaching figure for the bilateral grid, live-parametrised by the controls it explains, sitting inside a filter panel. It is the most unusual widget in this territory and it has never been photographed. Two things about it should be said plainly. Its own footer label reads <em>"Splat &rarr; Blur 3D &rarr; Slice (bilateral grid)"</em> and the widget performs the first of those three: the code splats and draws, and there is no blur pass over the accumulated cells and no slice back to the signal. <b>The catalogue's row says it animates all three; the source says otherwise, and the source wins.</b> The label is best read as naming the method the drawing belongs to rather than the stages the drawing shows. And the toggle is a function-local <span class="mono">static bool</span>, so unlike every other control in the panel it lives outside <span class="mono">UIManager</span> and is not saved with the rest of the UI state.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="slot">Screenshot — The widget that explains itself. No scene required — this is a panel crop, and it is the cheapest capture on the page. Between (b) and (c) the row count must not change at all while the grid LOSES columns and the ellipse widens, because the column count is derived from the spatial sigma alone. The caption must say the grid is drawn from a synthetic 1-D signal and never from the rendered frame.</div>
