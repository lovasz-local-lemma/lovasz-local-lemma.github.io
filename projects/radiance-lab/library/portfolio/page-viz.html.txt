<!-- ================= 01 · WHAT A FIREFLY IS ================= -->
<section id="firefly" style="--secc:var(--l400)" data-nav="The firefly">
  <div class="wrap">
    <div class="narrow">
      <p class="sec-label">01 — What a firefly actually is</p>
      <h2>A firefly is a Jacobian going to zero, and there are four ways it can do that</h2>
      <p class="lead">Every estimator in this renderer's analytic family sweeps two dimensions of a light path into a surface, intersects the camera ray with it, and divides by the co-area Jacobian of the sweep. The Jacobian sits in the denominator. A camera ray tangent to the swept sheet drives it toward zero and the sample explodes.</p>
      <p>So a bright isolated pixel is not a mystery to be denoised. It is a geometric event with a location, and the location is computable. The useful question is not <em>how big</em> the outlier was but <em>how</em> the Jacobian vanished, because that decides whether the estimator has a heavy tail at all.</p>
    </div>
    <div class="formula">
      <div class="f">W = f / ( p <span class="hl">·</span> J )<span style="opacity:.4">&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;</span>J = <span class="hl">|</span> ( ∂x/∂a × ∂x/∂b ) · d <span class="hl">|</span></div>
      <div class="n">One weight, one Jacobian, the whole family. Only the two swept dimensions <span class="mono">a, b</span> change.</div>
    </div>
    <div class="tw"><table>
      <thead><tr><th>How J vanishes</th><th>Members</th><th>What the tail does</th><th>Consequence</th></tr></thead>
      <tbody>
        <tr><td>constant</td><td>3</td><td>max / median <b>1.7–3.0</b></td><td>No in-pixel tail at all. The tangency shows up <em>across</em> pixels instead, as the crossing probability collapsing</td></tr>
        <tr><td>linear</td><td>10, 11, 14, 16</td><td>max / median <b>200–7000</b>, top-1% share 20–45%</td><td>Integrable. The mean converged on all 16,758 (ray, estimator) pairs tested</td></tr>
        <tr><td>square-root <b>fold</b></td><td>7, 9, 20, 22, 23, 24</td><td>W ~ ε<sup>−1/2</sup>, Hill index <b>2</b></td><td>E[W²] is log-divergent. The sample variance grows like ln N and <b>never converges</b></td></tr>
        <tr><td>Cauchy fan</td><td>8</td><td>relvar × δ<sub>axis</sub> = <b>0.42–0.45</b></td><td>Bounded weights, no fold. Two exactly silent rays instead — through the axis and parallel to it</td></tr>
      </tbody>
    </table></div>
    <div class="narrow">
      <p>The fold row is the one that changes how you read every other number on this site. Two quadric roots merge at the silhouette of the swept surface, the weight diverges like the inverse square root of the distance to that merge, and the resulting Pareto tail has index exactly 2 — measured at 1.96 for the photon sphere and 2.00 for the photon cone, over three decades of the complementary CDF. A tail index of 2 puts the second moment on the boundary of existence, so a variance estimate from a fold member is a lower bound that keeps rising as you sample.</p>

      <h3>What the renderer does with a firefly &mdash; and the one it structurally cannot see</h3>
      <p>There are two firefly instruments in the megakernel and they answer two different questions. <span class="mono">pathTrace.comp</span> carries <b>eight</b> non-finite tests. One discards a splat outright: <span class="mono">splatToImage</span> returns before writing anything, under the comment <em>&ldquo;discard invalid contributions to prevent pixel poisoning&rdquo;</em>. Two replace a sample with black rather than dropping it &mdash; the returned radiance and the eye-subpath result. The other five gate a connection&rsquo;s add: the BDPT connection, the light tracer&rsquo;s emitter and camera contributions, the internal-connection loop and next-event estimation. Separately, a large but <em>finite</em> sample meets a hard clamp at 1000.0 with no UI at all, applied after the MIS weight; the source records that it replaced a pre-weight clamp of 100 which had been truncating bright glossy splats the path tracer counts in full.</p>
      <p>Neither instrument can see the fireflies this page is about. A fold weight is <span class="mono">W ~ &epsilon;<sup>&minus;1/2</sup></span>: enormous, and perfectly finite. <span class="mono">isnan</span> and <span class="mono">isinf</span> are false for every one of them, so the guard never fires on a grazing event &mdash; it is there for a division that produced a NaN, which is a different failure. Only the clamp touches a fold sample, and the clamp lives on the GPU megakernel.</p>
      <p>The film that actually carries this family has neither. In <span class="mono">PPIntegrator.cpp</span> the only finiteness test on a contribution is inside the camera-side splat of strategy 17; the main film write is an unguarded, unclamped running mean. That is a decision, not an oversight &mdash; a clamp is a bias, and this family&rsquo;s whole acceptance argument is a ratio against a reference that has no clamp either. It is also the reason the statistics used on the evidence page are a per-pixel median or a top-0.1&thinsp;% clipped total and never a raw frame energy. The 8.44 in section 03 is one pixel arriving in a film with nothing at all in its way.</p>
      <div class="readout">

<section id="explorer" style="--secc:var(--l460)" data-nav="Explorer">
  <div class="wrap">
    <div class="narrow">
      <p class="sec-label">02 — The Primitive Explorer</p>
      <h2>Ray-trace the manifold itself and jet-colour it by its own singularity</h2>
      <p class="lead">Tick one checkbox and the renderer stops estimating light and starts drawing the object it would have sampled. One representative primitive, ray-traced per pixel, deterministic, coloured blue where the camera ray meets the sheet face-on and red where it grazes. The red band is the firefly locus, drawn in the picture.</p>
      <p>This is the difference between a theorem and an instrument. The variance study above locates the singularity in parameter space, as a condition on tangent vectors. The explorer locates it <em>on screen</em>, in the same scene and from the same camera you are about to render from — the difference between knowing an estimator has a grazing band and knowing where that band is going to land across your caustic.</p>
      <p>Nothing is hardcoded per primitive. The tool evaluates <code>x(a,b) = y(u,v) + t·ω(θ,φ)</code> on a grid, with <span class="mono">a</span> and <span class="mono">b</span> picked from {u, v, θ, φ, t}, and tessellates the result — so the sphere, the cone, the meridian half-plane and the ruled plane all emerge from which two dimensions you chose to sweep, rather than from twelve special cases.</p>
    </div>
    <figure>
      {{SHOT:preset 35 · Render Settings ▸ PP Estimator ▸ Primitive Explorer (theory) ▸ "Trace this primitive in the viewport" ON · Jacobian resolution 80 · strategy 9 · capture the whole window so the theory readout and the 2D schematic are both in frame}}
      <figcaption><b>Explorer · strategy 09</b><span>Three things are on screen at once, and they are the reason the estimator work moved as fast as it did: the traced manifold with its grazing band, the algebraic readout of the same object, and a 2D schematic driven by the same evaluator.</span></figcaption>
    </figure>
    <div class="narrow">
      <h3>What is in the panel</h3>
      <ul class="clean">
        <li><b>The traced manifold.</b> Blocked AABB acceleration over the tessellated grid, so it stays interactive while you orbit. The representative light path is drawn over it — yellow node at the emitter, yellow leg along ω, white node at the scatter point — solid where it passes in front of the sheet and marching-dashed where it passes behind. The camera leg is not drawn, because you are sighting straight down it.</li>
        <li><b>The theory readout.</b> Swept dimensions, both tangent vectors, the Jacobian written out for that primitive (<span class="mono">t·sinθ·|θ̂·d|</span> for the cone), and a green <span class="mono">Σ free = 2 swept + 1 camera-ray = 3 → UNBIASED</span> check. Dimension counting is where this family's bugs live, so the count is on screen rather than in a comment.</li>
        <li><b>A distance-term class.</b> Orange <span class="mono">EXPECTED VALUE</span> when <span class="mono">t</span> is one of the swept dimensions and the transmittance is carried explicitly, blue <span class="mono">TRACK LENGTH</span> when <span class="mono">t</span> is free-flight sampled and the transmittance has already cancelled to an albedo. The viewport reflects it: swept-<span class="mono">t</span> primitives are dimmed along <span class="mono">t</span> and drawn long, sampled-<span class="mono">t</span> primitives are flat and drawn at about a mean free path.</li>
        <li><b>A mixture draws its whole set.</b> For strategies 12, 13 and 15 every member appears at once, alpha-composited, each in its own tint — and the MIS weighting toggle is reflected in the shading, so switching from flat to balance weights visibly <em>dims</em> each member's grazing band. The picture and the estimator agree because the singular member really does down-weight itself.</li>
      </ul>

    <div class="narrow">
      <h3>What it does not draw</h3>
      <p>The explorer covers twelve of the thirty-one strategy slots — 3, 4 and 7 through 16: the quad, sphere, disk, cone and plane primitives and three of the four mixtures. The rest fall through to a flat grey fill: the NEE baselines, the volume-light chords, the camera-side splat, the rasterised gather, the cross-family mixture, the glossy ring, the one-dimensional group, and <b>the entire hourglass family</b>, which is the research this renderer exists for. The tool that best explains the estimators does not yet draw the best one; the frozen batch in the next section does, stochastically, and that is where the hourglass is photographed.</p>
      <p>Two smaller limits, both worth knowing before a capture session. It needs an emissive quad in the scene — <code>findEmissiveQuad</code> failing gives the same flat grey as an unsupported strategy, so grey is ambiguous. And the manifold trace runs at a maximum internal width of 420 pixels and is nearest-neighbour upsampled to the viewport, so a full-screen capture is soft. It is cached against a camera fingerprint and re-traced only when the camera or the parameters move, which is what keeps the orbit smooth.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
</section>

<!-- ================= 03 · FROZEN BATCH ================= -->
<section id="batch" style="--secc:var(--l500)" data-nav="Frozen batch">
  <div class="wrap">
    <div class="narrow">
      <p class="sec-label">03 — The frozen batch</p>
      <h2>Stop redrawing the primitive every sample and the estimator becomes a picture</h2>
      <p class="lead">Normally each camera sample draws a fresh photon primitive, so the sheets average away before anything is visible. Freeze a batch of sixteen instead, share it across the whole frame, and skip the divide by N — and the estimator's own geometry appears, one hue per member, at its own brightness.</p>
      <p>The hero image at the top of this page is that picture, and it is the clearest explanation of the family that exists anywhere in this project. Sixteen photon cones, apexes coincident at the light, each a distinct hue, each with a thin incandescent outline where the camera ray runs tangent to it. It is the same object the explorer traces analytically, except that here it was <em>sampled</em>: these are real primitives that a real render drew, carrying real weights, and the bright outline is a real firefly locus rather than a colour ramp.</p>
    </div>
    <figure>
      {{DOCPNG:correlated/fig-cone-9.png}}
      <figcaption><b>Strategy 09 · preset 41 · N = 16 · all-N</b><span>What to notice is the apex. All sixteen conics meet at one point, because the apex of a photon cone is the light and all sixteen were seeded from the same emitter. The half-angle differs per member because cos θ is the sampled dimension; φ and t are the swept ones. This camera sits almost level with the light, so the conics degenerate to near-straight wedges — exactly as predicted, the shape is camera-versus-axis dependent, and this camera picked the degenerate case.</span></figcaption>
    </figure>
    <div class="narrow">
      <h3>Three modes, because they are three different pictures</h3>
    </div>
    <div class="tw"><table>
      <thead><tr><th><span class="mono">pp.correlated</span></th><th>Estimator</th><th>Cost / pass</th><th>What it looks like</th></tr></thead>
      <tbody>
        <tr><td>0 · off</td><td>a fresh primitive per camera sample</td><td>1×</td><td><b>Byte-identical</b> to before the feature existed</td></tr>
        <tr><td>1 · stippled</td><td>one uniformly chosen member of the batch per sample; no 1/N, one uniform pick is already the mean</td><td>1×</td><td>Neighbours show different sheets — the primitives read as dithered together</td></tr>
        <tr><td>2 · all-N</td><td>every pixel sums the whole batch and divides by N <b>drawn</b></td><td>N×</td><td>Clean hard-edged overlapping sheets, roughly N× less noise</td></tr>
      </tbody>
    </table></div>
    <div class="narrow">
      <p>It cost almost nothing to build, for a structural reason worth stating. <code>sampleOnePixel</code> already draws every photon-side random number <em>after</em> the camera ray is built, in every one of its strategy blocks. So correlating is a split of the RNG stream rather than a refactor: re-seed once, immediately after <code>cam.generateRay</code> returns, and every pixel handed batch index <span class="mono">k</span> reconstructs primitive <span class="mono">k</span> bit for bit. Not one strategy block was touched, and the mode covers strategies 0–16 and 18–30 — the mixtures, the hourglass family, the glossy ring and the one-dimensional group included; the swarm of rings and hooks in section 04b of <a href="photon-hourglass.html#onedim" style="color:var(--l460)">Caustics without a kernel</a> is that reach arriving on three strategies that did not exist when the mode was written, and it needed no code. Only strategy 17 opts out, because it is a whole-image splat pass that returns before the branch.</p>
      <p class="note"><b>The count that used to be in that sentence.</b> It read &ldquo;all nineteen strategy blocks&rdquo;, which was true when it was written and is a figure the source's own comment still carries at the correlated branch. Strategies 26 and 27 have blocks of their own now, and rather than print a new number that will go stale the same way, this page states the property that is actually load-bearing: <em>every</em> block draws its photon-side randomness after the camera ray, and the byte-identity guarantee is what proves none was missed.</p>
      <p>The palette is deliberately shared with the explorer: primitive <span class="mono">k</span> gets the same hue circle the explorer uses for strategy 15's orientation set, so a sheet in the deterministic picture and a sheet in the stochastic one are the same colour.</p>
      <h3>The one way to ship this quietly wrong</h3>
      <p>The divisor is N <em>drawn</em>, never N that crossed the camera ray. Dividing by the crossing count inflates the image by the reciprocal of the crossing probability. That was built behind a debug flag, fired on purpose, measured, and deleted:</p>
    </div>
    <div class="readout">
      <div class="row"><span class="lbl">Frame energy, wrong divisor / reference</span><span class="val">2.1× – 6.8×</span></div>
      <div class="row"><span class="lbl">Per lit pixel, wrong / correct</span><span class="val">6.6× – 14.2×</span></div>
      <div class="row hi"><span class="lbl">Wrong / reference at 150 / 600 / 2400 passes</span><span class="val">4.39 <span class="d">/</span> 4.45 <span class="d">/</span> 4.45</span></div>
      <div class="row"><span class="lbl">Correct / reference, same passes</span><span class="val">0.96 <span class="d">/</span> 0.99 <span class="d">/</span> 0.99</span></div>

    <div class="narrow">
      <p class="note"><b>Read the third row again.</b> The wrong normalisation is flat to 1.5% over a sixteen-fold range in pass count. It does not flicker, it does not drift, it does not look noisy. It converges cleanly to four and a half times the right answer, and nothing in the image says so. This is the failure mode a picture cannot catch and only a reference can.</p>
      <p>The design record's own prediction was 2.5×–3.7×, from a Python model of the cone. The renderer's answer is 2.1×–6.8× on frame energy, because the crossing probability in these presets is much smaller than in the model's geometry. Same mechanism, bigger multiplier here — so the page quotes the mechanism, and the correction stands in the record next to the prediction it replaced.</p>
      <h3>How it was accepted</h3>
      <p>Twelve films were pinned from the pre-change binary and re-rendered from the final one at 300 passes each, across three presets and ten strategies. All twelve byte-identical, checked twice — once with the debug trap compiled in and once after removing it. Then the correlated films were compared against the uncorrelated reference at 2400 passes:</p>

    <div class="narrow">
      <p>The last two rows are the fold row of section 01 arriving in the shipped renderer.       <p class="note"><b>And one thing the frozen mode is not, by design.</b> Freezing the batch across passes converges the film to <em>that batch's realisation</em>, not to L. More camera samples only clean up the camera side; the light-side error is permanent. The UI says so in orange while the toggle is on. Relatedly, the per-pixel variance of a correlated pass is not an error estimate — the correlation coefficient is independent of N, so a bigger batch shrinks the amplitude of the structure without changing its shape, and any adaptive sampler or denoiser reading that variance is being lied to.</p>

<!-- ================= 05 · PREDICTIONS THAT FAILED ================= -->
<section id="wrong" style="--secc:var(--l580)" data-nav="Wrong twice">
  <div class="wrap">
    <div class="narrow">
      <p class="sec-label">05 — Two predictions that did not survive</p>
      <h2>The design record predicted every frame before it existed. Six landed as predicted; two did not, for the same reason</h2>
      <p class="lead">Section 3 of the correlated-mode design record predicted every frame in the gallery above before any of them existed, reading each prediction off the variance classification. Six landed as predicted. Two did not, and neither failure was in the mathematics — both were geometry that no preset in this repository can produce.</p>

      <h3>The disk's pinwheel is unreachable</h3>
      <p>Predicted: a pinwheel of blades with a dark hole through the light. What appeared: a fan of vertical blades hanging off the light, with a bright seam where the hole was supposed to be.</p>
      <p>The blades are half-planes that all contain the light's axis, so a pinwheel is what you see looking <em>down</em> that axis. The axis is the emitter's normal for an area light and world-up for a point light, so in every scene here it is vertical — and every PP preset points the camera level. The pinwheel view would require looking straight down at the ceiling light, and no preset does. From the side it projects to a fan; on preset 41, with the camera almost at the light's own height, it degenerates further into two half-frames split by the axis. The predicted dark column is there, but neighbouring blades close over it, so it reads bright.</p>

      <h3>The quad's tangency is one scanline, not scattered slivers</h3>
      <p>Predicted: a few blinding thin slivers among ordinary patches, wherever a quad happened to be seen edge-on. What appeared: a single incandescent line straight across the frame at the camera's own eye height, carrying essentially all of the estimator's variance.</p>
      <p>Strategy 3 <em>translates</em> the emissive quad along ω; it does not rotate it. A ceiling quad therefore stays horizontal at every distance, and a level camera sees every member of the batch near edge-on at exactly one height — its own. The slivers are not scattered because nothing scatters them. The same geometry explains why this family needs a batch in the thousands before anything crosses the frame: its screen footprint is small <em>because</em> of the edge-on geometry, not incidentally.</p>

      <p class="note"><b>Why this stays on the page.</b> Both misses came from reasoning about a sheet in isolation and forgetting that a camera has to look at it — a specific, repeatable failure mode, and the same one the triage document records after three confident wrong conclusions in a single session, each settled by one counter. The rule it left behind is the reason the two tools above exist: instrument first.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
</section>

      <p>Two independent estimator passes over the same scene and the same camera, composited as a wipe in the main viewport with a draggable divider, plus a radio for which side advances so you can freeze one and let the other climb. And identical settings on both sides give bit-identical panes, because the two sides share an RNG stream. That is correct and it reads as broken, so it is the first thing to test in a capture session.</p>

      <h3>The Photon Primitive Validator (PPE)</h3>
      <p>A separate tool with a different job: it visualises sampling geometry, it does not estimate. It is also a different object from the path-type filter on the tracer&rsquo;s own Filters tab, which does filter.</p>
      <p><b>The reachability cull carries this project&rsquo;s byte-identity convention into a visualiser.</b> With the gate off, per-photon visibility is left at 1.0 and the march shader&rsquo;s own comment records that the output is byte-identical to the behaviour before the feature existed. With it on, every segment of the reconstructed sub-path gets an any-hit test, and the photon is culled on the first blocked one. The tests are written inside the march shader to mirror the CPU intersector exactly &mdash; the comment says <em>same epsilons, same clip and slab arithmetic</em>, and the same <span class="mono">minT</span> semantics &mdash; because a cull that disagreed with the renderer&rsquo;s own intersector would delete photons the estimator can genuinely reach, and a picture of a sampling geometry that quietly drops reachable samples is worse than no picture.</p>
      <p>Underneath it is a taxonomy: a header-only classifier that maps a set of swept dimensions to an analytical shape, unit-tested by a target that links glm and nothing else. The test binary carries 61 assertions across two files — the shape classifier and the evaluator — and returns non-zero on any failure, so it doubles as a gate.</p>
      <p>Self-described status is <b>ongoing</b>, and the document says where. The analytic tracer handles sphere, cone, disk, parallelogram, parallelepiped, planar sector and ball sector; the higher-dimensional shapes have no closed form and are not traced. The rasterised MIS mode has a cache-timing bug where the density sometimes only appears after a resample, and its own notes propose deprecating it in favour of doing MIS in the analytic tracer instead.</p>

      <h3>The overlay bank, and what it reveals</h3>
      <p>Fifteen overlay draw functions cover the rest of the renderer — seven of them per-integrator path and photon visualisations for the photon mapper, VCM, MLT, PSSMLT, UPBP, path guiding and manifold exploration, plus ray differentials, normal arrows, the light tree, the acceleration structures and the BDPT strategy pyramid.</p>
      <p>The acceleration overlay is worth calling out because of what it reveals rather than what it draws. The BVH and the uniform grid are built, and the overlay is their <em>only</em> consumer — nothing traverses them on either the CPU or the GPU, where the tracer is a flat linear scan over all primitives. The visualiser is drawing a data structure the renderer does not use.</p>
      <p>All of the estimator-side drawing shares one glyph vocabulary: cyan for the camera side, warm yellow for the photon side, a red centre dot for a swept or specular vertex, a dashed ring for a camera or light endpoint and a solid ring for an emitter or a surface. The same vocabulary is used by the in-app 2D schematic, the 3D overlay, and the schematics drawn for these pages — which is why a diagram here and a screenshot from the app read as the same object.</p>
