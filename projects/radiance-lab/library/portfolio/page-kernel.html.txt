<section id="problem" style="--secc:var(--l400)" data-nav="Problem">
  <div class="wrap">
    <div class="narrow">
      <p class="sec-label">01 — The problem</p>
      <h2>A single grazing ray can ruin the whole frame</h2>
      <p class="lead">Volumetric caustics are the light that has already passed through glass before it scatters in fog. They are among the hardest paths in rendering: the light is focused to a thin, bright sheet, and almost no random path finds it.</p>
      <p>The usual answer is photon mapping — fire photons, store them, and gather with a kernel of radius <em>r</em>. That works, but the radius is a lie you agree to: it blurs the caustic and biases the answer, and shrinking it trades the blur for noise. This page is about refusing the lie and keeping the speed.</p>
      <p>An analytic estimator avoids the kernel entirely, but buys a new problem. Sweeping a photon into a surface and intersecting the camera ray with it introduces a <strong>Jacobian in the denominator</strong> — and when the camera ray grazes that surface tangentially, the Jacobian collapses toward zero and the estimator explodes.</p>
    </div>
    <figure>
      {{SVG:f09-photon-cone.svg}}
      <figcaption><b>Schematic</b><span>The photon cone: emission direction fixed in polar angle, swept in azimuth and distance. Where the camera ray runs tangent to the cone the co-area Jacobian vanishes — that arc is the firefly locus.</span></figcaption>
    </figure>
    <div class="narrow">
      <p class="note"><b>A photograph used to sit here, and it was pulled on 2026-09-08.</b> It showed the photon-cone estimator alone on preset 41 at 400 passes and its caption called the bright pixels the tangency locus, at <span class="mono">1189.8&times;</span> the scene mean. When every film on this site was regenerated from its own producer, that one did not come back: the estimator's own recorded config produces a frame with a twentieth of the shipped film's mean, and on this build the mixture that is supposed to fix it has the <em>higher</em> peak. The frames and the full retraction are kept in section 03 rather than deleted, but they are not evidence for this section's claim, so this section no longer shows them. <b>That the cone is the family's worst primitive is measured elsewhere and stands</b>: worst pixel against the reference, <b>6291 &rarr; 1</b> once it is mixed &mdash; the stat tile at the top of this page.</p>
    </div>

</section>

<!-- ================= IDEA ================= -->
<section id="idea" style="--secc:var(--l460)" data-nav="The idea">
  <div class="wrap">
    <div class="narrow">
      <p class="sec-label">02 — The idea</p>
      <h2>Sweep the photon into a surface you can intersect</h2>
      <p class="lead">A photon arriving at a scattering event still has unused degrees of freedom. Rather than committing them randomly and storing the result, let them sweep.</p>
      <p>Fix every dimension of a light path but two, and let those two vary. The scatter point traces out an analytic two-dimensional manifold in space — a sphere, a cone, a disk, a ruled plane. That manifold is a <strong>photon primitive</strong>, and a camera ray can be intersected with it exactly.</p>
      <p>The kernel disappears. In its place is a co-area Jacobian relating the swept parameters to the measure the camera ray actually integrates:</p>
    </div>
    <div class="formula">
      <div class="f">J = <span class="hl">|</span> ( ∂x/∂a × ∂x/∂b ) · d <span class="hl">|</span></div>
      <div class="n">One expression for every primitive in the family. The shape changes; the estimator does not.</div>
    </div>
    <div class="pair">
      <div class="pane">{{SVG:f01-nee-longball.svg}}</div>
      <div class="pane">{{SVG:f07-photon-sphere.svg}}</div>
    </div>
    <div class="paircap"><b>Schematics</b><span>Left: the reference — a light point connected to a scatter point, no sweep. Right: the photon sphere — sweep the emission direction at a fixed free-flight distance and the scatter point traces a sphere about the light; the camera ray meets it at isolated points, and the two tangents at that point are the Jacobian.</span></div>
    <div class="narrow">
      <p>Because the manifold is exact, the estimate is unbiased with <em>no radius parameter at all</em> — the caustic is as sharp as the geometry, not as sharp as your kernel. What remains is the variance problem from section one, and it has an answer that costs nothing but bookkeeping.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
</section>

Section 04b is where three more arrived, and why they had to.       <p class="sec-label">04 — Why a diverse set wins</p>
      <h2>The singular estimator down-weights itself</h2>
      <p class="lead">Each primitive has a grazing locus. The observation that makes the family usable is that <em>they are in different places</em>.</p>
      <p>Where one primitive's Jacobian collapses, another's is well-conditioned — and the balance heuristic weights each sample by how well every strategy could have generated it. The member that is about to produce a firefly is precisely the member that assigns itself a low weight. The singularity does not need to be clamped, guarded, or clipped away. It cancels.</p>
    </div>

      <h3>Which mixture, at which light size — and the two predictions the rig overturned</h3>
      <p>The two mixtures are built for different regimes. A second prediction sat beside it for the caustic: the vertex-seeded hourglass (22) overtakes the light-seeded one (20) at 35–41°.</p>

The light-seeded hourglass beats the vertex one at every size, by 236× down to 1.9×. Both trends run the predicted way, so the crossovers exist — beyond the rig, near an emitter edge of 9–10 for the airlight pair and further still for the caustic, where the lamp would be nearly half the width of the fog box. The miss was on the side the study trusted most: the trio's variance floor is 1.10 per pass, not 3.8, and it rises 1.35× over a 1,600× increase in emitter area, not 2×. Both corrections are written into the study beside the predictions they replace.</p>

Fixed the same day; the trio now reads 1.016, and the mixture inherits the fix.</p>

That is the honest boundary of this family. Sweeping a dimension of an area light's single scatter does not beat connecting to it; what sweeping buys is the mixture mechanism above, and the connections next-event estimation cannot make at all. Hold on to that sentence — the next section reproduces it exactly, on a different construction, and the section after that is where sweeping stops being optional.</p>

<section id="hourglass" style="--secc:var(--l580)" data-nav="Hourglass">
  <div class="wrap">
    <div class="narrow">
      <p class="sec-label">05 — The contribution</p>
      <h2>An analytic caustic through a glass sphere</h2>
      <p class="lead">Refraction is where analytic connections stop. A path that bends at two dielectric interfaces has no closed-form connection — which is why caustics have been left to photons and kernels. This one is not.</p>
      <p>For a spherical dielectric there is a symmetry that rescues it. Take the axis <code>a = normalize(C − y)</code> from the light through the sphere's centre. The entire refraction chain — entry, interior, exit — is <strong>rotationally symmetric about that axis</strong>. Rotating the whole chain is a rigid motion, so the exit rays sweep a surface of revolution: a waisted, hourglass-shaped caustic envelope that can be intersected in closed form.</p>
    </div>

    <figure>
      {{SVG:f20-hourglass.svg}}
      <figcaption><b>Schematic</b><span>The meridian chain at φ = 0 — emitted at polar angle ψ, refracted in at x₁, out at x₂ — and its revolution about the axis <code>a</code>. The exit rays sweep a waisted surface; the camera ray meets it where the white ring sits, and the two tangents there are <code>a × (x − y)</code> and <code>ω_out</code>.</span></figcaption>
    </figure>
    <div class="formula">
      <div class="f">J = <span class="hl">|</span> ( ( a × (x − y) ) × ω<sub>out</sub> ) · d <span class="hl">|</span></div>
      <div class="n">The co-area Jacobian of the swept exit surface. The fold dilation <code>det3</code> cancels per branch,<br>so the MIS weights are closed-form and O(1) — no root-finding anywhere in the estimator.</div>
    </div>

    <div class="narrow">
      <p>Two properties make it a result rather than a trick. First, the density <code>p = Σ p<sub>c</sub>·|J| / |det3|</code> was derived four independent ways and then <em>measured</em> against the sampler, agreeing to 1.0007. Second, as the index of refraction approaches 1 the hourglass degenerates exactly onto the photon cone — strategy 09 — which pins the measure convention against an estimator that was already validated. That limit matches to nine digits.</p>

    <h3 style="max-width:720px">How far the sheet can be pushed, and where it refuses</h3>
    <div class="narrow">
      <p class="lead">Three extensions landed after the hourglass itself, and they share a method worth naming before the numbers: each one asks whether some new transport is still a <em>rigid motion</em> of the same swept surface. Where it is, the existing quadric solver is untouched and the extension is exact. Where it is not, the estimator does not approximate &mdash; it refuses, by name, in the log.</p>
      <ul class="clean">
        <li><b>The refraction chain.</b> Push the sheet through a chain of glass balls instead of one. The revolution about the axis from the light to the first centre is a rigid motion of the <em>whole chain</em> if and only if every centre lies on that line — so a collinear chain costs nothing in the solver and a ball off the axis is refused rather than approximated. Which also makes it a <em>point</em>-source result: with a finite emitter the axis moves every sample, and the measured mean perpendicular offset for a half-0.4 quad is 0.395, with fifteen samples in a hundred thousand inside tolerance. The spec did not carry that correction; the measurement did.</li>
        <li><b>The post-ball mirror.</b> Light, ball, fog, then a mirror, then the eye. A reflection is an isometry, so the camera ray is reflected in the plane and the <em>same</em> quadric is solved against it; a root survives only when the bent path is real at every leg. It composes with the light-side relay and with the chain, and it is the one flag that turns the rasterised gather off — the screen bound projects the sheet where it is, not where its mirror image is seen.</li>
        <li><b>The glossy ring.</b> Strategy 24's reflection cone taken off the delta: the family's first vertex on a <em>rough</em> surface. A glossy vertex imposes no reflection law, so where every other member solves for the light point and the polar angle, this one samples both — five free dimensions rather than three — and evaluates the BRDF at every crossing rather than once.</li>

<section id="proof" style="--secc:var(--l620)" data-nav="Proof">
  <div class="wrap">
    <div class="narrow">
      <p class="sec-label">06 — How it was proven</p>
      <h2>Two independent estimators, or it didn't happen</h2>
      <p class="lead">Every primitive in the family had to divide against something it shares no code with, and land on 1.000. Every row that claims it has the run behind it; the ones that land elsewhere — the solo hourglass at 0.976, the beam at 1.048, the reflection cone at 0.998 — say so in the ledger, and the pair closes the first to 1.0000.</p>
      <ul class="clean">
        <li><b>Ratio against the oracle.</b> Each estimator renders the same configuration as the NEE reference until the per-pixel median ratio converges to 1.000. Median, not mean — a single firefly cannot hide a systematic error.</li>
        <li><b>Byte-identical regression.</b> The strongest test available, and the one that caught the most. A refactor that should not change the image must produce a bit-identical file; an extra random draw shifting the stream, or a gate that quietly became more permissive, shows up nowhere else.</li>
        <li><b>Degenerate limits as measure pins.</b> ior→1 collapses the hourglass onto the cone; σ→0 collapses a stochastic surface onto its mean. Limits where a new estimator becomes an old one are free, exact tests.</li>
        <li><b>Four-way derivation, then measurement.</b> The MIS density was derived independently four times before being compared against the sampler's empirical histogram. Agreement to 1.0007 — the derivation and the code were checked against each other, not against expectation.</li>
      </ul>
      <p class="note"><b>The habit that mattered most was negative.</b> Reasoning about why an image is dark does not work. One investigation ran for several rounds on careful inference, reached a confident wrong conclusion, and recorded it in a commit message as settled. A single counter in the actual code overturned it in one run: 27657 rays, 15131 hits, 63% correctly blocked. The bug was elsewhere. Instrument first.</p>

      <p class="sec-label">07 — What it does not do</p>
      <h2>The honest boundary</h2>
      <p class="lead">The scope is stated as precisely as the results, because a boundary you can name is what makes a result usable. These six are the boundary, and the first one is the thesis of the whole family.</p>
      <ul class="clean">
        <li><b>Not a better sampler for easy paths.</b> A swept primitive is more machinery than a light sample and a shadow ray, and on transport that was easy to sample to begin with, the machinery does not pay. Single scatter from an area light is the measured case: the camera walk's next-event estimate beats every swept member at every emitter size, 2.2 to 3.1× per pass. The family earns its cost on the paths that have no connection to make: the caustic through glass, where a connection would have to bend; the emitter too sharp to hit by sampling; the light source with more dimensions than a point sample can cover. There it lands what path sampling finds only by chance and what kernels can only blur. The hourglass is the proof; the sharp emitter and the volumetric light are the next two cases to measure.</li>
        <li><b>Medium-only.</b> Surfaces occlude and emit, but do not bounce. Walls look dark next to a full path tracer — that is the declared scope, and it is why every comparison here is against a medium-only reference that describes exactly the same transport.</li>
        <li><b>Straight-line primitives cannot refract.</b> Strategies 0–17 and the cross-family mixture at 26 are exact connections; none of them can bend at a dielectric interface. A glass ball under those estimators casts a correct shadow and no caustic. Neither can the one-dimensional group at 28&ndash;30, and there the failure is sharper than dimness: a smooth dielectric's emission profile is a two-dimensional delta, integrating a delta over one swept coordinate leaves a delta, and those three read <em>identically zero</em>. Structural, and the reason the hourglass exists. Strategy 27 is the one member that turns at a surface without being a dielectric &mdash; it reflects at a rough one, and still does not refract.</li>
        <li><b>The hourglass needs rotational symmetry, and now it is tested.</b> It is derived for spherical dielectrics: a glass box or a lens has no axis about which the refraction chain is invariant, and the derivation does not survive. The chain extension is the sharpest statement of that boundary, because it makes the condition <em>checkable at run time</em> — several balls are still one rigid motion if and only if every centre lies on the light-to-first-centre line, and the estimator tests that and refuses when it fails. It is also why the chain is a point-source result: a finite emitter moves the axis every sample. A fixed-apex form for the general case is derived and numerically validated; its light-side closure is measure-zero, and a standalone harness has since costed the general chain at fourteen to forty-two times one-ball strategy 20, which settles it as a no-go rather than an open problem.</li>
        <li><b>Solo, it is 2.4% low; paired, it is 1.0000.</b> The pairing with the stochastic beam, which carries internal-reflection chains at weight one, closes the gap: 0.976 → 1.0000. The attribution of the solo shortfall is not settled. The hourglass cannot express internal-reflection chains, which is the obvious explanation — but the grazing guard discards samples rather than clamping them, and the variance study measures it rejecting <em>2.4% of this strategy's energy at the waist</em> in the same configuration. The two candidate explanations are numerically the same size and have not been separated.</li>
        <li><b>Paired, it is consistent, not unbiased.</b> The pair's energy on the isolated caustic moves with the beam radius it carries — +0.9 % at r = 0.15, +1.9 % at r = 0.30, first order in r and resolved at 20 and 44 sigma on the paired bar — while the beam alone is flat in r to 0.2 %. Its waist does not move: 7.7 px at r = 0.15, 7.5 at r = 0.05 on the Gate. That is the same structure complement mode refuses strategy 30 for; the difference is that the pair's box is the one no other estimator can own, so it ships as the production primary with the number beside it rather than without it.</li>
      </ul>
