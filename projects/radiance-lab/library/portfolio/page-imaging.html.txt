      <p class="sec-label">01 &mdash; What is actually here</p>
      <h2>An instrument that scores its own answers, and has never been photographed</h2>
      <p class="lead">What is here, in one breath. A camera-mounted structured-light projector that is a real next-event light, an amplitude-modulation sensor model with four independent phase terms, four-shot continuous-wave time-of-flight capture, three holographic reconstructions, and a weighted-least-squares phase unwrapper solved by preconditioned conjugate gradient on the GPU. All of it builds and runs. None of it appears in a single figure anywhere in the repository &mdash; the project's own capture inventory lists it as the subsystem its earlier sweeps missed, and the one to shoot early.</p>
      <p>Unphotographed is a fact about the photography, not about the instrument. What this one does when you run it is the reason the page exists.</p>
      <ul class="clean">
        <li><b>It walks the whole inverse problem on screen.</b> One combo, fourteen entries, from the ground-truth depth through the synthesised hologram, the spectrum, the crop mask, the cropped spectrum, the inverse transform, the recovered phase, the recovered depth and the error against truth &mdash; plus a six-panel overview, a four-panel comparison, the amplitude mask, the solved unwrapped phase, and the phase residual (<code>UIManagerRender.cpp:4376-4391</code>). Every intermediate a textbook would draw is a texture you can put on the viewport.</li>
        <li><b>It grades itself in physical units.</b> Mean, RMS, minimum and maximum over the valid mask, reported in <em>wraps of object phase</em> with the gauge freedom of least-squares unwrapping explicitly cancelled, and, in the V2 panel, the solver&rsquo;s iteration count and residual &mdash; and it prints the value it expects, so a sign or scale error is legible without leaving the panel (<code>UIManagerRender.cpp:4749-4755</code>; the FFT panel prints the same four numbers without the solver line at <code>:4411-4422</code>).</li>
        <li><b>It diagnoses your scene.</b> Two one-click presets configure the projector, the carrier, the light strategy and the environment together, and beneath them ten pass-or-warn rows say <em>why</em> your fringes are missing: environment light adding a DC haze, no reference carrier so the sideband sits at DC, a cone projector whose carrier direction varies with angle (<code>:4657-4703</code>). Nothing else in this codebase diagnoses its own setup.</li>
      </ul>
      <p>So each section does two things. It states the capability, points at where it lives and says what it produces &mdash; and then carries the capture instruction that would put a photograph behind it. A reader who wants to know whether this works has a shooting list with the expected result already written in. A reader who wants to know whether it is <em>worth</em> shooting has the fourteen stages, the residual readout and the advisor, each with an anchor.</p>

<!-- ================= THE LAMP ================= -->
<section id="lamp" style="--secc:var(--l460)" data-nav="The lamp">
  <div class="wrap">
    <div class="narrow">
      <p class="sec-label">02 &mdash; The lamp, before the measurement</p>
      <h2>Everything else on this page treats the projector as a carrier. It is a light</h2>
      <p class="lead">The projector appears forty-odd times on this page and every one of those mentions is about phase: what it modulates, what it carries, what a decoder recovers from it. That is a real use and it is not the whole object. Underneath the modulation is a next-event light source with four distinct delta geometries, four falloff laws, seven patterns, a beam angle that is <em>solved</em> rather than typed, and a plan view of the entire rig &mdash; none of which any figure on this site has shown, and none of which the rest of this page needs in order to make its argument.</p>
      <p>The four shapes are not four presets on one light. Each is a different delta geometry with its own shadow-ray construction and its own distance law, which is exactly what makes the set useful: put the same power through all four and the differences in the image are differences in the physics, not in the artwork.</p>
    </div>
    <div class="tw"><table>
      <thead><tr><th>Shape</th><th>Geometry</th><th>Falloff</th><th>In a mirror?</th></tr></thead>
      <tbody>
        <tr class="key"><td>Point source</td><td>A cone from the camera position; UV is perspective, so a pattern projects the way a slide projector's does</td><td><b>1/r&sup2;</b>, inverse square</td><td><b>Yes</b> &mdash; a 0.05 m aperture disk</td></tr>
        <tr><td>Quad laser panel</td><td>A collimated plane wave covering everything the camera sees; the wavefront is flat</td><td><b>None.</b> Collimated light does not spread</td><td>No disk exists</td></tr>
        <tr><td>Spinning slit laser</td><td>A line emitter through the camera position, oriented by a sweep angle from &minus;90&deg; to +90&deg;; light reaches a surface from the nearest point on the line</td><td><b>1/r</b>, cylindrical spreading about the line</td><td>No disk exists</td></tr>
        <tr class="key"><td>Flashlight</td><td>A cone with a smoothstep halo and a <em>physical</em> emission-disk radius you set</td><td><b>1/r&sup2;</b>, with a soft edge</td><td><b>Yes</b> &mdash; at the radius you set</td></tr>
      </tbody>
    </table></div>
    <div class="narrow">
      <p>That last column is the one a reader would not guess. At any bounce past the first, the ray marches against an aperture disk sitting at the camera and facing backwards, and if it lands inside, the projector's own emission is returned and the path terminates &mdash; absorbed by the light source, which is what a real lamp does to a ray that finds it. So the projector <strong>appears in mirrors and in glass</strong>, correctly, as a bright disk. Two of the four shapes have such a disk and two do not, and the asymmetry is not arbitrary: a collimated panel and an infinite line have no aperture to hit.</p>

      <h3>The beam angle is derived, and the derivation is different for each shape</h3>
      <p>There is no &ldquo;cone angle&rdquo; slider. You size a quad, place it at a depth, set how far behind it the emitter sits, and the half-angle falls out &mdash; clamped to between 0.5&deg; and 89&deg; so it can never be degenerate. The arithmetic is shape-dependent, and one branch of it is worth reading closely:</p>
    </div>
    <div class="formula">
      <div class="f">point &nbsp;&rarr;&nbsp; atan2( <span class="hl">&radic;(W&sup2;+H&sup2;)</span>, emitterDist + quadZ )<br>
      line &nbsp;&nbsp;&rarr;&nbsp; atan2( <span class="hl">W</span>, emitterDist + quadZ )<br>
      panel &nbsp;&rarr;&nbsp; atan2( <span class="hl">W</span>, <span class="hl">1.0</span> )</div>
      <div class="n">The point source uses the quad's diagonal over the full setback; the line uses the width alone, because a line has no vertical extent to spread. The panel uses width at <em>unit</em> depth and ignores the depth you set &mdash; which is defensible for a collimated beam that does not spread, and is nowhere explained in the panel. All three are then clamped to [0.5&deg;, 89&deg;].</div>
    </div>
    <div class="narrow">
      <p>On top of the derivation sit three fitting aids, and the third is the one that makes the four shapes comparable at all. <em>Match Image Plane</em> puts the quad at the camera's focus distance spanning the horizontal field of view; <em>Match Aperture</em> puts the emitter at zero and sets the cone equal to the camera's field of view. <em>Size Matching</em> goes further: it solves the beam angle from a desired half-width at a reference depth, so &ldquo;point versus panel versus line&rdquo; becomes a controlled comparison at a fixed illuminated area rather than three differently-sized beams. It is also, incidentally, one of two controls that survived this project's own inert-control audit by reaching the renderer through a derived write rather than a direct one.</p>

      <h3>The plan view nobody has looked at</h3>
      <p>Beside all of this is a draggable top-down window that draws the whole rig in the XZ plane: the scene's primitives as circles, the camera frustum, the image and focal planes, the effective quad, the emitter's position behind it, a translucent beam cone with seven fan rays &mdash; and, when the path-length gate is on, <strong>the time-of-flight wavefront itself</strong>, drawn as an expanding arc for the point and line sources and as a straight advancing line for the panel.</p>
      <p class="note"><b>That last detail is this page's own argument, drawn.</b> &sect;04 spends several paragraphs, and a whole clip, on the difference between a curved wavefront and a flat one and what each does to a recovered depth. The renderer already draws that difference, in a plan view, live, next to the controls that cause it. It has never been photographed, and it is the first shot on this page's list for that reason.</p>

      <h3>Seven patterns, and two debug switches with physics in them</h3>
      <p>Grid, Checker, Rings, Bars, Gobo/Star, Gradient and Plain, evaluated in projector UV with a repeat-scale slider. <em>Plain</em> is not a null option: it is uniform emission, which is what makes interference fringes readable with no pattern printed on top of them. Beside the pattern list sit two controls that look like debug toggles and are really experiment design. <em>Ignore Lambert</em> bypasses the cosine and the light's directional term so every surface is equally bright &mdash; built to verify a wavefront's <em>shape</em> without brightness confounding it. And <em>Path Offset</em> shifts the effective path length uniformly, so the curvature of a point, panel and line wavefront can be compared at one time offset instead of three.</p>

      <h3>Where the lamp is not</h3>
      <ul class="clean">
        <li><b>Under naive path tracing it renders black</b>, and that is correct rather than broken: a delta light has zero measure and cannot be found by BSDF sampling. It is also skipped for the two pure light-tracing strategies.</li>
        <li><b>No CPU integrator sees it at all.</b> Every projector uniform is written by the megakernel's uploader and the rasteriser, and by nothing else &mdash; so the entire imaging subsystem is a GPU-only object, in a renderer whose research half is on the CPU.</li>
      </ul>

      <h3>And there is a second projector</h3>
      <p>The node editor carries an unrelated one. Wire a Camera node &mdash; which snapshots the live view-projection and basis at one button press &mdash; and a Texture node into a DataMosh Projector, and a photograph is cast into the scene from that frozen pose. The photograph need not come from a file: the frame loop snapshots the <em>clean, pre-mosh</em> display texture into any Texture node that has asked for a capture (<code>EngineRender.cpp:4127-4130</code>), so the renderer can photograph itself and then project the result back in. Two of the six modes are the ones a reader will want first: <b>Light cone</b> and <b>Light plane</b> turn the image into <em>emitted light</em> &mdash; a perspective cone from the captured apex, or an orthographic plane of a given world size, with an intensity slider. A photograph, used as a luminaire, inside a path tracer.</p>
      <p>The other four are not variations on that one idea. They enter the renderer at four different places, and two of them never enter the path tracer at all: the tracer's copy of the projector is switched on only for modes 1, 3 and 4 (<code>EngineRender.cpp:1709-1711</code>). So <b>mode 2 &mdash; Forward</b> is a rasteriser-only mode, and mode 0 &mdash; Reproject never reaches either renderer at all. <b>Mode 1 &mdash; Project</b> is the one that runs in both, and its two sub-branches do opposite things to the surface they land on. The gate is easy to miss, because the combo offers all six under one node with no indication that they run in different places.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="narrow">
      <p><b>The two halves of mode 1 are worth separating</b>, because only one of them is a photograph. <em>Re-lit</em> overwrites the surface albedo and then zeroes that surface's emission, so projecting onto an emitter silences it. <em>Unlit</em> adds the photo colour to the radiance and immediately <code>break</code>s the path &mdash; the surface stops being a surface and becomes the picture. And both light modes and the unlit branch are skipped whenever the light strategy is 3, 4 or 7, the two pure light-tracing strategies and Light Sample Only, because each adds its contribution at the hit rather than through a connection. The re-lit branch is the exception: it changes an albedo, so it survives every strategy.</p>
      <p class="note"><b>A grade corrected.</b> The landing page graded the projector <span class="st st-b">seen</span> until 2026-09-06, on the strength of everything else on this page. But everything else on this page uses it as a carrier for phase; not one figure, anywhere in the repository, shows what it looks like as a lamp. The catalogue's projector block runs to thirteen rows and grades <span class="st st-c">never viewed</span> every one of the ten that is a capability rather than a caveat &mdash; the lamp itself, the four shapes, the seven patterns, the derived beam geometry, the two fitting buttons, Size Matching, visibility in reflections, the plan view, Ignore Lambert and Path Offset. The other three are the two limitations named above and a debug frustum. It is right, and the ledger row now agrees with it.</p>

<section id="modulation" style="--secc:var(--l460)" data-nav="Modulation">
  <div class="wrap">
    <div class="narrow">
      <p class="sec-label">03 &mdash; Where the measurement enters</p>
      <h2>The modulation lives inside the path tracer, not after it</h2>
      <p class="lead">The projector is not a post-process. It is a delta light rigidly attached to the camera, sampled by next-event estimation inside the megakernel (<code>pathTrace.comp:2050-2062</code>), and the amplitude modulation is applied at that connection as a function of the path length the tracer actually walked &mdash; including, optionally, the whole multi-bounce eye path.</p>
      <p>A continuous-wave time-of-flight camera emits amplitude-modulated light and correlates the return against a reference. What a pixel measures is a cosine of a sum of phases, and the interesting design decision here is that those phases are kept <em>separate</em> rather than collapsed into one offset. There are four of them, entering at four different physical places.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="formula">
      <div class="f">I = base &middot; ( 1 &minus; c/2 + (c/2)&middot;cos( <span class="hl">&phi;<sub>path</sub></span> &minus; <span class="hl">&phi;<sub>emit</sub></span> &minus; <span class="hl">&phi;<sub>demod</sub></span> &minus; <span class="hl">&phi;<sub>sensor</sub></span> ) )</div>
      <div class="n">&phi;<sub>path</sub> = 2&pi;&middot;f&middot;L, the round-trip path length &middot; &phi;<sub>emit</sub> = 2&pi;(e<sub>X</sub>&middot;u<sub>proj</sub> + e<sub>Y</sub>&middot;v<sub>proj</sub>), a spatial phase ramp on the emitter, which is a tilted or scanning wavefront &middot; &phi;<sub>demod</sub>, the correlation bucket offset &middot; &phi;<sub>sensor</sub> = 2&pi;(r<sub>X</sub>&middot;u + r<sub>Y</sub>&middot;v), a rolling shutter, which is scene-independent by construction. <code>shaders/modules/pt_sky.glsl:246-307</code></div>

    </figure>

    <div class="narrow">
      <h3>Limits, in the same breath</h3>
      <ul class="clean">
        <li><b>The projector is collocated with the camera.</b> There is no projector position uniform anywhere in the kernel; every evaluation is relative to <code>uCamPos</code>. The baseline is exactly zero, so triangulation is not merely unimplemented, it is geometrically impossible in this configuration. Depth here comes from phase, never from disparity.</li>
        <li><b>Tracer only.</b> The <code>uProjector*</code> uniforms are uploaded from one place, the projector block at <code>ComputePathTracer.cpp:1939</code>, and from nowhere else in <code>src/render/</code>. The rasteriser's <code>uProjector*</code> set is an unrelated system that casts a captured photo. Press <code>2</code> before any capture on this page.</li>
        <li><b>Naive path tracing cannot see it.</b> The projector contributes only through the direct-lighting site, and the guard at <code>pathTrace.comp:2061-2062</code> skips strategies 0, 3 and 4 &mdash; a delta light cannot be hit by BSDF sampling, and pure light tracing does no eye-side next event. The panel's health check flags exactly this.</li>
        <li><b>The spatial carriers are not applied everywhere.</b> The primary next-event site passes the real pixel coordinate and the real surface point (<code>pathTrace.comp:2229</code>), as does the light-tracer splat (<code>:3038</code>). Three secondary sites reach the projector through <code>evalProjectorTOF</code> instead (<code>:904</code>, <code>:939</code>, <code>:3511</code>): the projector seen head-on through the aperture, its reflection off an escaped ray, and light-tracer vertices. That helper calls the two-argument overload, which substitutes <code>vec2(0.5)</code> for the pixel and <code>uCamPos + uCamForward&middot;L</code> for the surface point (<code>pt_sky.glsl:313-315</code>).</li>
      </ul>
      <p class="note"><b>A finding from writing this page, sharpened on the second pass.</b> The first draft said the three secondary sites carry no rolling shutter. They carry no <em>emitter ramp</em> either, and that follows by construction rather than by inspection: the substituted position lies on the optical axis, so <code>dot(toPoint, uCamRight)</code> and <code>dot(toPoint, uCamUp)</code> are identically zero in the projector-UV block at <code>pt_sky.glsl:273-291</code>, and &phi;<sub>emit</sub> collapses to zero along with &phi;<sub>sensor</sub>. Both spatial carriers vanish at those three sites; only &phi;<sub>path</sub> and &phi;<sub>demod</sub> survive. It is small &mdash; it affects the source's appearance in a mirror, not the illumination &mdash; but it is exactly the sort of thing a reader who opens the shader will notice, so it belongs here rather than in a commit message.</p>
    </div>

<section id="flight" style="--secc:var(--l500)" data-nav="Light in flight">
  <div class="wrap">
    <div class="narrow">
      <p class="sec-label">04 &mdash; Gating on arrival time</p>
      <h2>A window on path length turns a still into a wavefront</h2>
      <p class="lead">Independently of the modulation, every contribution in the kernel knows its own accumulated path length, and a filter can keep only the contributions whose length falls inside a window. Sweeping that window forward is light in flight.</p>
      <p>This is one of the few parts of the subsystem the feature catalogue grades <em>seen</em> rather than never viewed: it is item B2 in the capture inventory, and the top-down setup view draws the wavefront position whenever the filter is active. It also works at volume scattering vertices, not only at surfaces, so in a foggy scene the window shows an expanding shell rather than a moving contour on the walls.</p>
      <p>Two qualifications, both checkable in one file.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="readout">
      <div class="row"><span class="lbl">Control</span><span class="val">Tracer tab &rsaquo; Filters &rsaquo; Path Length Filter (TOF), <code>UIManagerRender.cpp:1144-1161</code></span></div>
      <div class="row"><span class="lbl">Window</span><span class="val">[from, from + range] in metres, both log sliders</span></div>
      <div class="row"><span class="lbl">Light-side only</span><span class="val">gate on the final leg instead of the round trip</span></div>
      <div class="row"><span class="lbl">Sites that apply the window</span><span class="val">eight, in three different profiles</span></div>
      <div class="row hi"><span class="lbl">Six of them</span><span class="val">admit to relD &lt; 1.5, then 1 &minus; smoothstep(0.8, 1.2, relD)</span></div>
      <div class="row hi"><span class="lbl">One of them</span><span class="val">hard interval, no attenuation</span></div>
    </div>

    <div class="narrow">
      <p><b>The gate is not one gate.</b> Eight sites in the kernel apply the path-length window and they do not agree on its edges. Six feather it identically &mdash; volume next-event (<code>pathTrace.comp:823</code>), surface next-event (<code>:1966</code>), point-light next-event (<code>:2033</code>), the projector's own direct lighting (<code>:2264</code>), and two light-tracer connections (<code>:3173</code>, <code>:3489</code>) &mdash; admitting out to 1.5 window half-widths and attenuating smoothly. A seventh, the light-tracer projector splat, tests a hard interval first (<code>:3018</code>) and only then feathers inside it (<code>:3042</code>), so its window is effectively narrower than everyone else's. The eighth is a path that lands on an emitter by chance rather than by connection (<code>:1164</code>): hard interval test, no attenuation at all.</p>
      <p>So a scene lit by an emissive quad shows a soft-edged wavefront through its next-event contributions and a hard-edged one through its BSDF-sampled contributions, at the same window setting. For the illustrative use this filter is put to, that is harmless. For anything quantitative it is not, and it is the first thing to reconcile if the gate is ever promoted from a display filter to an estimator.</p>
      <p><b>The gate is a display filter, not an estimator.</b> Contributions outside the window are zeroed or attenuated and nothing is renormalised, so a gated frame is not a correctly-normalised transient image. It is a picture of which light paths fall in a length band. That is the right tool for showing wavefront geometry and the wrong tool for measuring transient radiance, and the distinction should be stated wherever the images are used.</p>
    </div>

<section id="fourshot" style="--secc:var(--l540)" data-nav="Four shots">
  <div class="wrap">
    <div class="narrow">
      <p class="sec-label">05 &mdash; Recovery</p>
      <h2>Four buckets, one arctangent</h2>
      <p class="lead">A real continuous-wave sensor takes four correlation measurements per depth frame. This one does the same thing across four consecutive rendered frames, accumulating each into its own layer of a four-layer floating-point image array, and then demodulates.</p>
      <p>The CPU cycles the demodulation phase through 0, &pi;/2, &pi;, 3&pi;/2 on the frame index and tells the kernel which bucket it is writing (<code>ComputePathTracer.cpp:2002-2014</code>). The kernel accumulates into that layer with the same running-mean weight as the main accumulator but at a quarter rate, so after 4N frames every bucket holds N samples (<code>pathTrace.comp:3767-3776</code>). A post-process then reads all four and applies the standard formula.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="formula">
      <div class="f">&phi;<sub>rec</sub> = atan2( I<sub>3</sub> &minus; I<sub>1</sub>, &nbsp;I<sub>0</sub> &minus; I<sub>2</sub> ) &nbsp;=&nbsp; <span class="hl">&phi;<sub>path</sub></span> &minus; &phi;<sub>emit</sub> &minus; &phi;<sub>sensor</sub></div>
      <div class="n">The subtraction cancels the DC term, which is why four shots rather than two. The emitter ramp and the rolling shutter are then added back from the values the user set, giving &phi;<sub>path</sub>, and depth follows by dividing by 2&pi;f and halving for the round trip. <code>shaders/tofRecovery.frag</code>, 271 lines</div>
    </div>

    <div class="narrow">
      <h3>What the nine display modes do and do not prove</h3>
      <p>The recovery pass offers the four raw shots tiled, recovered phase as a hue wheel, wrapped recovered depth, recovered amplitude, ground-truth depth, a wrapped-versus-truth error view, a six-panel pipeline overview, a GT-guided unwrapped depth, and an unwrapped-versus-truth error view (<code>UIManagerPanels.cpp:1041-1051</code>). Three caveats travel with them, all in the shader source.</p>
      <ul class="clean">
        <li><b>The unwrapped modes are ground-truth-guided.</b> Modes 7 and 8 compute <code>k = floor((d<sub>GT</sub> &minus; d<sub>rec</sub>)/wrap + 0.5)</code> &mdash; they pick the integer wrap count by rounding against the depth buffer (<code>tofRecovery.frag:217-238</code>). That is a legitimate diagnostic, because it isolates within-wrap accuracy from wrap ambiguity, but it is not a blind reconstruction and it must not be captioned as one. Blind multi-frequency unwrapping exists elsewhere in the stack and is described in the next section.</li>
        <li><b>The emitter phase is approximated at recovery time.</b> The recovery pass has no scene geometry, so it uses the screen coordinate as a stand-in for the projector coordinate, which the shader's own comment at <code>:82-89</code> notes is exact only for a cone or point source in the paraxial limit. Any error there shows up as a slow spatial ramp in the recovered phase.</li>
        <li><b>Convergence and demodulation are entangled.</b> Each bucket is an exponential running mean over every fourth frame, so a bucket read too early is a noisy bucket, and the arctangent of four noisy buckets is noisier still. The recovery view needs a few hundred accumulated frames before it means anything, and a camera move resets all four.</li>
      </ul>
    </div>

    <div class="narrow">
      <p class="sec-label">06 &mdash; The distinction that matters</p>
      <h2>Half of this measures the render; the other half measures the depth buffer</h2>
      <p class="lead">This is the section a reader will check first, so it is stated plainly. The modules in this subsystem split into two families that measure different things &mdash; one the render, one the depth buffer &mdash; and the page keeps them apart because the instrument does: the same recovery code can be pointed at either, which is what makes the bench a controlled experiment rather than a demo.</p>
      <p>One family reads the renderer's own transport. The projector modulation rides on the traced path length, the four buckets accumulate rendered radiance, and the recovery pass demodulates what came out. If multipath interference corrupts the phase &mdash; and in a Cornell box it should &mdash; that corruption is present, because it is a property of the light that was actually traced.</p>
      <p>The other family reads the ground-truth depth buffer, synthesises the measurements an ideal sensor would have made from it, and then runs the recovery algorithm on that. Those modules are correct implementations of published methods, and their error readouts are real, but what they measure is the numerics, not the renderer. The CW time-of-flight shader says so itself in its header: it synthesises four phase-shifted intensities at up to three frequencies from the z-buffer (<code>tofCW.frag:69-70</code>).</p>
      <p>The holography bench is the interesting case, because it can be pointed at either ladder from a combo box. Its <code>Source</code> setting chooses between synthesising the hologram from depth and taking the luminance of the rendered frame (<code>HoloDepthV2.cpp:168-190</code>), which is what makes it usable as a controlled test of the numerics <em>and</em> as a test of the whole chain.</p>

<section id="bench" style="--secc:var(--l600)" data-nav="The bench">
  <div class="wrap">
    <div class="narrow">
      <p class="sec-label">07 &mdash; The whole inverse problem, one stage at a time</p>
      <h2>Fourteen stages, and every intermediate is a texture you can look at</h2>
      <p class="lead">Off-axis holography adds a tilted reference wave to an object wave and records the intensity. The interference term separates in the Fourier domain into two sidebands displaced from DC by the carrier, so isolating one sideband and inverting recovers the complex object field &mdash; amplitude and phase &mdash; from a single real-valued image. That is four or five textbook diagrams. Here it is a combo box, and each entry is one of those diagrams rendered from the live data.</p>
      <p>There are two holography panels and they are not the same size. <b>Off-Axis Holography [FFT]</b> carries the fourteen-entry display combo tabulated below (<code>UIManagerRender.cpp:4376-4391</code>) &mdash; that is the teaching instrument. <b>Off-Axis Depth V2 (clean)</b> is the later rewrite: nine display stages of its own (<code>:4729-4740</code>), but it is the one that carries the residual readout, the setup advisor and the two presets, so it is the panel every capture instruction on this page names. Internally V2 runs the same reconstruction as fourteen lettered stages, (a) through (n) &mdash; produce the hologram, extract its real part, forward FFT over rows then columns, log-magnitude for display, crop and re-centre the sideband, log-magnitude again, inverse FFT twice, extract amplitude and wrapped phase, unwrap, then three diagnostic reductions (<code>HoloDepthV2.cpp:168-348</code>).</p>
      <p>What follows is the FFT panel's combo, entry by entry. This is the difference between a filter and a teaching instrument: a filter hands you the output, this hands you every intermediate that the output depended on, in the order the algorithm produced them.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="tw"><table>
      <thead><tr><th>#</th><th>Stage</th><th>What it shows, and what a failure looks like</th></tr></thead>
      <tbody>

      <h3>The sign convention, written down where you would fall over it</h3>
      <p>There are three holographic reconstructions in the tree. The first synthesises the hologram, demodulates by multiplying by the conjugate carrier, and low-passes with a Gaussian; no FFT at all. The second does it properly, with a real GPU FFT: a radix decomposition in <code>fft1d.comp</code>, recompiled when the transform size changes, run over rows then columns. The third, <code>HoloDepthV2</code>, is a clean-slate rewrite whose header carries the whole derivation, and it is the interesting one because of what the derivation is <em>for</em>.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="formula">
      <div class="f">H = |O + R|&sup2; = (A&sup2; + 1) + 2A&middot;cos(&phi;<sub>O</sub> &minus; &phi;<sub>R</sub>) &nbsp;&nbsp;&rarr;&nbsp;&nbsp; O&middot;R* sits at <span class="hl">&minus;K</span>, &nbsp;O*&middot;R at <span class="hl">+K</span></div>
      <div class="n">Isolate the &minus;K sideband, recentre it to DC <em>in the spectrum domain</em>, then inverse-transform directly. The result is A&middot;exp(+i&phi;<sub>O</sub>) with no post-transform demodulation needed &mdash; which is why the unwrapper is called with <code>demodulateCarrier = false</code>. Setting the tilt to zero would <em>not</em> be enough, because that pass conjugates its input unconditionally and would flip the sign of the recovered phase. <code>src/spectral/HoloDepthV2.h:9-30</code></div>
    </div>

    <div class="narrow">
      <p>That comment is the reason to show this module. It is a sign-convention trap written down at the point where someone would fall into it, with the specific wrong fix &mdash; zero the tilt &mdash; named and rejected. The same codebase records a second finding of the same kind a few files away, and this one is a numerical result rather than a convention.</p>

      <h3>Why the unwrapper stopped converging, and what fixed it</h3>
      <p>Phase unwrapping here is a weighted least-squares problem solved by preconditioned conjugate gradient across eleven compute shaders, with an FFT-based Poisson solve as the preconditioner. The natural weighting is by amplitude &mdash; trust the bright pixels &mdash; using <code>W<sub>ij</sub> = min(A<sub>i</sub>&sup2;, A<sub>j</sub>&sup2;)</code>. It stalls.</p>
      <p>The recorded cause, in an eleven-line comment on the config field itself (<code>PhaseUnwrap.h:26-36</code>): when |U| varies by roughly a hundredfold across the mask &mdash; dark halos around depth discontinuities &mdash; the condition number of the weighted operator explodes, and the unweighted Poisson preconditioner is then nothing like the operator it is meant to precondition, so it stops accelerating anything. Setting every valid neighbour pair to weight one makes the operator match the preconditioner exactly, and PCG converges in a handful of iterations. The unweighted form is exposed as a checkbox labelled <em>Unweighted Laplacian</em>, with that explanation in its tooltip (<code>UIManagerRender.cpp:4722-4727</code>).</p>
      <p>The cost is real and the source states it in the same comment: unweighted least squares trusts a dark, noisy pixel exactly as much as a bright one, so where the fringe contrast is genuinely poor the solution is smoothed rather than downweighted. This is a conditioning trade, not a free win. Note also that the config field defaults to <code>false</code> &mdash; the weighted operator is the default, and the fast path is the checkbox.</p>
    </div>

      <h3>Reading the source rather than the labels</h3>
      <p><b><code>mean(diff)</code> is a gauge, not an error.</b> It is computed by a GPU block reduction of &phi;<sub>u</sub> &minus; &phi;<sub>gt</sub> over the valid mask (<code>holo_v2/errMean.comp</code>), and the same value is then passed to the display shader as <code>uErrorMeanRef</code> and subtracted from every pixel before colouring (<code>holo_v2/display.frag:100</code>). This is correct &mdash; least-squares unwrapping recovers phase only up to an additive constant, so the constant must be removed before the residual means anything &mdash; but it means the printed number quantifies the ambiguity that was cancelled, not the accuracy that remains.</p>
      <p><b><code>RMS(phi_u)</code> is the spread of the signal, not of the error.</b> The readback loop takes the standard deviation of the unwrapped phase about <em>its own</em> mean (<code>HoloDepthV2.cpp:370-384</code>), and min/max are reported the same way, as offsets from that mean. The panel is honest about this in the line underneath, which states the expected value as <code>wavelength / sqrt(12)</code> on a uniform-depth ground truth: the standard deviation of a uniform distribution spanning one wavelength's worth of wraps. At the shipped wavelength of 2.0 that is 0.577 wraps. It is a range check, and a useful one, and it is not an accuracy figure.</p>
      <p><b>So there is no scalar accuracy number yet.</b> After the gauge is removed, the accuracy view is the error image itself &mdash; the signed residual against ground truth, in wraps, with the gauge already cancelled. The missing number is one reduction away: reduce the squared residual <em>after</em> gauge subtraction in the same shader that already computes the mean, and print an RMS error alongside the RMS spread. Until that exists, no number from this panel should be quoted as an accuracy.</p>
      <p class="note"><b>Correction recorded, and the code is not the culprit.</b> This page's own source material described the panel as reporting <code>RMS(&phi;<sub>u</sub>)</code> as though it were an error metric. The engine says otherwise in a comment three lines above the assignment &mdash; <em>"RMS/min/max = stats of the unwrapped phase over the valid mask"</em> (<code>EngineVFX2.cpp:5936-5938</code>) &mdash; and the UI label is literally correct, because it says <code>phi_u</code> and not <code>error</code>. The mistake was made downstream, by summaries that read the label and not the line. The readout is a convergence and range monitor; the scoring against ground truth is the residual image, in wraps, gauge-cancelled. The distinction matters precisely because this subsystem's claim is that it checks itself, and a self-check only counts if it is read correctly.</p>

      <h3>The Ambient slider cannot change any recovered number, and the Shot Noise slider is not shot noise</h3>
      <p>Both time-of-flight simulators build their four measurements the same way: <code>I<sub>k</sub> = clamp(clean<sub>k</sub> + ambient + noise, 0, 1.5)</code>, where <code>clean<sub>k</sub></code> spans [0,&nbsp;1] and the noise term is <code>(hash(&hellip;) &minus; 0.5)&middot;2&middot;uNoise</code>. Two consequences follow from the arithmetic, and neither is stated in the panel.</p>
      <p><b>Ambient is a constant added to all four shots, and every recovered quantity is built from differences of them</b> &mdash; <code>I<sub>3</sub>&minus;I<sub>1</sub></code> and <code>I<sub>0</sub>&minus;I<sub>2</sub></code>, for the phase and for the amplitude alike. A constant cancels identically in both. The only route by which it could survive is the clamp, and at the sliders' own maxima the sum reaches 1.4 against an upper clamp of 1.5, so the top never bites; the bottom bites only when Ambient is set below Shot Noise. So over essentially its whole range the Ambient slider changes the raw-shot display and nothing else &mdash; not the phase, not the depth, not the amplitude, not any error figure. <code>tofTracer.frag</code> goes further and deliberately holds ambient out of the illumination scaling (<code>:343</code>), which preserves the cancellation exactly.</p>
      <p><b>&ldquo;Shot Noise&rdquo; is uniform additive noise.</b> The term is drawn from a hash and is independent of the intensity it is added to; real shot noise scales with the square root of the signal, which is the whole reason a time-of-flight paper models it. It is also seeded from the pixel and the shot index alone, with no frame or time input, so it is <em>frozen</em>: it does not vary between frames and cannot be averaged away by letting the render sit. That makes it a fixed fault pattern rather than a noise process, which is a perfectly good thing to have &mdash; it is repeatable &mdash; but it is not the thing the label promises, and a robustness claim made from that slider would not carry.</p>
      <p class="note"><b>Where this leaves the shader's own sanity note.</b> <code>tofCW.frag:117-121</code> tells you that in the noiseless case the recovery is bit-for-bit identical to the input depth, because the four-phase formula is an exact inverse of the synthesis, and invites you to raise Shot Noise <em>&ldquo;to see the recovery fail realistically&rdquo;</em>. The first half is exactly right and it is the reason this ladder is worth having. The second half is the sentence this section is qualifying: the failure you get is real and it is not realistic.</p>

      <h3>Two more, smaller, and both in the same shape</h3>
      <ul class="clean">
        <li><b>Mask Erode is a slider with two states.</b> The V2 panel offers 0&ndash;3 (<code>UIManagerRender.cpp:4834</code>), and the mask shader's uniform is documented in its own header as <em>&ldquo;0 = no erosion, 1 = one-pass 3&times;3 erosion&rdquo;</em> and used as a boolean gate around a single fixed 3&times;3 pass. Settings 1, 2 and 3 produce byte-identical masks. The field behind it is separately misnamed &mdash; it is called <code>maskDilate</code> and passed as <code>uErodeRadius</code>, which shrinks the valid region rather than growing it &mdash; and the header flags that in a four-line comment while the slider says nothing.</li>
        <li><b>The frustum overlay knows a cone and a cylinder.</b> The light has four shapes; the overlay branches on one of them. Shape 1, the quad panel, draws with a start square the size of the end square; shapes 0, 2 and 3 all collapse the start square to a point and draw a cone. The label is chosen the same way, from the same single test, so the spinning slit laser and the flashlight are both drawn as cones and both labelled <em>&ldquo;Projector (cylinder)&rdquo;</em> &mdash; unless the derived half-angle falls under 2&deg;, at which point every shape is labelled <em>&ldquo;Projector (laser)&rdquo;</em> instead (<code>EngineRenderViz.cpp:2509</code>, <code>:2551-2552</code>). The line source, whose whole point is that it has no vertical extent, is the one shape the overlay can never draw correctly.</li>
        <li><b>Three fields in the unwrap config have no reader.</b> <code>tiltRemove</code>, <code>enableInpaint</code> and <code>inpaintIters</code> are declared in <code>PhaseUnwrap.h:23-25</code> and appear nowhere else in the tree &mdash; not in the solver, not in a caller, not in the UI. They are not inert controls, because no control was ever built for them; they are the shape of two features that were planned beside the ones that shipped.</li>
      </ul>

    <div class="narrow">
      <p><b>On the branch-cut row.</b> The claim it replaces was not invented; it was read off a declaration in a header without opening the definition three hundred lines away. That is a specific failure mode worth naming, because a declared method, an allocated output texture and a live <code>else</code> branch are exactly the evidence a real implementation leaves behind. The falsifying detail is in the UI: the display combo skips the two indices reserved for branch-cut residues and cuts, so the feature's own display stages were never wired either. The reachability record has been corrected in place; this page states the corrected version and the route by which the wrong one was reached.</p>

      <p>The triangulation row is the one worth dwelling on, because it is a design consequence rather than an omission. Collocating the projector with the camera is what makes the time-of-flight path clean: the outgoing and return legs share a geometry, the round-trip length is twice the camera-to-surface distance for a point source, and the shape-specific corrections for a collimated panel or a line source are one branch each in <code>evalProjectorTOF</code>. A projector with its own transform would buy triangulation and would cost that. The stack chose phase over disparity, and every capability and every gap on this page follows from that one choice.</p>

      <h3>The camera-side thread, corrected</h3>
      <p>An earlier version of this page put a fourth item in the list above: camera-side sweeping primitives, graded <em>design only</em>, on the grounds that they would need a splat-buffer film path and this renderer does not have one. That was wrong, and it was wrong in the specific way this project has a written rule against &mdash; it answered <em>is this file live</em> when the question was <em>is this feature reachable</em>.</p>
      <p>The estimator research elsewhere on this site sweeps sampling dimensions of the <em>light</em> subpath into a surface. The construction dualises to the camera subpath, and the sphere case is <b>built and reachable</b>: it is strategy 17 in the PP estimator, a live entry in the strategy combo (<code>UIManagerRender.cpp:249</code>, and again in the full PP tab at <code>:7793</code>), implemented as <code>ppCameraSphereSplatPass</code> (<code>PPIntegrator.cpp:7719-7804</code>) and dispatched as its own branch of the film loop (<code>:8935-8941</code>). It samples a photon, free-flights it to a scatter point in fog, projects that point to a pixel, and splats a single-scatter contribution weighted by the pinhole camera importance <code>We = (w&middot;h)/(4&middot;halfW&middot;halfH&middot;cos&sup3;&theta;)</code>. The splat buffer is real: sixteen per-thread images summed and blended into the running-mean film, which is how it avoids atomics.</p>
      <p>It is a first version, and its limits are legible in its own first eleven lines. It takes the PP config by reference and never reads a single field of it &mdash; not one <code>cfg.</code> in its eighty-six lines &mdash; so every setting in the panel is ignored. It requires the photon to <em>start</em> inside the fog (<code>:7741</code>), which restricts it to a light-in-fog scene. It applies fog transmittance on the camera leg but performs no occlusion test, so it casts no shadows. And the area-emitter cosine is unhandled, named as a follow-up in the header comment. It was validated at a whole-image ratio of 0.9992 against a reference &mdash; a converged number from the renderer, not from a derivation.</p>

      <h3>Shooting order</h3>
      <p>When this page carries results instead of instructions, the captures have a dependency order, and the early ones are the ones that can fail.</p>
      <ul class="clean">
        <li><b>First, the raw fringes</b> &mdash; section 03. Everything downstream consumes them. If the walls do not fringe, nothing else on this page can be shot, and the likely causes are already enumerated in the health checklist.</li>
        <li><b>Second, the four tiled buckets</b> &mdash; section 05. The cheapest test that the phase cycling actually reaches the kernel, and it fails visibly, as four identical quadrants, rather than subtly.</li>
        <li><b>Third, the advisor pair</b> &mdash; section 08. Two frames, one button press between them, no accumulation needed. It is the cheapest capture on the page and it carries one of the three claims the page is built on.</li>
        <li><b>Fourth, the spectrum frame</b> &mdash; section 07. Diagnoses the carrier before any depth is computed: sidebands overlapping DC means the carrier is too low, clipped at the edge means too high.</li>
        <li><b>Fifth, the conditioning A/B</b> &mdash; section 07. The only capture here that can contradict a written claim, which is exactly what makes it the most valuable one.</li>
        <li><b>Sixth, the Ambient A/B</b> &mdash; section 09. The other capture that can contradict a written claim, and the cheaper of the two: two frames, one slider, no accumulation, and the prediction is that the two images are identical.</li>
        <li><b>Then everything else</b>, in page order.</li>
      </ul>
