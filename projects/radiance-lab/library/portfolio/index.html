The ten-second answer

Can you compute a caustic instead of gathering one?

RadianceLab may be the most feature-rich open real-time ray-tracing framework on GitHub. I have not found another that ships bidirectional path tracing inside its real-time kernel, interactive Metropolis, VCM, photon mapping and a full-reservoir ReSTIR next to fourteen material models, 157 image operators and a node graph, in one binary. The one open repository with comparable transport breadth (yuphin/Lumen) lists no participating media and no analytic estimators; the one with comparable material and volume breadth (LuxCore) is offline.

The research question underneath all of it is the one above. A refractive caustic in fog is the light path standard sampling cannot reach: next-event estimation cannot connect through a glass sphere, because the refraction is a delta, and path sampling finds it only by chance. The usual answer is to stop connecting and gather instead, with a kernel, and accept the blur. RadianceLab asks the other question. Sample some dimensions of a light path, sweep the rest into a geometric surface, intersect the camera ray with that surface in closed form, and divide by the co-area Jacobian of the sweep. No kernel, no radius, no blur. The answer is a family of twenty-one analytic swept manifolds — fix one of the photon’s three coordinates and sweep the other two, and fix two and sweep one for the three members that close the lattice — one of which carries a photon through two refractions and lands a volumetric caustic exactly; paired with the stochastic beam it converges to 1.0000 against a traced reference. The family is not a better way to sample what is already easy, and the site says so: where a connection exists, the camera walk's next-event estimate beats every swept member. It exists for the paths that have no connection to make — the caustic through glass, the emitter too sharp to sample, the light with more dimensions than a point sample covers — and there it lands what path sampling finds only by chance and kernels can only blur.

Sweeping a photon's free dimensions into an analytic manifold is an older idea than this project — Bitterli and Jarosz's photon planes, Deng et al.'s photon surfaces — and it was proven at offline cost: minutes to over an hour per image on sixteen Xeon threads for the planes, and no wall-clock published at all for the surfaces. This project does not implement either paper's primitive. It shows what the idea does when the implementation is fast: 1.17 ms per pass, no neural network anywhere, and a caustic waist nine times narrower than the kernel estimator's at equal passes. That is the true power of the idea, and the recording on the estimator page is built to show it. Every number on this site carries the measurement that settled it, and where a measurement contradicted something written earlier, the correction is on the page rather than quietly removed.

Shape of the thing

One scene, two render paths, one measured film

A scene is built procedurally from analytic shapes. It feeds two independent renderers that share nothing but the scene and the film format: a GPU megakernel that renders in real time, with bidirectional path tracing inside it, and a CPU integrator bank — Metropolis, VCM, UPBP, the photon primitives — that runs interactively. Sharing nothing is exactly what makes them useful as checks on each other. Agreement between two independent estimators is the strongest kind of evidence here, and where a claim rests on something weaker — a numpy port of the estimator, or a single run with no partner — the page that makes it says so.

Stated, not discovered

What this is not

There is no mesh loading, by decision. Analytic primitives give exact ray intersections, and exactness is the whole point: the hourglass estimator depends on rotational symmetry about the axis from the light to the sphere's centre, and a tessellated sphere destroys it. Spheres, planes, boxes, fog and light is the entire palette, and the scenes are composed for it.

There is no acceleration structure on the GPU. Every ray tests every primitive. With scenes of a few dozen analytic shapes that is the right trade, and the tree code that exists is used by the photon mapper rather than the tracer.

There is no dedicated denoiser, no image export from the viewport, and no temporal accumulation across camera moves. No OIDN, no OptiX denoiser, no A‑SVGF: those three names occur in this repository only in the comment that says they are absent, and the two blurs in the post panel are blurs — the panel prints “Blurs, not a denoiser. There is no denoiser here.” in its own header. The one route a picture takes out of this program is the comparison report, which writes PNGs. A denoiser in particular would undermine the argument: this project is about where the noise comes from, and every comparison on this site is readable because nothing hides it.

There is no caustic on a surface in the analytic family. It deposits only at scattering vertices in the medium, so the bright pool a real glass ball throws on a floor is absent by construction. It is the largest hole in the family and the next one to fill, and it is named here rather than cropped out of the renders.

RadianceLab

A from-scratch physically-based renderer in C++ and OpenGL compute, written to make estimator research directly visible.

RendererGPU megakernel path tracer, rasterizer, and seven CPU integrators — photon mapping, VCM, MLT, PSSMLT, UPBP, path guiding

Scope note. This page covers one subsystem. The renderer also carries a structured-light projector and time-of-flight pipeline, off-axis holography with a GPU FFT and phase unwrapping, five physics solvers coupled to volumetric density, a node-graph material and projection editor with texture-space baking, and roughly 140 post-process and NPR passes. Those are documented separately.
