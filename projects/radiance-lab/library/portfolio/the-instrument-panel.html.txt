<section id="claim" style="--secc:var(--l400)" data-nav="The claim">
  <div class="wrap">
    <div class="narrow">
      <p class="sec-label">01 — The claim</p>
      <h2>The tools were built alongside the estimator, not after it</h2>
      <p class="lead">A photon-primitive estimator fails in ways a single number cannot describe. It is unbiased and still wrong at one arc of one surface; it converges and still to the wrong constant; it looks perfect and is four and a half times too bright. Debugging that requires <em>looking at the sampling geometry</em>, not at the image.</p>
      <p>So the instruments came first, or at least alongside. The primitive explorer that draws a swept manifold in world space, the correlated batch that freezes the sample set so a weight change is the only thing that moves, the split wipe that puts two estimators in one frame — those three are argued in full on <a href="where-the-fireflies-come-from.html" style="color:var(--l500)">Where the fireflies come from</a>, and this page does not repeat them.</p>
      <p>What this page covers is the rest of the panel: the parts that are not about photon primitives at all, and which a reader would have no way to guess exist. A node graph with fifty-seven node types whose output the GPU tracer evaluates per entity. A comparison-report generator that renders each configuration and writes a browsable HTML page. A ray tree you can fire backwards out of a light. An alarm that notices the renderer has been left in a diagnostic state and offers a way out.</p>
      <p><strong>Most of it has never been deliberately rendered, and that is said with some pride.</strong> It is the most interesting category on the site: the tools were built to answer a question, the question got answered, and nobody went back to photograph the tool. Instruments get used, not photographed. Where that is the case, the row says so.</p>
    </div>
    <div class="readout">
      <div class="row"><span class="lbl"><span class="st st-a">measured</span> &nbsp;a number exists, and the page says which source produced it</span><span class="val">92</span></div>
      <div class="row"><span class="lbl"><span class="st st-b">seen</span> &nbsp;looked at, unquantified</span><span class="val">251</span></div>
      <div class="row hi"><span class="lbl"><span class="st st-c">never viewed</span> &nbsp;reachable, apparently never deliberately rendered</span><span class="val">116</span></div>
      <div class="row"><span class="lbl"><span class="st st-d">inert / absent</span> &nbsp;a reachable control that reaches nothing, or a stub</span><span class="val">31 / 24</span></div>
      <div class="row"><span class="lbl">superseded &middot; scene-dependent &nbsp;works but elsewhere &middot; real but silent without particular content</span><span class="val">18 / 16</span></div>
      <div class="row hi"><span class="lbl">Total</span><span class="val">548</span></div>
    </div>
    <div class="narrow">
      <p>The first four rows are the four marks used across this site; the catalogue keeps two more, and the sibling pages fold them into <span class="st st-c">never viewed</span> and <span class="st st-d">inert / orphan</span> rather than drawing new glyphs.       <h2>Fifty-seven node types is a subsystem, not a feature</h2>
      <p class="lead">Every page on this site closes with the same scope note, and buried in it is one clause: the renderer "also carries a node-graph material and projection editor with texture-space baking". That clause has been carrying a two-and-a-half-thousand-line editor, a thousand-line preview engine and fifty-seven node types on its back. This section is that clause spelled out.</p>
      <p>It is a <strong>parameter graph, not a shader compiler</strong>. Nodes are evaluated on the CPU into per-entity material overrides and a GPU-uploaded noise configuration; nothing is codegen'd and no pipeline is rebuilt. That is the design constraint that makes everything else in this section cheap — a live preview is just the same evaluator on a small domain.</p>

      <h3>What actually reaches the render</h3>
      <p>Wiring a BSDF, Medium or Emission output node bakes the graph into <strong>three RGBA8 layers</strong> of a 2-D texture array, one slice per entity, at a resolution the user sets between 64 and 1024 px (default 256). The packing is fixed and worth stating plainly, because it is the contract between the editor and the tracer:</p>
    </div>
    <div class="formula">
      <div class="f">layer 0 &nbsp;=&nbsp; <span class="hl">albedo</span>.rgb + roughness.a<br>
      layer 1 &nbsp;=&nbsp; <span class="hl">emission</span>.rgb + metallic.a<br>
      layer 2 &nbsp;=&nbsp; ior.r + opacity.g + anisotropy.b + <span class="hl">dispersion spread</span>.a</div>
      <div class="n">Twelve channel slots, three uploads, one array — and the twelfth slot is booked twice. A wired <em>dispersive gate</em> reads that same alpha and zeroes the spread below 0.05, so the gate and the value it gates cannot both be authored.</div>
    </div>
    <div class="narrow">
      <p class="note"><b>A correction to this page, found by reading the write instead of the comment.</b> That last line said <em>anisotropy angle</em> until now, because the vector's declaration comment says <span class="mono">AnisoAngle.A</span>. Four hundred lines below it the byte that is actually stored is <span class="mono">clamp01(dispersionSpread / 10)</span>, and the megakernel decodes it by multiplying by ten again. Anisotropy angle is baked nowhere: the channel mask has ten bits — albedo, roughness, metallic, emission colour, emission strength, IOR, opacity, anisotropy, dispersion spread and the dispersive gate — while the auto-seeded BSDF node offers <em>eleven</em> inputs. Three of them, anisotropy angle, thin-film thickness and glint density, can be wired to anything you like and will never leave the editor. The angle still reaches the shader, as a per-material uniform; it just cannot be driven by a graph.</p>
      <p>Two details in that path are more interesting than the bake itself. A <strong>directly-wired 3-D procedural is deliberately excluded from the bake mask</strong> and evaluated in the shader instead, so a socket-level Multiply keeps the real material's base value rather than flattening it into a texture. And box faces, which normally deduplicate to a single primitive, are <strong>kept unmerged when baking to UV</strong>, so a photograph projected onto a box puts a different part of itself on each of the six faces.</p>
      <p>Selecting an entity auto-creates and pre-seeds a graph from its current material — eleven BSDF inputs including anisotropy angle, thin-film thickness, glint density and dispersion spread — with an automatic unique-material split so an override on one box does not leak to its siblings.</p>

      <h3>The medium half is a different machine, and the page that says otherwise is being generous</h3>
      <p>Everything above is the <em>surface</em> half. Two node types are the medium — a <span class="mono">MediumOutput</span> terminal carrying absorption, scattering, phase <em>g</em> and colour, and a bakeable <span class="mono">MediumContainer</span> — and eleven upstream types can drive a volume live. It is tempting to describe that as the same machinery pointed at fog. It is not. There are exactly three routes from a medium graph to a rendered voxel, and they behave differently enough that a user would be surprised by two of them:</p>
      <ul class="clean">
        <li><b>Constant fold.</b> Anything the resolver does not recognise as a live 3-D generator is evaluated <em>once</em>, at the entity's centre, and averaged to a scalar. So an arbitrary math chain does not become a field; it becomes one number for the whole volume, silently and at full fidelity to what you wired.</li>
        <li><b>The live handshake, and its one slot.</b> A recognised generator reaches the shader as a procedural-type integer plus a three-component multiplier, <em>per primitive</em> — and the source says the limit out loud: <span class="mono">&ldquo;The live GPU path only has one procedural payload slot per primitive.&rdquo;</span> Wire two different generators into absorption and scattering and the second wins while the first's target bits are cleared. Noise Lab 3-D is deliberately routed around this into its own block of the noise buffer, which is why it is the only route that carries a per-channel replace-or-multiply blend mask.</li>
        <li><b>The explicit bake.</b> <span class="mono">bakeMediumContainer</span> walks a resolution-cubed grid on the CPU and uploads a <em>pair</em> of floating-point 3-D textures — absorption, scattering and two channels of tint in one, the third tint channel and emission in the other — up to eight containers at a time. This is the only route that stores a real field, and it is the one route on this site graded <span class="st st-c">never viewed</span> on both halves of the catalogue.</li>
      </ul>
      <p><strong>And none of it reaches the CPU.</strong> The photon-primitive estimator contains no reference to the node graph at all; it reads a volume's scattering and absorption coefficients straight off the entity. The CPU integrators that <em>do</em> consult the graph — every one of them except the estimator, through the photon mapper's header — share a single helper whose output struct has twelve fields: albedo, roughness, metallic, IOR, opacity, anisotropy, anisotropy angle, thin-film thickness, glint density, dispersion spread, emission colour and emission strength. None for absorption, scattering or phase. There is no medium equivalent on the CPU to leave unimplemented; the structure has no slot for one. A node-authored fog is a GPU-only object, and the baked-node-graph row of the density-source table on <a href="ten-integrators-one-scene.html" style="color:var(--l460)">Ten integrators, one scene</a> now says so.</p>

      <h3>Detached editing, and why it is the right default</h3>
      <p>The editor keeps a shadow copy of the graph. With <em>Detached Editing</em> on, the scene points at the committed copy and keeps rendering it while you rewire; the <em>Commit</em> button is disabled until there is something to commit, and pressing it swaps the graphs. A renderer that resets a 4000-pass accumulation every time you drag a node is not usable for material work, and this is the mechanism that avoids it.</p>

      <h3>Previews</h3>
      <p>Per-node inline 64&sup2; thumbnails, lazily-created 256&sup2; detail views, and — for Medium outputs — a <strong>ray-marched 3-D volume preview with its own orbit camera</strong>, drawn by a dedicated preview shader. Three preview modes (GPU, CPU Fast, CPU Full) trade fidelity for frame time. Double-clicking a node detaches it into its own manipulator window with its own orbit preview, and a checkbox decides whether you may have several open at once.</p>
      <p>The create menu is <strong>type-filtered</strong>: dragging from a pin and releasing on empty canvas offers only nodes whose primary output type is compatible with that pin, and creates the node already connected.</p>

      <h3>Two instruments on the toolbar, one of which explains itself</h3>
      <p>The editor is a floating window over a viewport in which you have just selected the thing you are editing, so it has a <strong>placement solver</strong>: it measures the selected entity's screen rectangle and tries to fit itself left, right, above or below it. When it cannot, it says which of six reasons applied — the screen is really tiny, the item takes most of the screen, the item is larger than half the screen, there is not enough free space around it, the item is clipped by the viewport edge, or none of those and it covers the selection anyway. Each reason exists in two forms, <em>overlaps</em> and <em>fully covers</em>. <strong>Placement debug</strong> is where you watch it think: a scaled map of the work area with four rectangles drawn on it — the viewport, the selection's screen bounds, where the window actually is, and where the solver would put it — under the sentence it chose.</p>
      <p>A window that tells you why it is in your way is a small thing, and it is the same instinct as the alarm in &sect;06: the program would rather explain a bad state than present it.</p>

      <h3>Three things it does not do, and one of them is a bug</h3>
      <p><strong>The export is 8-bit.</strong> Capture reads the display texture back as float, clamps each channel to [0,1], quantises to a byte and writes PNG. So the "only working image export in the program" is a post-tonemap, low-dynamic-range screenshot. It is fine for a side-by-side and useless for a ratio — which is exactly why <em>not one number anywhere on this site came from it</em>. Every measured result was taken from a PGR1 float film dump instead (§04).</p>
      <p><strong>Equal time is a choice, and it is the harder one to read.</strong> Eight integrators with wildly different per-sample costs, each given the same wall clock, produces the comparison a user cares about and not the comparison a paper reports. Nothing in the panel records sample counts, so a report cannot be converted to an equal-sample comparison after the fact.</p>
      <p><strong>The JSON round-trip has a hole</strong>, and it is the whole reason the <em>Comparison JSON import</em> is graded <b>scene-dependent</b> — one of the two grades this site draws no glyph for — rather than <span class="st st-b">seen</span>: what it restores is restored correctly, and whether the restored run means anything depends on which room happens to be loaded. The exporter writes <span class="mono">scene.preset</span>. The importer never reads it. Everything else comes back — camera pose, focal length, f-stop, focus distance, film size, aspect, max bounces, exposure, light strategy, spectral mode, resolution scale, the budget, the layout, the eight selection flags and every parameter of all eight integrators — but the scene does not. Load a saved comparison while a different preset is up and you will replay the settings against the wrong room, silently.</p>
      <p class="note"><b>And one latent trap worth naming.</b> The importer restores the photon mapper's <span class="mono">v2Mode</span> flag from JSON. That flag gates the entire photon-gather pass.       <h3>Byte-identity as a contract</h3>
      <p>Five options in the estimator ship with an explicit guarantee in their own tooltip — <em>off is byte-identical</em>. Correlated batching off, surface NEE off, specular continuation off in both surface-NEE states, Lambertian emitter off, camera-leg far cap off. The claim is testable in the strongest way available: render, hash, flip the switch off, render, hash again, compare. A feature that promises to be free when disabled and is checked by md5 is a different kind of object from a feature that promises it in a comment.</p>
      <p>Beside them sits the comp-mode class counter, which is the same instinct pointed at a harder target: per-class hit and energy counters on both estimator layers, printed at a fixed pass count, that raise <span class="mono">&lt;&lt;&lt; BOTH LAYERS -- DOUBLE COUNT</span> when a bucket is non-empty on both. Its own comment explains why it has to exist — a mean averages a double count away, a median ignores it, and a seam shift cancels it out of both films. <span class="st st-a">measured</span></p>
    </div>
