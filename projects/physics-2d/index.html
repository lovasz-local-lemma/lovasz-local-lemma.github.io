01 · Purpose

Three solvers. One material laboratory.

Description · at a glance

Crucible2.5D is a material laboratory built around three simulation methods: MLS/APIC MPM, weakly compressible SPH, and Eulerian fluid flow. Its mechanics remain planar; the 2.5D name reflects selected thickness and support approximations. The point is to combine their strengths in scenes that can be edited and inspected while they run: liquid jets, tearing solids, heat-driven processing, ferrofluid, fire and blast.

I built Crucible2.5D as a 2D testbed for advanced simulation techniques I encountered in SIGGRAPH papers and demonstrations, and wanted to understand by implementing and combining them. Keeping the world planar makes local interventions, fields and failures easier to see, and leaves more of an interactive frame budget for material state and solver coupling. Particle count, grid resolution, field iterations and stiffness-dependent substeps still determine what fits that budget.

The recordings below show desktop sessions: 35 complete takes, just over eighteen minutes in all. Start with living material growing over a support, then compare food surfaces and follow heat, tearing, magnetism and blast in their own sections. Alternate takes are grouped under expandable headings; playback stays at its original speed with audio available.

The laboratory overview, finished food surfaces and representation comparison introduce the whole project. MPM, SPH and Eulerian flow then have their own sections. The coupling section traces what the solvers exchange, where each quantity is stored, and when it is applied. Later material extensions build on that foundation.

The native application combines C++ orchestration, OpenGL compute passes and an interactive material editor. A scene can contain deformable or flowing particles, surrounding air, analytic terrain, temperature, damage and external fields. Not every combination is a validated multiphysics model. Some are deliberate visual experiments, and others have specific numerical support that can be traced through the pipeline viewer and source.

The project grew through hands-on implementation: write a material law, build a revealing scene, inspect a failure, then retain an alternative when it answers a different question. Some changes repair a specific exchange or scale error; others trade physical scope for a more expressive interactive model. Keeping those branches visible makes the engineering history part of the experiment.

Watch the material. Then inspect the mechanism.

Videos record the native application. The browser studies isolate particular equations and assumptions; they are labeled computed explanations. These captures show behavior and workflow, not calibrated material measurements. Some scenes change or receive additional brush input during a take.

ExplanationPlanar mechanics and approximate depth supportA smaller computational domain makes experimentation practical, but it also changes topology and mechanics.＋

A pore that crosses a narrow ligament in a planar body can cut its only load path. In a three-dimensional porous solid, material may still connect around that opening through depth. Increasing a 2D stiffness parameter cannot recreate a missing connection, so a crumb-like sample may separate or slump differently even when its appearance resembles a 3D porous material.

Magnetic peaks have a similar dimensional boundary. A pointed profile in this plane does not establish the curvature, spacing or connectivity of a three-dimensional spike array. The visible height is simulated in-plane; the shaded depth is presentation. The magnetic model also includes explicit surface traction and corrugation seeding.

Thickness, occupancy and support controls explore selected missing effects with affordable approximations. They remain useful comparative models inside the testbed, with their assumptions exposed; a full volumetric solid or ferrofluid would require a different spatial representation and a larger computational budget.

The thermal chapter makes this visible in four native recordings: excessive breakup, larger cracked pieces, cohesive heated bodies with 2.5D support, and variants of that extra support.

03 · Architecture / three simulation methods

Water and jelly need more than bouncing dots

Description · at a glance

A handful of beads and a drop of water can both be drawn as particles, but they answer different mechanical questions. Independent particles carry position and velocity; contacts keep them from overlapping. Water also needs a response to local compression. Jelly needs that response plus a memory of how the material was stretched or sheared.

In SPH, a particle is a sample of a continuous material. Neighbor kernels estimate density, compression creates pressure, and viscosity responds to relative motion. The native pressure law is weakly compressible: it penalizes excess density rather than enforcing exact incompressibility. In MPM, material points retain deformation and history while a temporary grid mediates forces. That stored deformation lets an elastic body push back toward its rest shape.

So particles are not the limitation: SPH and MPM both use them. Molecular dynamics, spring networks and particle constraints can also produce fluid or solid behavior with suitable interactions. The missing ingredients in a simple ballistic/contact demo are the constitutive law, collective spatial response and—in a solid—persistent strain. MPM can represent a fluid too; its material branch deliberately removes elastic shear memory.

Theory / mathematicsDensity answers compression; deformation answers shapeThe SPH pressure law and MPM elastic update show what each particle needs to store.＋

The SPH branch estimates density with its normalized two-dimensional kernel and uses a clamped linear pressure law. Negative pressure is suppressed; free-boundary support and thin structures receive additional treatments. Viscosity and surface tension are separate mechanisms, not consequences of drawing overlapping circles.

An MPM material point carries a deformation gradient F. The affine velocity field updates it, and the constitutive branch converts deformation into stress. Rotation by itself is not strain: the corotated model removes the polar rotation R before penalizing distortion. Plasticity, temperature and damage can then change the rest state or stress response.

04 · 01 / Material point method

MPM: deformable matter keeps its history

Description · at a glance

An MPM step transfers particle mass, momentum and stress information onto the grid, updates grid motion under the selected forces and constraints, then transfers the result back. The implementation uses MLS/APIC-style information and a smooth local stencil to preserve more structure than a position-only particle update.

The same transfer framework serves several constitutive families. Corotated elastic solids recover shape; snow-style plasticity changes the stored rest state; fluids retain volumetric response without shear memory; fiber stress reinforces a preferred direction. Thermal response, damage and magnetic stress extend those material branches. This is why melting, tearing and field-driven liquids belong to the same MPM story.

The constitutive branch determines what deformation means. Elastic response, plastic flow, thermal softening and damage require different state and update rules. A material preset therefore represents a collection of model choices and coefficients, not merely a color and viscosity slider. The grid visualization and transfer schematic make those choices easier to connect to the resulting motion.

This shared transfer is a versatile starting point, not a complete recipe for every material. Tearing needs a fracture or damage law; baking-like behavior needs heat transfer, processing history and changing constitutive response. The MPM extensions later in this brochure add those mechanisms, with their assumptions and current limits stated alongside the results.

Theory / mathematicsBoth transfers must use the same nine-node stencilQuadratic B-spline weights give a fixed support and a constant affine moment matrix.＋

For one coordinate, the stencil starts at floor(x/h − 1/2), so the local coordinate f lies in [1/2, 3/2). The three weights below are combined by an outer product in two dimensions. Their sum is one; with complete support this is the mass-partition property used by the scatter.

The same origin, weights and distances must be used by both directions. A mismatch can produce apparently plausible motion while corrupting a derived field. The solver internals document an earlier scatter/gather mismatch in the damage Laplacian as a concrete example.

For this complete quadratic stencil the second moment is h²I/4. Its inverse supplies the factor 4/h² in both stress transfer and reconstruction of the affine velocity matrix. That coefficient belongs to the basis; changing it independently changes the method.

Theory / mathematicsOne transfer pipeline, several constitutive responsesThe material branch produces stress; the shared transfer transports its effect.＋

The particle deformation gradient is advanced using its affine velocity field. Elastic branches derive stress from that deformation; plastic branches modify singular values or stored rest state; thermal and damage branches modify the response using additional histories. These choices change the mechanics without replacing the transfer machinery.

In the notation below, τ denotes the Kirchhoff-like stress passed to the MLS transfer and Vₚ the reference particle area used by the implementation. The stress impulse and APIC affine momentum share the same node displacement, so the code combines them before scattering. Grid velocities then rebuild the particle velocity and affine matrix.

The implementation accumulates scaled integers on the grid, with finite precision and range. Velocity clamps and bounded substeps also influence the visible result. Partition of unity alone does not prove exact conservation for the complete sandbox with these interventions.

Resolution and stepping remain important. Stiffness-dependent substep choices are authored and capped; they do not establish a universal stability guarantee. Increasing stiffness or changing the grid can move a scene outside a previously useful operating range even when the same preset name is retained.

05 · Connection / choose a representation for the question

Why the same mechanics becomes FEM in Modal2D

Description · at a glance

Crucible2.5D asks how material moves through large deformation, contact, flow and changing topology. Modal2D asks which small vibrations a particular object supports, and what a strike will sound like. Those goals favor different numerical work, even though both start from mass, internal forces and boundary conditions.

Modal2D assembles a mass matrix and stiffness matrix from a mesh whose geometry, thickness, material and supports matter to the instrument. Its eigenmodes turn that spatial problem into a compact set of oscillators. A small frequency error can mean an audibly mistuned note, so the shape model, discretization and convergence matter—not simply whether the motion looks convincing.

FEM is possible for large-deformation physics too; it needs suitable nonlinear, contact and topology handling. MPM itself uses a grid basis and a weak-form discretization with moving material samples. This is a choice of representation and workload, not a rule that one project needs accurate physics and the other does not.

The base solver is only the beginning.

MPM provides the particle–grid machinery; extra material laws decide whether an object tears, softens, cures or responds to a field. Crucible adapts research ideas into a planar interactive laboratory, simplifying selected formulations and omitting some large-scale optimizations. The following extension chapters show what was retained, what was changed and which behaviors remain approximate.

Theory / mathematicsA fast spring preview is a different approximationThe native editor uses a small spring-lattice eigensolve during shape dragging; full FEM follows on release.＋

The current quick preview uses a 10×6 lumped-mass lattice with axial and diagonal springs and computes six modes. It provides immediate qualitative feedback while dimensions change. It does not preserve arbitrary mesh holes, detailed boundary geometry or the same stiffness formulation as the full FEM solve. The source warns that its absolute frequencies can differ substantially; that warning is not a new measured error bound.

A separate cavity shortcut is a user-tuned two-pole bandpass resonance. It is not a geometry-derived spatial pressure-mode solve. Conversely, fixed-mode Rayleigh-quotient sensitivities accelerate shape adjustment using FEM matrices; those are FEM-derived approximations, not another spring solver.

06 · 02 / Smoothed particle hydrodynamics

SPH: pressure and interfaces from particle neighborhoods

Description · at a glance

The SPH solver uses weakly compressible pressure, viscosity, local surface-tension terms and additional velocity smoothing. Neighbor structure determines which particles exchange these quantities. Signed-distance collision handling and continuous collision checks help keep fast particles from simply passing through boundaries.

A hashed cell structure finds nearby particles. Density is accumulated with a normalized 2D cubic spline of support 2h; the force stage combines pressure gradients, viscosity and interface terms. XSPH then smooths relative particle velocities. The Codim Threads branch detects a local tangent and thinness, adapting the density treatment when a fluid bridge becomes line-like.

The sand-castle and jet-style scenes are useful because their expected interaction is legible: the liquid should transfer momentum to the deformable material rather than merely overlap it. The current SPH-to-MPM path records a reaction impulse, scatters it to a persistent grid and lets the MPM side consume it in the following update. This is a concrete return path, improving on a one-way visual interaction.

The sand-castle jet setup isolates a readable interaction between a liquid-like SPH stream and an MPM body. It is more informative than enabling every available subsystem at once.

ExplanationBulk liquid and thin filaments use different local informationDensity estimation, smoothing and codimensional adaptation explain the visible motion.＋

WCSPH estimates density from neighboring masses and turns density deviation into pressure. Viscosity and XSPH velocity smoothing damp different kinds of local motion; surface-tension terms encourage coherent boundaries. Changing the smoothing scale changes both the neighborhood and which features the solver can resolve.

The Codim Threads route estimates local tangent and thinness information and adapts the density kernel toward a line-like treatment. This helps thin bridges retain a different character from a bulk 2D blob. It is an explicit adaptive approximation, rather than a general dimensional-invariance result.

In the jet experiment, the contact reaction is stored as an impulse. Dividing it by grid mass supplies a velocity change; multiplying by the timestep again would incorrectly make this interaction depend on the number of updates. Clearing even nodes with no current MPM mass prevents old reactions accumulating until new material arrives.

That mechanism does not justify a blanket claim of exact coupled momentum conservation. Sampling, spatial transfer, boundary constraints and the staggered update can still introduce error. The meaningful diagnostic is to inspect the jet, reaction transfer and material response together, then compare parameter changes under the same setup.

07 · 03 / Grid-based fluid

Eulerian flow: transport, projection and reactive air

Description · at a glance

The air route uses a staggered velocity grid with pressure projection, advection and buoyancy-related forcing. Smoke, temperature, fuel and oxygen fields add visible and reactive quantities to that motion. The pressure solve uses an iterative grid method, so its iteration budget and boundaries are part of the experiment.

A MAC grid separates cell-centered scalars from velocities on faces. Advection transports the fields; force passes add buoyancy and selected injections; a pressure-potential solve repairs the velocity divergence; boundary passes enforce no-through-flow at solid faces. Temperature diffusion and the oxygen/fuel stages add further field updates. This is a full solver family, with smoke serving as a visible tracer of its motion.

Combustion-inspired behavior includes oxygen limitation, fuel accumulation, smoldering and a backdraft scenario. The chamber demonstration makes the causal sequence readable: a restricted environment can retain fuel while oxygen is scarce; changing access to oxygen changes the reaction. These are authored reduced rules rather than detailed chemical kinetics or a validated fire-safety model.

Theory / mathematicsPressure projection removes unwanted divergenceFace velocities and cell-centered pressure turn a flow constraint into a grid solve.＋

The MAC grid stores horizontal and vertical velocity on cell faces. Advection and forces produce a provisional velocity. The pressure solve then finds a correction whose gradient removes divergence, with solid faces constrained by the SDF boundary.

The implementation stores a pressure potential whose scale already incorporates the velocity-correction convention. Its projection shader subtracts pressure differences divided by grid spacing, without another timestep multiplier. Additional blast or expansion sources alter the target divergence instead of behaving like ordinary incompressible advection.

The iterative pressure budget determines how well this discrete constraint is met. Pressure and divergence views diagnose the correction and residual; smoke brightness alone cannot establish that the projection has converged.

Airtightness turns heating into a pressure event

Description · at a glance

An open flame can shed hot gas into its surroundings. The same source inside a sealed pocket has to interact with the enclosure: energy and gas accumulate, the walls carry a load, and a breach changes the possible escape paths. The interesting behavior is the transition from storing an effect to releasing it through a newly available opening.

The native sandbox builds coarse occupancy and outside-reachability fields. A cell is enclosed when the outside cannot reach it through the current mask. Trapped pressure, heat and fuel-related state evolve alongside the material; cracks and moving matter can change that connectivity. Airtightness and pressure are different questions. Connectivity identifies a possible escape route; conductance, stored state and the release model determine the resulting motion. A pressure projection that tries to remove velocity divergence cannot, by itself, store the thermodynamics of a sealed gas volume. The ferrofluid branch is built into MPM rather than applied only as a visual force on dots. The magnetic work combines external fields, material response and iterative field calculations. Current scope

  • A real-time experimental sandbox with authored constitutive responses, stabilization and optional artistic forces; it is not a calibrated engineering or biological simulator.
  • Dimensionality changes the mechanics: pores that cross a narrow 2D ligament can sever its load path, while a 3D porous solid may retain connections around the opening through depth. Thickness and support heuristics approximate selected missing behavior; they do not recover full 3D topology.
  • Damage regularization is currently parameterized relative to grid spacing, and elastic substeps are capped. Resolution independence and unrestricted numerical stability are not established.
  • SPH–MPM reaction exchange is staged across steps. The entire coupled world is not claimed to conserve energy or momentum exactly.
  • Magnetic spikes and explosion fronts include explicit approximations. Rendered depth, glow and surface detail do not imply a full three-dimensional simulation.
  • The recordings demonstrate selected material responses and interactions. Broader numerical convergence and calibration against real materials remain open work.
