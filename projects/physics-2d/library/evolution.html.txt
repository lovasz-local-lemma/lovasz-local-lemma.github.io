      <h2>How to read this page</h2>
    </div>
    <div class="grid-2">
      <div class="panel">
        <h3>Why old versions are still here</h3>
        <p>The project convention is that an improvement ships as a <strong>selectable</strong>
        version and the old one is never deleted. That is not sentiment. Every one of these rewrites
        was justified by an A/B that a person had to be able to run, and several of the old paths
        still own mechanisms the new path never ported. A toggle that is only ever left at its
        default is a toggle that was never worth the cost; each section below therefore ends with a
        concrete reason to move it.</p>
        <p>Almost every entry in the changelog carries <code>"verified": false</code>. That flag means
        the numeric bounds were machine-checked and the build ran clean, but the question of whether
        the thing <em>reads right</em> was deferred to a human looking at it. Treat the numbers as
        measured and the aesthetics as claimed.</p>
      </div>
      <div class="panel">
        <h3>What the badges mean here</h3>
        <div class="legend">
          <span><span class="b green">default</span>What you get out of the box</span>
          <span><span class="b yellow">selectable</span>Still in the UI, still useful</span>
          <span><span class="b orange">superseded</span>Selectable, but strictly worse</span>
          <span><span class="b gray">gone</span>Removed, or archived only</span>
        </div>
        <p class="footer-note">A generation marked <span class="b orange">superseded</span> is one
        where the only remaining reason to select it is diagnosis or demonstration. A generation
        marked <span class="b yellow">selectable</span> owns behaviour the newer one does not have.
        The distinction matters: several of the nine families below have no strictly dominant
        generation at all.</p>
      </div>
    </div>
    <div class="note">
      <p><strong>A pattern worth naming up front.</strong> Most of these rewrites replaced a
      <em>compensator</em> with a <em>cause fix</em>. The legacy magnetic force multiplied a wrong
      field by five gates; the legacy ash regrowth had two shaders writing one buffer and tuned the
      thresholds instead; the legacy contact heat could not cool, so a second one-way channel was
      bolted on to cool. In every case the tuning log embedded in the shader comments is the tell:
      when a comment reads like a history of threshold changes, the layer underneath it is wrong.</p>
    </div>
  </section>

      <p class="eyebrow">Magnetics &#183; three backends</p>
      <h2>Ferrofluid: from a truncated Poisson solve to a closed-form field</h2>
      <p class="lede">A ferrofluid needs a field <em>H</em> everywhere a particle might be, so it can
      feel the Kelvin body force <em>F</em> = &#189;&#967;&#8711;|H|&#178;. Getting that field right in
      2D is the whole problem: solve it on a grid and you pay for accuracy in iterations; evaluate it
      analytically and you give up the contribution of the magnetized matter itself. Both mistakes
      were made here, in that order, and each produced a distinct, nameable artifact.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="chip-row">
      <span class="chip">Legacy (Poisson + gates)</span>
      <span class="chip">New Magnetic</span>
      <span class="chip">Fantasy (arcane)</span>
    </div>

    <h3>The generations</h3>
    <table>
      <tr><th>Generation</th><th>What it did</th><th>The symptom you saw</th><th>Root cause</th></tr>

      <tr>
        <td class="name">Pre-April 2026<br><span class="b gray">gone</span></td>
        <td>Per-particle magnetization from <code>langevin_response()</code>, which dodged the
        <code>coth(0)</code> singularity by flooring the argument:
        <code>x = max(alpha * hmag, 1e-4)</code>.</td>
        <td class="notes">Ferrofluid clumped into lumps and then dots <strong>with no external field at
        all</strong> &#8212; cursor off, no scene magnets, ambient H = 0. A puddle that should have been
        a plain viscous fluid gathered itself.</td>
        <td class="notes">The floor is applied <em>per particle</em>, but the rasterizer <em>sums</em> M
        into shared cells. At the floor, L(x) = x/3 = 3.33e&#8722;5, so N co-located particles create
        roughly N&#183;sat/30000 of cell magnetization out of nothing. That non-zero source closed a
        feedback loop with no entry condition. Only the high-x branch ever needed divisor
        protection.</td>
      </tr>

      <tr>
        <td class="name">Legacy backend<br><span class="b green">still default</span><br><span class="b yellow">selectable</span></td>
        <td>Cold-start Jacobi Poisson on 1024&#178;, run <strong>seven times per frame</strong>
        (scene, drive, three induction passes, remanent particles, total). On top of the solved field,
        <code>magnetic_body_force()</code> multiplies the Kelvin force by five stacked heuristic gates
        before a per-material acceleration cap.</td>
        <td class="notes">The project's number one complaint, verbatim: <em>"moving the magnet doesn't
        pull the ferrofluid; it affects a ring, then a new ring; most material doesn't follow."</em>
        A held, stationary magnet also did not hold &#8212; the gathered blob slumped and fell.</td>
        <td class="notes"><strong>Two independent causes, and the ring is where they meet.</strong>
        (A) <em>Truncation:</em> &#966; is cleared to zero before every solve and Jacobi moves
        information one cell per iteration; at the runtime setting of 52 iterations (the header's 144
        is overridden), |H| exists only about 10 to 20 cells out and is <em>exactly</em> zero beyond,
        recovering under 0.4&#37; of the 1/r&#178; tail. Converging that grid needs roughly 30k to 490k
        iterations. And because &#966; restarts cold, moving the cursor drops the old field entirely.
        (B) <em>The gate stack:</em> <code>ext_drive</code> closes far from the pole,
        <code>hf_suppress</code> divides by field sharpness so it closes <em>at</em> the pole,
        <code>wall_gate</code> multiplies by 0.22 within about 2 cells of an SDF surface (which is where
        a ferro pond rests), <code>density_damp</code> cuts the inward force to 8&#37; inside a blob,
        and <code>sink_cancel</code> subtracts up to 92&#37; of the rest. Their pass-bands overlap only
        on a thin annulus. (C) <em>Holding</em> was pure velocity entrainment toward the cursor
        velocity, with a gate that floors at 0.35 when the cursor is still &#8212; so a stationary held
        magnet drives particle velocity toward zero. It <em>brakes</em>.</td>
      </tr>

      <tr>
        <td class="name">Ferro D1<br><span class="b yellow">selectable</span></td>
        <td>Five opt-in patches rather than a rebuild: an M Raw debug view, a runtime
        256/512/1024/2048 grid selector, capillary halved 0.34 to 0.18 with spike strength raised
        1.0 to 1.6, surface-only corrugation, and an explicit <code>apply_distance_falloff</code>
        offered as a cleaner replacement for the ad-hoc gates.</td>
        <td class="notes">Spikes still did not appear; the ring survived every setting.</td>
        <td class="notes">D1 tuned the constitutive and surface layer, and added a <em>sixth</em>
        composable gate, while the field underneath was still truncated and cold-started. Raising
        spike strength cannot manufacture a spike lattice when the surface has no symmetry-breaking
        perturbation to amplify. Its own changelog entry pre-registered the failure: <em>"D1 is the
        experiment, D2 is the principled rebuild&#8230; if no spike-like behavior appears,
        escalate."</em></td>
      </tr>

      <tr>
        <td class="name">New Magnetic v1<br><span class="b gray">folded in</span></td>
        <td>Deleted the solver from the drive path. Cursor presets and SDF magnets are decomposed on
        the CPU into at most 32 signed 2D pole kernels and evaluated in closed form per particle:
        H(p) = ambient + &#8721; q<sub>i</sub>(p&#8722;x<sub>i</sub>)/(|p&#8722;x<sub>i</sub>|&#178;+c<sub>i</sub>&#178;).
        One plain Kelvin force, one per-material accel cap, no spatial gates. Holding became a real
        positional spring-damper.</td>
        <td class="notes">Not a failure, a deliberately narrow v1 &#8212; but the field looked
        <em>too clean</em>: two magnetized bodies did not distort each other's pull, no dipole chains
        formed, and a magnet over a pool produced a smooth dome instead of a Rosensweig spike row. The
        field overlay did not work at all, because it sampled a texture this path never produces.</td>
        <td class="notes">The analytic pole list contains only <em>external</em> sources. Magnetized
        matter contributed nothing to the field, so a neighbour's M was simply not in the force
        gradient.</td>
      </tr>

      <tr>
        <td class="name">New Magnetic (current)<br><span class="b yellow">selectable</span></td>
        <td>Three additions closed the v1 gaps. (1) Per-particle M state in its own SSBO, with
        per-material relaxation and thermal decay &#8212; hard magnets keep M, Curie ferro fades past
        about 680&#160;K, superconductors expel field below about 180&#160;K. (2) <em>NM Self-Field</em>:
        particle M is scattered onto a 256&#178; grid tracking the MPM grid and solved with a
        <strong>warm-started</strong> Jacobi (32 iterations, &#966; deliberately never cleared).
        (3) Rosensweig spikes on that field, with a corrugation seed.</td>
        <td class="notes">Current. Its live defects are listed below, not fixed by a later
        generation.</td>
        <td class="notes">The structural decision that makes the self-field safe is an
        <strong>asymmetry</strong>: <code>H_here = H_at(pos)</code> <em>excludes</em> the demag texture
        and is the only thing that magnetizes M; <code>h2_at()</code> <em>includes</em> it and is the
        only thing that feels it. The self-field enters the force and never the magnetization.</td>
      </tr>

      <tr>
        <td class="name">Fantasy v1<br><span class="b yellow">selectable</span></td>
        <td>A byte-for-byte clone of New with its physical safety switches deliberately flipped to
        wild. M is magnetized off the <em>total</em> field so activation propagates edge to edge; a
        write gate latches M under a weak field, giving remanence; an orbital vortex makes matter
        spiral rather than clamp; chain boost 2.2&#215;, self-field 1.6, spike strength 3.0 against
        New's 1.0 / 1.6 / 9.0.</td>
        <td class="notes">A permanent-magnet body that had been magnetized and then left alone
        <strong>strobed</strong> &#8212; its colour and its overlay membrane oscillated frame to frame.
        Legacy and New never flickered.</td>
        <td class="notes">Follows directly from the design choice. For a latched body with the cursor
        away the external field is near zero, so H<sub>total</sub> is dominated by the body's
        <em>own</em> demagnetizing field, which by definition points opposite its M. The target
        <code>langevin(|H_total|) * dir(-M)</code> tried to reverse M every frame; the demag field
        reversed with it. The bug and the feature share one mechanism, which is why the fix had to be
        a coercivity model rather than a retreat to the external field.</td>
      </tr>

      <tr>
        <td class="name">Fantasy + Anti-Flicker<br><span class="b yellow">selectable</span></td>
        <td>A coercivity rule that forbids the <em>self</em>-field from reversing a latched M while
        leaving a strong <em>external</em> field free to re-magnetize. The target is decomposed along
        and perpendicular to the previous M; the reversing component is clamped out
        (<code>max(along, 0)</code>), the perpendicular swing is damped to 0.6, and the raw target is
        blended back by <code>smoothstep(0.15, 0.60, |H_external|)</code>.</td>
        <td class="notes">Current for Fantasy. Latched bodies settle and hold; propagation and
        activation strength still come from the total field, so the wild behaviour survives.</td>
        <td class="notes">A heuristic ratchet, not hysteresis &#8212; see the caveats below.</td>
      </tr>
    </table>

    <div class="grid-2">
      <div class="panel">
        <h3>Why the current backend is good enough</h3>
        <p>It removed a cause instead of adding a compensator. The field is now a closed-form sum of at
        most 32 softened 2D pole kernels evaluated per particle: exact at every distance, zero solver
        cost, zero lag, and structurally incapable of truncating. Once the field was exact, all five
        gates became unnecessary <em>at once</em>, because every one of them had been tuned against the
        truncated field. The ring had an outer edge (Jacobi never reached there) and an inner edge
        (<code>hf_suppress</code> and <code>sink_cancel</code> closing at the pole and inside blobs);
        both edges disappear when the field is right. What survives as force shaping is one
        per-material acceleration cap, so the falloff you see is physics rather than tuning.</p>
        <p>The collapse those gates were also fighting was independently re-diagnosed as a
        <strong>Langevin feedback runaway</strong> rather than mechanical compression &#8212; M read
        off the full field grows M &#8212; and that loop is now broken structurally rather than damped.
        The dead numerical seed is gone at the source. Holding became a spring-damper, so a stationary
        held magnet holds instead of braking.</p>
        <p>It is also two orders of magnitude cheaper: seven cold-start 1024&#178; solves at 52 to 96
        iterations per frame (roughly 380 to 700 million Jacobi cell updates) became one
        warm-started 256&#178; solve at 32 iterations (about 2.1 million), and that solve accumulates
        convergence across frames instead of throwing it away.</p>
      </div>
      <div class="panel">
        <h3>What is still wrong</h3>
        <ul class="bullet-list">
          <li><strong>The biggest one is a regression the other docs do not mention.</strong> Selecting
          New or Fantasy sets <code>suppressed_by_new_backend</code>, which makes
          <code>physics_active()</code> return false, which sets <code>u_use_real_magnetics = 0</code>
          in both p2g and g2p. That silently switches off five of the seven anti-compaction mitigations
          the status page still advertises: the demag pressure, the occupancy anti-collapse pressure,
          <code>hf_suppress</code>, the positional cursor gate and the per-cell M cap
          (<code>CELL_M_CAP = 28</code>), plus the legacy surface force and chain force. Under New and
          Fantasy, compaction is held by the unconditional Tait <code>j_floor</code> alone (0.85 ferro,
          0.90 heavy, i.e. 10 to 15&#37; maximum compression) and one accel cap. <em>Ferro compacts more
          than physical under a strong source</em> is therefore <strong>not</strong> fixed by the
          current backend; the stack that used to fight it is off and a blunt clamp replaced it. The
          project's own remedy is a slider.</li>
          <li><strong>A world-up bias.</strong> With poles off and ambient zero but self-field on,
          <code>field_dir</code> falls back to a hardcoded (0,1) and
          <code>chi_eff = dot(m, field_dir)/M_REF</code> collapses to <code>m.y/M_REF</code>. A remanent
          body whose M points sideways gets near-zero force; one pointing downward is
          <em>repelled</em> from its own gradient.</li>
          <li><strong>The demag solve lags.</strong> Warm-started but never converged (32 iterations,
          256&#178; over the whole MPM grid, far coarser than the legacy 1024&#178;). Chain spacing and
          spike sharpness are resolution-limited, and fast material outruns its own field. Truncation
          was traded for lag, not eliminated.</li>
          <li><strong>Silent pole overflow.</strong> <code>kMaxPoles = 32</code> with an early return;
          permanent magnets cost two poles each, so past about 16 magnetic SDF objects the extras
          contribute literally nothing, with no warning.</li>
          <li><strong>Soft iron does not self-consist.</strong> It is a single-pass induced estimate
          evaluated in pole-insertion order, so two soft-iron pieces near each other never mutually
          magnetize. The legacy ran three induction passes for exactly that.</li>
          <li><strong>Fantasy's anti-flicker is a ratchet, not hysteresis.</strong> There is no
          per-material coercive field and no switching model, so the 0.15 / 0.60 thresholds are global
          constants applied identically to a hard magnet (saturation 22) and Para Mist (1.0). A latched
          M can rotate but effectively cannot shrink along its own axis under the self-field alone, so
          a body can hold a physically unjustified magnetization for a long time. Fantasy also runs
          self-field 1.6 and chain boost 2.2, which makes it the generation most likely to
          over-compact.</li>
          <li><strong>The default is still Legacy</strong> (<code>g_magnetic_backend = 0</code>). Out of
          the box you get the ring, despite the consolidation proposal recommending New as canonical.
          Only the combo, or a scene preset carrying <code>"magnetic_backend": 1</code>, switches it.</li>
          <li><strong>Doc drift, code wins.</strong> The backend combo tooltip still claims New
          <em>does not yet do per-particle remanence, Rosensweig spikes, or the field overlay</em>
          &#8212; all three shipped. The status page's Heavy Ferrofluid row still lists saturation 14
          and alpha 3.0 while both shaders use 8.0 and 2.0 (its own caveat list, four lines above, has
          the correct numbers).</li>
          <li><strong>A deliberate 2D compromise.</strong> The pole kernel falls off as 1/r for a
          monopole and 1/r&#178; for a dipole pair, not the 3D 1/r&#179;. Consistent for a 2D world, but
          <em>no ring</em> is partly bought with <em>the magnet reaches the whole world</em>.</li>
          <li><strong>Vestigial, harmless.</strong> <code>magnetic_rasterize.comp</code> still carries
          the old <code>max(alpha*hmag, 1e-4)</code> form. It cannot reproduce the clumping &#8212; its
          only call site is guarded by <code>hmag &gt; 1e-5</code> and applies to SDF shell pixels, not
          thousands of particles &#8212; but it should go.</li>
        </ul>
      </div>
    </div>

    <div class="note">
      <p><strong>When you would still pick an older version.</strong> Four real reasons, not nostalgia.
      (1) Legacy is the only way to <em>see</em> the ring and the drop-on-move, which is the
      demonstration the whole rebuild exists to justify. (2) Six mechanisms are legacy-only and go
      completely dead under New and Fantasy because they are gated on
      <code>u_use_real_magnetics</code>: demag pressure, occupancy anti-collapse pressure, the per-cell
      M cap, the interface Maxwell shaping in <code>ferro_surface_force</code>, the legacy dipole chain
      force, and <code>hf_suppress</code>. If a scene depends on demag-pressure anti-packing, New has no
      equivalent. (3) The Mechanic Toggles panel exposes each legacy gate individually, which is how you
      attribute a symptom to a specific gate; New has no analogue. (4) Legacy is the only backend that
      produces a real solved field <em>texture</em>, so every debug view that samples one &#8212; M Raw,
      the source texture, the magnetization texture &#8212; works only there.</p>
      <p>Fantasy versus New is not old versus new at all. Pick Fantasy when you want the game feel
      &#8212; activation that propagates and latches, orbiting matter, bold chains, arcane colour
      &#8212; and New when you want the physically defensible answer. Fantasy is a deliberate sibling,
      not a successor.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="callout">
      <p><strong>The A/B to run.</strong> Load <em>Ferro Spike Bench</em> (it already sets
      <code>magnetic_backend: 1</code>, self-field on, spikes on), let a pool settle, then flip
      Magnetics &#8594; Magnetic Backend between the three entries with the same pool and the same
      cursor motion.</p>
      <ul>
        <li><strong>The ring.</strong> On Legacy, hold a magnet above the pool: only a thin annulus
        moves, the fluid directly under the pole and everything past about 10 to 20 cells sits still.
        Sweep sideways and the gathered blob is <em>dropped in place</em> while a new ring forms at the
        new position. On New the whole pool leans toward the cursor and follows continuously.</li>
        <li><strong>The hold.</strong> Gather a blob and stop moving. Legacy: it slumps and falls. New:
        the spring-damper holds it at the cursor.</li>
        <li><strong>The self-field.</strong> On New, toggle NM Self-Field off &#8212; the spike row
        collapses into an undifferentiated mound and two magnetized bodies stop distorting each other's
        pull. NM Spike Spacing changes the lattice pitch.</li>
        <li><strong>The 1e-4 bug.</strong> Select Legacy, set cursor strength 0, no scene magnets,
        Ambient H = 0, and watch a puddle. Today it stays a viscous puddle. The pre-April build that
        lumped under zero field is preserved in the archived snapshots for comparison.</li>
        <li><strong>Fantasy.</strong> Sweep the source over a permanent-magnet body: the glow propagates
        edge to edge and <em>holds</em> after the source leaves; hold it over ferro and matter orbits in
        a vortex instead of clamping on. Then turn FM Anti-Flicker off, magnetize a body and move the
        cursor away: colour and field membrane strobe frame to frame. Turn it back on and it
        settles.</li>
        <li><strong>The hidden regression.</strong> On Legacy, watch the demag-pressure and occupancy
        anti-collapse toggles do something. Switch to New: those toggles no longer do anything at all,
        because <code>u_use_real_magnetics</code> is 0 on that backend.</li>
      </ul>

  <section id="living">
    <div class="section-top">
      <div class="colorbar mpm"></div>
      <p class="eyebrow">Living material &#183; four modules, three selectable</p>
      <h2>Growing tissue: what actually produces new area</h2>
      <p class="lede">A living material has to answer two separate questions &#8212; <em>where</em>
      should the body be, and <em>how</em> does material get there &#8212; and every failure in this
      family came from answering them with one mechanism. Growth also has to terminate: a body that
      keeps growing is not an organism, it is a leak.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="chip-row">
      <span class="chip">Culture</span>
      <span class="chip">Automata [legacy]</span>
      <span class="chip">Colony + Bloom [legacy]</span>
    </div>

    <h3>The generations</h3>
    <table>
      <tr><th>Generation</th><th>What it did</th><th>The symptom you saw</th><th>Root cause</th></tr>
      <tr>
        <td class="name">Colony &#183; spawn-first<br><span class="b yellow">selectable</span></td>
        <td>A dedicated 128&#178; life grid with occupancy, a diffusing energy field sourced by placed
        cores, and a selectable SmoothLife or Gray-Scott guide. <strong>Growth is particle
        spawning</strong>: a frontier cell that is wanted, fed and empty atomically claims a slot, and
        the CPU expands it into a 2&#215;2 cluster of new cells. Energy is debited at each spawn, so a
        colony cut off from a core runs down; cores latch dead below vitality 0.06, making
        "burn every heart" a permanent kill.</td>
        <td class="notes">The body did not grow, it <em>appeared</em> &#8212; discrete 2&#215;2 clumps
        popping in at the edge (the changelog's "strange cell-generating look") &#8212; and it ate the
        particle budget at 256 cells &#215; 4 particles every 3 frames.</td>
        <td class="notes">Making discrete particle insertion the primary motion makes the visual
        quantum of growth a whole cluster. There is no continuous mechanism between <em>nothing</em>
        and <em>four new particles</em>.</td>
      </tr>
      <tr>
        <td class="name">Colony &#183; expand + fill<br><span class="b yellow">selectable</span></td>
        <td>Expansion becomes primary, spawning demoted to gap-fill. A per-cell
        <code>growth_drive</code> is baked into phase data and read by the constitutive branch as
        <code>J_rest = 1 + phase_data</code>, so fed cells push outward continuously; the frontier scan
        can now only repair already-living cells that thinned.</td>
        <td class="notes">It grows forever. The body inflates smoothly and pleasantly and keeps
        inflating until it hits the soft particle cap or the energy radius. There is no state at which
        it is done.</td>
        <td class="notes">Named in the design spec that replaced it: <em>"the current EXPAND_FILL growth
        never saturates"</em>. The drive uses <code>max(want, sparse)</code>, and the edge of a spreading body
        is permanently sparse, so the drive never turns off. The first cut also spawned at the empty
        edge, compounding spawn front and expansion into a 36k-particle runaway before the predicate
        was narrowed.</td>
      </tr>
      <tr>
        <td class="name">Bloom &#183; bounded<br><span class="b yellow">selectable</span></td>
        <td>Eases each cell's rest-volume excess toward a fixed target and holds there; spawning fires
        only in a genuine interior tear (an empty cell with tissue on <em>opposite</em> sides), never at
        the advancing edge. The same entry also made the living materials burnable at all &#8212; they
        had been absent from the thermal gate, so their temperature literally never moved and fire did
        nothing to them.</td>
        <td class="notes">It inflates once and is then inert. A balloon, not an organism. Roughly four
        tear repairs across a whole verified run at about 725 particles.</td>
        <td class="notes">The bounded target has <em>no guide term and no occupancy term at all</em>.
        The pattern engine is still computed every frame and contributes nothing to a bloom's shape.
        Boundedness was achieved by removing the feedback rather than closing it.</td>
      </tr>
      <tr>
        <td class="name">Automata<br><span class="b yellow">selectable</span></td>
        <td>Split the two questions properly for the first time: a SmoothLife field that is an automaton
        on <em>itself</em> (so it self-bounds) decides where, and a purely geometric ring rule decides
        whether to insert &#8212; one particle into an empty cell encircled by at least 6 of 8 occupied
        neighbours. A <strong>bio force</strong> is the link: particles drift up
        grad(field &#8722; occ) into the field's empty birth ring. Two distinct burn end-states as
        separate materials: hard brittle ash that shatters, and soft wood char.</td>
        <td class="notes">The ring rule essentially never fires: <strong>8 insertions in 2520
        frames</strong> (about 21 s at 120&#160;Hz) on an 882-particle blob. Tissue thickens and drifts
        but never develops the porous structure the field preview promises.</td>
        <td class="notes">The field is force-fed the body's own shape: <code>target = max(target, occ)</code>,
        then an ash boost on top. The field can never dip below occupancy inside the body, so no
        interior hole can be <em>carved</em> &#8212; only torn open mechanically &#8212; and the ring
        rule then demands that torn hole also be ringed by 6 of 8. Compounding it, the kernel is a toy:
        17 inner plus 32 ring taps at radii 2 and 5 cells, which can only low-pass occupancy into a
        blob.</td>
      </tr>
      <tr>
        <td class="name">Culture<br><span class="b green">default</span></td>
        <td>A 256&#178; module with a faithful port of a reference SmoothLife: <strong>489 taps at
        radius 13</strong>, an order of magnitude more kernel, and critically <em>not</em> max()'d with
        occupancy. Occupancy only <em>confines</em> the field (a blurred mask multiplies it) and
        <em>nucleates</em> it (hash noise injected where tissue exists but the field is still flat).
        The coupling is inverted: instead of asking for particles to be inserted into holes, every
        particle is pushed <strong>up grad(field)</strong> onto the membrane ridge, so holes evacuate
        and the material inherits the porous structure. Sculpt mode is mass-conserving; grow mode
        inserts only on membrane cells adjacent to tissue, under a hard lifetime budget.</td>
        <td class="notes">Current. Its own first cut ran away to the 100k cap because the density target
        was never reached, so the same cells re-fired forever.</td>
        <td class="notes">That took <em>three</em> stacked guards to bound &#8212; a build-once density
        threshold, a frontier adjacency gate, and a hard lifetime counter &#8212; rather than one
        principled bound. Worth knowing before trusting grow mode in a new scene.</td>
      </tr>
    </table>

    <div class="grid-2">
      <div class="panel">
        <h3>Why Culture is good enough</h3>
        <p>It is the first generation where the pattern engine and the material are coupled in the
        direction that works. Colony asked <em>spawning</em> to be the growth (quantised, popcorn);
        expand+fill asked <em>inflation</em> to be the growth (never terminates); Automata asked the
        geometry to <em>find</em> holes the field never carved (8 firings in 21 seconds). Culture
        separates them: a properly resolved continuous automaton &#8212; 489 taps at radius 13, with
        occupancy confining and nucleating it but never overwriting it &#8212; genuinely produces
        membranes and holes; then a gradient force drags existing matter onto the ridges.</p>
        <p>That is the whole argument. The porous structure is achieved by <strong>rearrangement</strong>,
        which is mass-conserving and therefore bounded by construction, and insertion becomes an
        optional budgeted extra rather than the mechanism. Sculpt mode <em>cannot</em> run away; grow
        mode's bound is a counter, not a tuned feedback loop.</p>
        <p>The regrowth rule is deliberately asymmetric &#8212; living tissue heals to full unless
        actually hot, dead tissue converts back only where green is nearby &#8212; so green always wins
        at a boundary and a burn scar heals as a visible front. Every earlier heal/starve pair diluted
        into a grey equilibrium instead. The project confirmed the result in-app before building on it,
        and tagged a checkpoint at that state.</p>
      </div>
      <div class="panel">
        <h3>What is still wrong</h3>
        <ul class="bullet-list">
          <li><strong>It cannot die of anything but heat.</strong> The starve rate is still passed in,
          and the shader marks it <em>unused: living tissue is sticky, only burning kills</em> &#8212;
          while the panel tooltip still tells you the body dies if you do not beat the starve rate.
          That tooltip is false.</li>
          <li><strong>No kill switch.</strong> Culture has no cores and no energy budget, so it lost
          Colony's best mechanic: a permanently latched dead core. Any surviving green reclaims any
          adjacent white, so the only kill is burning 100&#37; of the body.</li>
          <li><strong>The changelog overstates the material transition.</strong> It says burnable tissue
          becomes a brittle husk material; the code never changes a material id &#8212; the husk is a
          <code>vitality &lt;= u_dead_floor</code> branch (default 0.15) <em>inside</em> the tissue material, and the husk id
          is only ever placeable by hand.</li>
          <li><strong>The revival field is not self-limiting</strong>, contrary to the changelog. Its
          source is the blurred <em>living</em> occupancy; the husk texture is read only by the stats
          pass. Ash gating happens per particle, not in the field, so the field is computed over empty
          air around any green tissue.</li>
          <li><strong>Hidden coupling.</strong> One mask texture is used both as the field's confinement
          mask and as the revival field's green source, so moving <em>Confine Radius</em> silently
          changes regrowth reach. No tooltip says so.</li>
          <li><strong>It only reads correctly at low gravity.</strong> The showcase scenes hard-code
          &#8722;1.5&#160;g with the comment <em>"so the porous structure shows"</em>. At &#8722;9.81 the
          membrane impulse (clamped to 1.8) loses to gravity and the tissue puddles.</li>
          <li><strong>It is the most expensive backend</strong>: 489 taps &#215; 65,536 cells per frame,
          8 to 14&#160;ms/frame against Automata's 6 to 8.</li>
          <li><strong>Grow-then-hold is a lifetime counter, not physiology.</strong> The counter resets
          only when the module is disabled, so a body cut in half can recover vitality but never
          mass.</li>
          <li><strong>Discoverability.</strong> The Creation menu still lists all eight living materials,
          but placing a Colony core in a default scene does nothing, because scene load forces the
          Version selector back to Culture and disables the other two modules. Only one tooltip
          mentions the Version dropdown at all.</li>
        </ul>
      </div>
    </div>

    <div class="note">
      <p><strong>When you would still pick an older version.</strong> <em>Colony + Bloom</em> is the only
      generation with cores, metabolic energy and a permanent kill &#8212; "burn the heart and the
      creature stays dead" exists nowhere else. It is also the only one whose growth follows level
      geometry (a bias toward SDF surfaces) and the only one offering Gray-Scott mitosis as a growth
      guide; within it, Bounded organism is the cheapest way to get a fixed-size organic body (about 725
      particles, about 4 spawns for a whole run). <em>Automata</em> is the cheapest per frame and the
      only one with two <em>distinct</em> burn end-states as separate materials &#8212; hard brittle ash
      that accumulates fracture and shatters into chunks, and soft wood char &#8212; plus an ash
      accelerant that makes regrowth race into dead ash. If you want a shatterable burnt organism rather
      than a porous one, Automata is still the right pick.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="callout">
      <p><strong>The A/B to run.</strong> Scene load sets the backend to Culture and disables all three
      modules every time; only the five legacy showcase scenes override it.</p>
      <ul>
        <li>Load <em>Showcase: Living Culture</em> and open Advanced &#8594; Living Material. Three live
        previews: field, occupancy, revival. The field should be bright rings around dark holes, and the
        occupancy preview should have holes <em>in the same places</em> &#8212; that is the membrane
        force working. Watch <code>grown: N / 4000</code> climb and then stop.</li>
        <li>Switch Version to <em>Automata [legacy]</em>, tick Enable, load its showcase. Its single
        field preview is a smoothed copy of the body silhouette with no interior holes, and

        &#8212; and never stops until it hits the soft cap. Set Bounded organism and it swells once and

        for the regrowth field itself.</li>
      </ul>
    </div>

  <section id="regrowth">
    <div class="section-top">
      <div class="colorbar heat"></div>
      <p class="eyebrow">Ash regrowth &#183; two generations</p>
      <h2>Two shaders, one buffer: an ownership bug wearing a tuning bug's clothes</h2>
      <p class="lede">This family is small and worth reading anyway, because it is the cleanest example
      in the project of a defect that no amount of threshold tuning could have fixed. The goal is
      modest: burnt ash next to living tissue should come back.</p>
    </div>

    <h3>The generations</h3>
    <table>
      <tr><th>Generation</th><th>What it did</th><th>The symptom you saw</th><th>Root cause</th></tr>
      <tr>
        <td class="name">Legacy<br><span class="b orange">superseded</span></td>
        <td>Two independent state machines wrote the <em>same</em> two per-particle buffers every
        frame. p2g integrated regrow and ash from <strong>temperature alone</strong>
        (heal below 360&#160;K, burn above 540&#160;K) and stored them; g2p then integrated the same
        two channels from a <strong>field-gated neighbour rule</strong> and stored them again. g2p runs
        last, so g2p won every frame.</td>
        <td class="notes">Burn a corner off an ash block, let it cool: the black patch either sits there
        or briefly lightens and then goes dark again. After you release the heat gun it idles for a long
        time doing nothing at all.</td>
        <td class="notes">Three defects stacked. <em>Fighting:</em> g2p ran second and relaxed regrow
        toward a near-zero field target, stalling or reversing p2g's temperature heal.
        <em>Dead code:</em> the whole g2p neighbour branch sat behind a bio-field toggle that was off by
        default <em>and</em> reset to false on every scene load, so in ordinary scenes the rule never
        executed at all. <em>Band mismatch:</em> p2g healed below 360&#160;K while g2p's ash gate faded
        from 365&#160;K, leaving a recovery-critical window where neither machine progressed. The p2g
        comment block still preserves the archaeology (<em>420K was too low, raise to 540K, widen the
        neutral band</em>) &#8212; a log of failed tuning against an ownership bug.</td>
      </tr>
      <tr>
        <td class="name">Living Regrowth v2<br><span class="b green">default</span></td>
        <td>Single-owner state. p2g still computes its values for this frame's stress but
        <strong>defers the writes</strong>; g2p owns the buffers. Impact crumble stays p2g-owned,
        because g2p does not compute it. The neighbour branch is forced on rather than depending on
        legacy toggles. "Living nearby" is redefined as a robust locality envelope, seeded by both ash
        and living tissue and surviving fire heat. The temperature band collapses to a single ramp over
        380 to 420&#160;K, eliminating the dead zone. The integrator is an explicit per-second rate
        (0.25/s, boosted up to 2.5&#215; when adjacent), so roughly 3 to 8&#160;s to full. Finally, the
        Eulerian bio passes auto-enable whenever the solver reports living materials present.</td>
        <td class="notes">Current and default-on.</td>
        <td class="notes">The remaining defects are scoping, not correctness &#8212; see below.</td>
      </tr>
    </table>

    <div class="grid-2">
      <div class="panel">
        <h3>Why v2 is good enough</h3>
        <p>The legacy failure was not a tuning problem, it was an ownership problem: two writers to one
        buffer, with the loser recomputed from scratch every frame. That is why the shader comment reads
        like a log of failed thresholds. v2 makes g2p the sole owner and leaves p2g's values as
        read-only intermediates for the current frame's stress. The second fix is equally structural:
        the neighbour rule had been gated behind a toggle scene loading reset to false, so the feature
        was unreachable in normal play; v2 forces the branch on and auto-enables the bio passes from
        spawn-time material detection, so it works without the user knowing a bio field exists. Aligning
        the temperature bands &#8212; the only part that looks like tuning &#8212; only became possible
        once there was a single owner to align.</p>
      </div>
      <div class="panel">
        <h3>What is still wrong</h3>
        <ul class="bullet-list">
          <li><strong>"Same object" is a proximity heuristic.</strong> The changelog admits it: no
          per-particle object id exists. Two unrelated ash bodies touching will heal each other.</li>
          <li><strong>The detection latch is sticky.</strong> It is cleared only by a full particle
          clear, so once any living material has existed in a session the Eulerian bio passes stay on
          for the rest of that scene even after the material is gone.</li>
          <li><strong>It costs work the default configuration does not use.</strong> The detection list
          includes the Culture tissue materials, so placing Culture tissue flips the bio passes on
          &#8212; but nothing in the Culture backend reads that field. Culture has its own 256&#178;
          revival field.</li>
          <li><strong>Narrower scope than the name suggests.</strong> The v2 branch applies to the two
          ash-regrowth materials only. Morph Tissue and Cell Sheet are the <em>neighbours</em> that
          make ash regrow; they do not regrow through this path themselves.</li>
          <li><strong>It is state recovery, not resurrection.</strong> No material-id transition and no
          particle spawn, so a fully destroyed region cannot come back.</li>
          <li><strong>No measured regrow curve.</strong> Only a smoke-test recipe was recorded.</li>
        </ul>
      </div>
    </div>

    <div class="note">
      <p><strong>When you would still pick the older version.</strong> Never for behaviour &#8212; the
      legacy path is strictly worse. The toggle survives as a diagnostic bisector: if regrow or ash
      values look wrong, turning v2 off tells you whether the g2p neighbour path or the p2g temperature
      path produced them, and <em>Force Bio Passes</em> separately isolates whether the problem is the
      field gate or the two-writer conflict.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="callout">
      <p><strong>The A/B to run.</strong> In an ordinary scene (not a bench), place an ash-regrowth block
      directly beside Morph Tissue or Cell Sheet. Burn part of the ash, release, let it cool. With v2 on
      the burnt region darkens and then visibly regrows to alive in roughly 3 to 8&#160;s, bulging
      slightly past its original outline; raise <em>Regrow Rate</em> to move the front faster. Untick
      <em>Living Regrowth v2</em> and repeat: either no neighbour regrowth at all, or a front that starts
      and stalls. Then turn <em>Force Bio Passes</em> on with v2 still off &#8212; the passes now run, so
      whatever stall remains is the two state machines fighting, not the dead gate.</p>
    </div>

    <p class="note"><strong>2026-09-19 qualification:</strong> the nonlocal damage path exists, but
    <code>l0 = profile_ratio × scale × dx</code> in <code>shaders/physics/mpm_p2g.comp</code>.
    Keeping the ratio fixed changes the physical length with resolution. References below to
    resolution-independent crack width or speed describe an intended property, not a demonstrated
    result. See <a href="model_audit_20260919.md#7-tearing-anisotropic-damage-and-solver-coupling">the current audit</a>.</p>
    <div class="section-top">
      <div class="colorbar mpm"></div>
      <p class="eyebrow">Fracture &#183; three models</p>
      <h2>A threshold makes a scatter of failures; a length scale makes a crack</h2>
      <p class="lede">Breaking a solid in MPM means deciding, per particle, how much of its stress to
      keep. The hard part is that a crack is not a property of any single particle: it is a
      <em>front</em>, and a front needs its neighbours in the equation. The legacy model had no
      neighbour term at all, which is why its cracks had no tip and why their speed depended on the grid
      you happened to be running.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="chip-row">
      <span class="chip">Legacy (hand-tuned)</span>
      <span class="chip">AnisoMPM isotropic</span>
      <span class="chip">AnisoMPM anisotropic</span>
    </div>

    <h3>The generations</h3>
    <table>
      <tr><th>Generation</th><th>What it did</th><th>The symptom you saw</th><th>Root cause</th></tr>
      <tr>
        <td class="name">Legacy<br><span class="b green">still default</span><br><span class="b yellow">selectable</span></td>
        <td>Per-material, hand-tuned strain thresholds on the singular values, replicated roughly 35
        times across the constitutive branches with their own magic numbers. The glass branch is
        representative: <code>crack += max(tensile*1.6 + shear*1.2 - thresh, 0) * rate * dt</code>, then
        <code>integrity = max(1 - crack*0.82, 0.18)</code> multiplying the already-summed stress.</td>
        <td class="notes">What looks like a crack is a <em>scatter</em> of independently failed
        particles. Tears go limp and mushy instead of stringy. The same scene cracks at a visibly
        different speed if you change the grid resolution.</td>
        <td class="notes">Four separate problems. The drive was singular-value <em>stretches</em>, not
        energy or stress &#8212; no fracture energy, no critical stress anywhere in the code. There was
        <strong>no regularization</strong>, so no crack tip, no propagation front, and a crack width
        equal to the particle spacing, which is exactly why the crack speed tracks the resolution.
        Degradation was linear with a large floor (18&#37; of the stress survives full damage) instead
        of (1&#8722;d)&#178;. And it multiplied the <em>already-summed</em> stress including the fiber
        term &#8212; the direct cause of limp tears &#8212; while the drive itself was essentially
        isotropic even though fiber directions were driving orthotropic stiffness.</td>
      </tr>

        <td class="name">AnisoMPM isotropic<br><span class="b yellow">selectable</span></td>

        built on the <em>polar</em> rotation, an elastic threshold below which no damage accrues at
        all, a monotone driving-force history so cracks do not stall mid-propagation, and explicit
        damage evolution with the non-local term
        &#951;&#7691; = &#10216;(1&#8722;d)D&#771; &#8722; (d &#8722; l&#8320;&#178;&#8711;&#178;d)&#10217;.</td>

        critical stress had been normalized hydrostatically while the stress it was compared against was
        uniaxial, so the isotropic mode needed &#8730;2&#215; more stress than authored. Mode 1 to mode 2
        therefore changed toughness as well as direction, defeating the entire point of having a
        control.</td>
      </tr>
      <tr>
        <td class="name">AnisoMPM anisotropic<br><span class="b yellow">selectable</span></td>

        stringy.</td>
        <td class="notes">Selectable, off by default. Enrolled so far: the orthotropic tear material,
        brittle and tough.</td>
        <td class="notes">The legacy orthotropic drive

    <div class="grid-2">
      <div class="panel">
        <h3>Why AnisoMPM is the right model, and why it is not the default</h3>
        <p>The single load-bearing change is the non-local term. Everything else &#8212; an energy-based
        drive, an elastic threshold, a monotone history, (1&#8722;d)&#178; degradation &#8212; is
        correctness housekeeping that a careful person could have hand-tuned into the legacy branch. The
        Laplacian cannot be: it introduces a physical length l&#8320; into a model that previously had
        none, and that is what converts a threshold heuristic into an actual propagating crack with
        resolution-independent speed. It also makes the anisotropy real, because the structural tensor
        is built on the polar rotation rather than the deformation-gradient pushforward, which would
        contaminate direction with stretch.</p>
        <p>It stays off by default because the shipped v1 had six real defects, all found by a re-run
        adversarial review after the first review was cut off mid-flight. The most instructive: the
        damage transfer <strong>was not weight-normalized</strong> &#8212; the scatter early-returned on
        d &#8804; 0, so the denominator accumulated weight only from already-damaged particles, making
        the node value a mean over damaged particles that intact neighbours never diluted, and making
        the free-surface fallback fire for nodes full of intact material. All four review lenses
        converged on that one line independently. The gather's Laplacian was also a hand-rolled 5-point
        stencil centred on <code>floor(cell_pos)</code> while the scatter deposits on
        <code>round(cell_pos)</code>, so for about half of all particles it was sampled a full node
        down-left, and the offset flipped discontinuously as a particle drifted across each half-cell
        line. A third defect is a good warning for this codebase generally: the per-particle damage aux
        was cleared only in <code>init()</code>, but the particle buffer is a bump allocator whose
        rewind does not re-run init, and the driving-force history is monotone &#8212; so a respawned
        particle could be born at maximum driving force and damage itself to failure under no
        load.</p>
        <p>Storage deserves a note, because it is a reusable trick here: both p2g and g2p sit
        <em>at</em> the 16-SSBO driver cap, so the per-material fracture profile rides a
        <code>samplerBuffer</code> and the per-particle damage aux rides an <code>imageBuffer</code>
        &#8212; image uniforms live in a separate binding namespace. That escape hatch is now used by
        three subsystems on this page.</p>
      </div>
      <div class="panel">
        <h3>What is still wrong</h3>
        <ul class="bullet-list">
          <li><strong>Enrollment is real work, not a data edit</strong> &#8212; and the first changelog
          entry said otherwise, which the correctness pass explicitly retracts. p2g has exactly
          <em>two</em> enrolled hooks, which is why exactly three materials work. Ceramic, splinter,
          tear-skin, laminated pastry, stoneware, composite and aniso each have their own constitutive
          branch with no hook, so setting the allow bit would enrol them in the scatter and gather
          &#8212; polluting the shared damage grid &#8212; while p2g never ran the damage step.</li>
          <li><strong>The damage grid carries no material key.</strong> Damage can diffuse across a
          contact between two different enrolled materials under zero load.</li>
          <li><strong>The free-surface test is a magnitude test.</strong> <code>den &lt; 1e-8</code>
          cannot express "negligible weight" given the 1e&#8722;6 fixed-point quantization.</li>
          <li><strong>&#934; is exactly invariant to thermal softening</strong>, because
          &#963;<sub>c</sub> and the stress scale with the same moduli. That is arguably correct for a
          strain criterion, but the code comment claims otherwise, so one of the two is wrong.</li>
          <li><strong>A claim in the record did not survive measurement, and it is left standing on
          purpose.</strong> The verifier argued Lap(d) was identically zero for an isolated damaged
          particle, i.e. that the regularization was inert. It did not reproduce: a seeded probe gives
          min &#8722;811 / mean &#8722;327 with the bug against &#8722;604 / &#8722;201 with the fix
          &#8212; nonzero and correctly signed in both, because the idealized isolation is destroyed
          within a frame or two. The real effect was that the length scale came out 1.5 to 2.8&#215; too
          large, not that the term was dead. Fixed anyway, because it is not what the spec
          prescribes.</li>
          <li><strong>Nine lower-severity findings were capped unverified</strong> when the review run
          hit a usage limit, and live A/B in the tearing-lab scenes &#8212; the actual calibration step
          &#8212; has not been done.</li>
          <li><strong>Doc drift.</strong> The status page still says phase-field brittle has
          <em>no Laplacian regularization</em> and calls the AT2 PDE a future improvement of about 300
          lines. That is essentially what shipped here, for the enrolled materials.</li>
        </ul>
      </div>
    </div>

    <div class="note">
      <p><strong>When you would still pick Legacy.</strong> For everything except the three enrolled
      materials, Legacy <em>is</em> the model &#8212; there is no alternative branch to select. Beyond
      that: Legacy is byte-identical off (the CPU gates both damage dispatches entirely, so it is also
      strictly cheaper: zero GPU cost, two fewer passes per substep), and its per-material hand tuning
      encodes a lot of look development that the AnisoMPM profiles have not been calibrated against yet.
      If a scene's brittle or glass shatter reads right today, switching fracture models will change it.
      Mode 1 against mode 2 is the interesting comparison; mode 0 against the rest is a different
      material model entirely.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="callout">
      <p><strong>The A/B to run.</strong> Take a tearing-lab scene with the orthotropic tear material and
      pull it apart with the cursor. On <em>Legacy</em>, watch the failure region: a widening mush with
      no leading point, and the tear goes limp because the fiber term is degraded along with everything
      else. Switch to <em>AnisoMPM: anisotropic</em> and repeat: the crack acquires a tip, and because
      the fiber term is never degraded you get stringy fibrils bridging the gap. Then switch to
      <em>AnisoMPM: isotropic</em> with the same toughness slider &#8212; the crack should be equally
      easy to start but should no longer prefer the grain direction. That pair is the controlled
      experiment. To see the resolution dependence directly, run the same scene at two grid resolutions
      on Legacy and time the crack across the plate.</p>
    </div>

  <section id="stiffness">
    <div class="section-top">
      <div class="colorbar mpm"></div>
      <p class="eyebrow">Stiffness &#183; three models</p>
      <h2>Why everything was too soft: a catalog written for the wrong world size</h2>
      <p class="lede">A one-metre column of material has to support &#961;gH at its base, which in this
      6.4&#160;m world is roughly 10 to 20&#160;kPa. The material catalog was authored at 4 to
      12&#160;kPa. That single mismatch produced most of the project's "everything feels floaty"
      complaints, and two of the three generations here are attempts to fix it without retuning the
      catalog.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="chip-row">
      <span class="chip">Legacy (v1)</span>
      <span class="chip">v2: Barrier</span>
      <span class="chip">v3: World Scale</span>
    </div>

    <h3>The generations</h3>
    <table>
      <tr><th>Generation</th><th>What it did</th><th>The symptom you saw</th><th>Root cause</th></tr>
      <tr>
        <td class="name">Legacy v1<br><span class="b yellow">selectable</span></td>
        <td>Plain fixed-corotated elasticity with a deformation-gradient singular-value clamp to
        [0.5, 3.0] and a stress-norm ceiling at roughly 0.8&#215;E.</td>
        <td class="notes"><em>Collapse into a line.</em> Drop a heavy ball on a brittle pillar and past
        about 50&#37; compression it keeps compacting with no growing resistance. Spawn a rope or fiber
        horizontally and it flattens to a literal line.</td>
        <td class="notes">The clamp and the ceiling together <strong>saturate</strong> the compressive
        restoring stress, so gravity keeps loading a material that has stopped pushing back &#8212;
        bottomless compaction. Separately, the orthotropic cross-fiber stiffness was 5 to 8&#37; of an
        already-kPa-scale modulus, which is <em>below the weight of one grid cell</em>, so those
        materials had no cross-section to defend.</td>
      </tr>
      <tr>
        <td class="name">v2: Barrier<br><span class="b yellow">selectable</span></td>
        <td>An isotropic compression-barrier pressure that keeps growing with the <strong>raw
        pre-clamp</strong> overcompression, applied <em>after</em> the ceiling so it cannot be clipped;
        floors on the ortho cross and shear scales while keeping strong anisotropy; and a global
        log-scale stiffness multiplier (default 1.0&#215;, i.e. unchanged) instead of blind-retuning
        the whole catalog.</td>
        <td class="notes">Columns stop collapsing into lines and ropes keep a cross-section. But
        structural solids are still soft: a tall column still compacts, just more gently, and the fix
        for that is a slider the user has to find.</td>
        <td class="notes">v2 fixed the <em>saturation</em> without fixing the <em>scale</em>. The
        volumetric law is hard-bounded at 0.25&#955;, so at E = 9000 the maximum support is around
        1.3&#160;kPa &#8212; an order of magnitude short of what the column base needs, and the material
        compacts until the SVD floor freezes it in a shrunken, non-physical rest state. A global
        multiplier can reach the right number, but applying one multiplier to bread and to granite is
        not a model, it is a knob.</td>
      </tr>
      <tr>
        <td class="name">v3: World Scale<br><span class="b green">default</span></td>
        <td>Three coordinated changes. (1) Per-<em>family</em> multipliers: structural solids
        &#215;12 so &#955; finally exceeds &#961;gH, the puffy/soft-body family &#215;2 so it firms up
        without losing squish, fluids and living culture untouched. (2) The solid SVD compression floor
        rises 0.5 to 0.85, so the clamp becomes a rare safety guard rather than the resting state
        &#8212; and since the compression barrier activates at J = &#963;<sub>min</sub>&#178;, it now
        engages at J about 0.72 instead of 0.25, growing support continuously. (3) Substeps scale with
        &#8730;(E ratio), 5 up to 20, so the raised elastic wave speed stays CFL-stable.</td>
        <td class="notes">Current, and promoted to default after live user validation: <em>"some overly
        weak materials are now firm enough (like the glass / tempered glass)"</em>.</td>
        <td class="notes">The cost is real and known: at &#215;12 the solver runs about 18 substeps,
        measured at 7.5 to 9.3&#160;ms/frame against a roughly 7&#160;ms baseline.</td>
      </tr>
    </table>

    <div class="grid-2">
      <div class="panel">
        <h3>Why v3 is good enough</h3>
        <p>Because it is the first version that made the three changes <em>together</em>. Raising E alone
        blows up an explicit scheme &#8212; that was the audit's own caveat &#8212; so the substep
        scaling is not a performance afterthought, it is part of the fix. Raising E without moving the
        SVD floor would have left the clamp as the resting state, so the material would still have been
        frozen in a shrunken configuration, just a stiffer one. And doing it per family rather than
        globally is what lets bread stay squishy while a column holds: the soft-body list is shared with
        the existing stress-ceiling branch, so the two agree by construction rather than by
        coincidence.</p>
        <p>The honest framing is that v3 is a <em>calibration</em>, not a new constitutive model. It
        does not change any law; it puts the catalog's numbers into the right decade for the world size
        and then pays the CFL bill that implies.</p>
      </div>
      <div class="panel">
        <h3>What is still wrong</h3>
        <ul class="bullet-list">
          <li><strong>The multipliers are per family, not per material.</strong> Twelve is one number
          covering every structural solid; the right answer is a re-authored catalog in physical units,
          which nobody has done.</li>
          <li><strong>It costs up to 4&#215; the substeps.</strong> That is the known trade, and it is
          charged to every MPM material in the scene, not only the stiffened ones.</li>
          <li><strong>Real-Time Pacing is a separate, still-off toggle.</strong> The legacy loop runs
          exactly one fixed 1/120&#160;s step per rendered frame, so at 60&#160;Hz vsync the entire
          simulation runs at <em>half real-time speed</em> and sim speed depends on frame rate &#8212; a
          large part of the same floaty feel that v3 addresses from the other side. If a scene still
          feels weak with v3 on, check the pacing toggle before touching the multipliers.</li>
          <li><strong>&#215;12 changes look, not just strength.</strong> Any scene tuned against v1 or
          v2 will read differently: stiffer bounces, less settle, different pile angles.</li>
          <li><strong>The technique guide's glass section is now partly stale.</strong> "Why glass still
          collapses too easily" describes a v1/v2 world; glass is one of the materials the user
          specifically confirmed as fixed by v3.</li>
        </ul>
      </div>
    </div>

    <div class="note">
      <p><strong>When you would still pick an older version.</strong> Pick <em>v2: Barrier</em> when frame
      budget matters more than realism &#8212; it keeps the anti-collapse fix, which is the one that
      prevents outright broken-looking behaviour, at 5 substeps instead of up to 20. Pick it also for any
      scene whose look was authored before v3 became default. Pick <em>Legacy v1</em> only to demonstrate
      the collapse: it is the cleanest possible illustration of what a saturating stress law does under
      sustained load, and the pillar test takes about five seconds.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="callout">
      <p><strong>The A/B to run.</strong> Build a tall column of Elastic or Ceramic. On
      <em>Legacy (v1)</em> it compacts and collapses. On <em>v2: Barrier</em> it resists with growing
      force but still settles noticeably shorter. On <em>v3: World Scale</em> it should hold its height.
      Then check the other end of the range in the same run: bread, goo, crumb and tissue should still be
      squishy under v3 &#8212; if they have gone rigid, the soft multiplier is too high, not the solid
      one. Watch the substep counter while you switch; the frame-time rise from 5 to 18 substeps is the
      price on the label.</p>
    </div>
  </section>


  <section id="blast">
    <div class="section-top">
      <div class="colorbar air"></div>
      <p class="eyebrow">Explosions &#183; two models</p>
      <h2>One energy, or four unrelated polynomials</h2>
      <p class="lede">An explosion in this engine has to do five things at once: push air, push
      particles, heat, make smoke, and draw a ring. The legacy model did all five from separate
      hand-authored curves, so they could not agree with each other; the current one derives all five
      from a single energy scalar and a closed-form front radius.</p>
    </div>

    <h3>The generations</h3>
    <table>
      <tr><th>Generation</th><th>What it did</th><th>The symptom you saw</th><th>Root cause</th></tr>
      <tr>
        <td class="name">Legacy (per-frame push)<br><span class="b green">still default</span></td>
        <td>A per-frame emitter at the blast site that injects strength, heat, smoke and velocity
        divergence into the air grid from four independent polynomial falloffs, while the drawn
        shockwave ring integrates its own <code>speed &#215; dt</code> radius separately.</td>
        <td class="notes">Blasts feel weak or too strong and the knobs fight each other; the drawn ring
        and the thing that actually pushes you are not in the same place; the outward push is largely
        eaten by the pressure projection.</td>
        <td class="notes">Named in the audit as "independent polynomials". With four unrelated curves
        there is no conserved quantity anywhere, so tuning the push changes the heat by an unrelated
        amount and the visible ring is decoration rather than a readout. Worse, a per-frame velocity
        injection is exactly the kind of source a divergence-free projection is designed to
        annihilate.</td>
      </tr>
      <tr>
        <td class="name">Sedov Front<br><span class="b yellow">selectable</span></td>
        <td>One analytic front per rupture, with radius following the 2D Sedov&#8211;Taylor law
        r(t) = &#958;(Et&#178;/&#961;&#8320;)<sup>1/4</sup>, transitioning to a constant acoustic speed
        once it slows below c&#8320;. <strong>One energy scalar E</strong> drives front speed,
        overpressure, heat, smoke and particle impulse together. Each substep the CPU advances the
        radius and three GPU passes read the annulus between the old and new radius: a divergence, heat
        and smoke deposit <em>before</em> the projection; a one-frame directional gust on the MAC faces
        <em>after</em> it; and an outward particle kick where each particle is hit
        <strong>at most once per front</strong> via a persistent guard buffer.</td>
        <td class="notes">Selectable, still off by default pending live-fire calibration.</td>
        <td class="notes">The ordering is the point: depositing divergence before the projection makes
        it the one blast channel the projection <em>cannot</em> annihilate &#8212; it gets converted
        into wall-aware outward flow instead &#8212; and reflection and diffraction of the aftermath
        then come free from the Neumann boundary conditions.</td>
      </tr>
    </table>

    <div class="grid-2">
      <div class="panel">
        <h3>Why Sedov is the better model</h3>
        <p>Two structural wins. First, single ownership of energy: under Sedov the legacy per-frame
        emitter is skipped entirely, so exactly one thing owns strength, heat, smoke and divergence, and
        a follow-on pass extended that discipline to vents (the trapped pressure inside a vented cluster
        is deducted so the legacy cavity-vent branch cannot release the same energy twice). Second,
        correct time semantics: all front-crossing deposits are dt-free impulses consumed by exactly one
        projection per step, which is what makes the behaviour independent of substep count.</p>
        <p>The follow-on pass also made the drawn ring honest: it now follows the physical front radius
        instead of a fabricated integration, and a retired front hands its record back to the legacy
        integrator so it fades from its last radius with no pop. Casing particles are pre-seeded into
        the guard buffer at rupture, so the front no longer double-kicks the shell that the legacy burst
        pushes already carry.</p>
      </div>
      <div class="panel">
        <h3>What is still wrong</h3>
        <ul class="bullet-list">
          <li><strong>It is not the default</strong>, because live-fire calibration was never done. Every
          number in it &#8212; &#958;, c&#8320;, overpressure, impulse, thickness &#8212; is a live knob
          with an unvalidated starting value.</li>
          <li><strong>Vent fronts and backdraft flash fronts are both default-off</strong> even under
          Sedov, so out of the box a dug-wall rupture still uses the legacy path.</li>
          <li><strong>Mirror and reflection fronts were deliberately deferred</strong>: the image-source
          origin lands inside the reflecting wall, so it would need a change to the front packing to
          paint anything. Reflections you see are the aftermath through the projection, not reflected
          fronts.</li>
          <li><strong>Two real defects were found by review after shipping</strong>, both fixed, both
          worth knowing as failure modes of this design: the ring froze at radius 0 in single-substep
          pacing because the radius query shared its zero sentinel between "absent" and "alive at
          t = 0"; and backdraft fronts replayed frozen bins after the backdraft feature was turned
          off.</li>
          <li><strong>The cosmetic emitters still fire at rupture</strong> (shockwave, scorch, crack
          ring) independently of the model, so there remain two sources of visual truth.</li>
        </ul>
      </div>
    </div>

    <div class="note">
      <p><strong>When you would still pick Legacy.</strong> It is the default and the calibrated one:
      every shipped explosion scene was authored against it. Sedov is the physically defensible model
      with unvalidated constants; Legacy is the tuned model with no conserved quantity. Until the
      calibration pass happens, that trade favours Legacy for authored content and Sedov for anything
      where you care that the ring you draw is the thing that pushed you.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="callout">
      <p><strong>The A/B to run.</strong> Rupture a vessel in an enclosed room with the Explosion panel
      open. On <em>Legacy</em>, watch the drawn ring and the debris: they diverge, and the outward gust
      is weak relative to the ring because the projection ate most of it. Switch <em>Blast Model</em> to
      <em>Sedov Front</em>: the ring now <em>is</em> the front, its radius visibly decelerates and then
      settles to a constant acoustic speed, and the wind reaches around obstacles because it went
      through the projection rather than around it. Sweep the single <em>Energy</em> knob and check that
      speed, heat, smoke and impulse all move together &#8212; on Legacy the equivalent sweep requires
      four sliders that do not agree.</p>
    </div>
  </section>


  <section id="contact-heat">
    <p class="note"><strong>2026-09-19 qualification:</strong> both hot and cold participants now
    respond, which fixes the one-way ratchet. However, each particle gathers a different normalized
    mixture; equal relaxation rates alone do not establish global energy conservation. The word
    “conserving” in this historical section should be read as the design intention. The
    <a href="model_audit_20260919.md#1-heating-phase-change-and-thermal-contact">current audit</a>
    gives a counterexample and separates this path from the new shared-face grid alternative.</p>
    <div class="section-top">
      <div class="colorbar heat"></div>
      <p class="eyebrow">Thermal &#183; two generations</p>
      <h2>A maximum is not a mixture</h2>
      <p class="lede">Two touching particles at different temperatures should end up somewhere between
      them. The legacy path implemented that with an <code>atomicMax</code> on a shared grid cell,
      which is a perfectly reasonable way to make a lock-free write and a completely unreasonable model
      of thermal contact &#8212; it has no way to express cooling, no way to add contributions, and no
      way to run backwards.</p>
    </div>

    <h3>The generations</h3>
    <table>
      <tr><th>Generation</th><th>What it did</th><th>The symptom you saw</th><th>Root cause</th></tr>
      <tr>
        <td class="name">Legacy ratchet<br><span class="b yellow">selectable</span></td>
        <td>SPH particles scatter an <code>atomicMax</code>'d temperature into a grid channel; MPM
        particles only ever <em>absorb</em> from it in g2p. A second, separate channel &#8212; also an
        <code>atomicMax</code>, of <code>2000 &#8722; T</code> &#8212; was bolted on later to allow
        cooling at all.</td>
        <td class="notes">Drop burning embers into water: the water heats, the embers never cool, and
        they keep igniting things. Ten warm particles in a cell read as a single warm particle. SPH
        itself never receives contact heat at all, in either direction.</td>
        <td class="notes">The audit called it "energy from nothing", severity 8. A maximum cannot
        conserve: contributions do not add, nothing can lower a cell (hence the second channel), and the
        exchange is structurally one-way, so a hot source heats an unlimited amount of cold material
        while staying exactly as hot as it started.</td>
      </tr>
      <tr>
        <td class="name">Contact Heat v2<br><span class="b green">default</span></td>
        <td>A conserving <strong>mixture grid</strong>. Two fixed-point accumulators hold
        &#8721;w&#183;mC and &#8721;w&#183;mC&#183;T. <em>Every</em> thermal particle, SPH and MPM alike,
        deposits with <code>atomicAdd</code> once per frame; then each particle relaxes toward
        T<sub>avg</sub> = num/den at 0.45/s in its own dedicated pass. Water carries 2&#215; thermal
        mass. Impact heating still injects as genuinely generated energy, numerator only. The legacy g2p
        ratchet consume is disabled under v2 so heat is not applied twice.</td>
        <td class="notes">Current, and default-on since 2026-07-27. Embers visibly quench while the
        water warms; a pile of warm particles reads hotter than one.</td>
        <td class="notes">Relaxing every participant toward the common mass-weighted mean at roughly
        equal rates conserves energy in aggregate, which is the property the ratchet could not have at
        any threshold setting.</td>
      </tr>

    <div class="grid-2">
      <div class="panel">
        <h3>Why v2 is good enough</h3>
        <p>It replaces a comparison with an average, which is the entire content of the fix. Once the
        cell holds &#8721;w&#183;mC and &#8721;w&#183;mC&#183;T instead of a maximum, three separate
        symptoms stop being symptoms: contributions add, so a crowd is hotter than an individual; the
        mean can be below a participant, so hot things cool; and water having twice the thermal mass
        makes quenching fall out of the same arithmetic instead of needing its own quench channel.
        Impact heating is correctly left as a numerator-only deposit, because kinetic-to-thermal really
        is generated energy and should not be conserved against the mixture.</p>
        <p>It cost two dedicated passes rather than an edit to the existing shaders, because both g2p
        and the SPH force shader sit exactly at the driver's SSBO-block limit and adding two buffers
        fails to link at all. That is the same wall the fracture and coupling work hit, and it is worth
        internalising: in this codebase, new shared state usually means a new small pass.</p>
        <p>Measured cost: no measurable frame-time change over 15&#160;s forced on, about
        7&#160;ms/frame.</p>
      </div>
      <div class="panel">
        <h3>What is still wrong</h3>
        <ul class="bullet-list">
          <li><strong>The SDF-wall and air-injection heat paths are still one-way</strong>, explicitly
          deferred. A hot wall still heats a particle without cooling; that half of the audit finding is
          open.</li>
          <li><strong>The relaxation rate is a single global constant</strong> (0.45/s) that stands in
          for every real contact conductance. Two materials with wildly different conductivity exchange
          at the same rate.</li>
          <li><strong>Conservation is aggregate, not exact.</strong> "Roughly equal rates keep the
          exchange near-conserving" is the actual claim; nothing enforces a balance per pair, and the
          fixed-point accumulators quantize.</li>
          <li><strong>The deposit is once per frame, the relax is its own pass</strong>, so the
          effective exchange rate has a mild dependence on how the frame is subdivided.</li>
          <li><strong>The sound-field SDF diffusion is untouched</strong> and remains a separate thermal
          path with its own semantics.</li>
        </ul>
      </div>
    </div>

    <div class="note">
      <p><strong>When you would still pick the legacy ratchet.</strong> Only two reasons. It is the A/B
      that makes the conservation argument visible, and it is the behaviour every pre-July scene was
      authored against &#8212; a scene tuned so that embers stay hot forever will read very differently
      when the embers start quenching. If an old fire scene suddenly goes out, this toggle is the first
      thing to check.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="callout">
      <p><strong>The A/B to run.</strong> Drop burning embers into a pool of SPH water. With
      <em>Contact Heat v2</em> on (default) the embers visibly cool and go out while the water warms;
      turn it off and the embers heat the water, stay exactly as hot as they were, and keep igniting
      whatever they touch. Second test, for the summing property: put one warm particle against a cold
      block, then ten. Under v2 the ten drive the block hotter; under the ratchet they read identically
      to one, because a maximum ignores the other nine.</p>
    </div>

  <section id="burn">
    <div class="section-top">
      <div class="colorbar heat"></div>
      <p class="eyebrow">Fire &#183; two generations</p>
      <h2>Why the burning wavefront never read as a front</h2>
      <p class="lede">Fire spreading through a solid should look like a narrow advancing band with cool
      spent material behind it. It looked instead like a whole object turning orange at once, and no
      amount of water would put it out. Five separate mechanisms were responsible, and one of them made
      fire <em>physically</em> inextinguishable rather than merely hard to extinguish.</p>
    </div>

    <h3>The generations</h3>
    <table>
      <tr><th>Generation</th><th>What it did</th><th>The symptom you saw</th><th>Root cause</th></tr>
      <tr>
        <td class="name">Legacy<br><span class="b yellow">selectable</span></td>
        <td>Deterministic ignition above a fixed temperature, exothermic self-heating, and &#8212; the
        decisive line &#8212; a <strong>temperature floor</strong> applied to any burning particle:
        <code>temp = max(temp, 505 + &#8230;)</code>, with ember ash clamped to 410&#160;K forever.</td>
        <td class="notes">Ignite one end of a wood log row: the whole log lights up at once, there is no
        ragged edge, and the ash glows forever producing infinite smoke. Pouring water on it does
        nothing.</td>
        <td class="notes">Five stacked causes. (1) The floor <em>teleported</em> a barely-ignited
        particle at burn = 0.011 straight to at least 505&#160;K, so every touched particle instantly
        became a full-power heat source &#8212; smearing the front &#8212; and because the floor is
        re-applied every frame, no amount of cooling could ever reduce the temperature. Fire was
        unextinguishable by construction, not by tuning. (2) Ignition was fully deterministic, so whole
        bands lit in lockstep. (3) Burn duration far exceeded layer-to-layer conduction lag, so the
        whole object burned as one phase. (4) A one-way preheat ratchet let fuel absorb heat ten times
        faster than it shed it, creeping entire bodies to ignition. (5) Ash was clamped hot forever: an
        eternal pilot light.</td>
      </tr>
      <tr>
        <td class="name">Burn Front v2<br><span class="b green">default</span></td>
        <td>Bounded exothermic self-heating gated by a <code>sustain</code> ramp instead of a floor, so
        active cooling can smother a flame; per-particle ignition jitter so neighbours do not light in
        lockstep; 2.2&#215; default burn-through so the front is thin with a trailing char edge; the
        preheat ratchet softened 0.1 to 0.45; ash cools to near ambient. Shipped with
        <em>Water Quench</em>, a cold-contact grid channel that lets cool SPH liquid pull grid
        temperature down hard.</td>
        <td class="notes">Current and default-on. A visible advancing front with a ragged edge and
        burnt-out cool ash behind it; water puts fire out.</td>
        <td class="notes">Replacing a clamp with a gate is the whole structural change: a clamp is
        unconditional and destroys information, a gate multiplies and leaves the cooling term able to
        win.</td>
      </tr>
    </table>

    <div class="grid-2">
      <div class="panel">
        <h3>Why v2 is good enough</h3>
        <p>Because the floor was not a tuning parameter, it was a <em>lower bound on the state
        variable</em>, and no other subsystem could beat it. Nothing about a heat-conduction term, a
        water quench, an air-cooling term or an oxygen gate matters if the last thing that runs sets the
        temperature back to 505&#160;K. The replacement &#8212; a <code>smoothstep(330, 380)</code>
        sustain multiplier on the self-heating &#8212; keeps the property the floor was there to provide
        (a burning particle resists ordinary grid cooling) while restoring the property it destroyed
        (enough cooling wins).</p>
        <p>The other four fixes are each small and each removes a distinct reason the front smeared. The
        one worth calling out is jitter: a deterministic threshold on a regular particle lattice is a
        <em>synchroniser</em>, and any spatial randomness at all breaks the lockstep. Water Quench is
        the complementary half &#8212; the legacy SPH-to-MPM heat exchange was strictly hot-to-cold via
        <code>atomicMax</code>, so water had no channel through which to cool anything, which is the
        same defect the contact-heat family fixed properly a fortnight later.</p>
      </div>
      <div class="panel">
        <h3>What is still wrong</h3>
        <ul class="bullet-list">
          <li><strong>Water Quench is now partly redundant.</strong> Contact Heat v2 makes cold water
          quench <em>natively</em> through the mixture mean. Two mechanisms now cool with water, both
          default-on, and nothing reconciles them.</li>
          <li><strong>The sustain thresholds are global constants</strong> (330 to 380&#160;K for
          burning material, 320 to 370 for embers), not per-material. A material that should smoulder
          at low temperature and one that should not are gated identically.</li>
          <li><strong>Burn-through speed is a look knob, not a fuel property.</strong> The 2.2&#215;
          default multiplies every material's burn rate; per-material burn duration remains
          hand-authored.</li>
          <li><strong>Ignition jitter is a hash of the particle index</strong>, so it is spatially
          uncorrelated. Real flame fronts are rough at a length scale; this is rough at the particle
          scale.</li>
          <li><strong>It interacts with the later fire oxygen chemistry and structural fire
          damage</strong>, neither of which was re-tuned against v2's thinner front.</li>
        </ul>
      </div>
    </div>

    <div class="note">
      <p><strong>When you would still pick Legacy.</strong> One legitimate use: a scene that wants
      guaranteed, unstoppable, decorative fire &#8212; a permanently burning brazier, a torch that must
      not go out when a splash lands on it. The legacy floor gives you exactly that, for free, and there
      is no "unquenchable" flag in v2 to reproduce it. It is also the A/B that demonstrates why a clamp
      on a state variable is a different kind of bug from a badly chosen constant.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="callout">
      <p><strong>The A/B to run.</strong> Lay a row of wood logs and ignite one end. With
      <em>Burn Front v2</em> on you should see an advancing band with a ragged leading edge and cool
      dark ash behind it; toggle it off and the whole row lights at once and the ash keeps glowing and
      smoking indefinitely. Then pour SPH water on burning material: with v2 and Water Quench on the
      fire goes out; with Water Quench off the water does nothing at all. Turn Water Quench off but
      leave Contact Heat v2 on to see the newer, more general quench path working on its own.</p>
    </div>
  </section>


  <section id="coupling">
    <div class="section-top">
      <div class="colorbar sph"></div>
      <p class="eyebrow">MPM &#8596; SPH &#183; two generations</p>
      <h2>A contact that teleported, and a force that only pointed one way</h2>
      <p class="lede">The MPM and SPH solvers share a world but not a pressure solve, so they meet
      through a contact model. That model had two defects, one per direction: it moved positions
      instead of applying forces, and it never applied any force back at all.</p>
    </div>

    <h3>The generations</h3>
    <table>
      <tr><th>Generation</th><th>What it did</th><th>The symptom you saw</th><th>Root cause</th></tr>
      <tr>
        <td class="name">Legacy one-way penalty<br><span class="b green">still default</span></td>
        <td>SPH particles that penetrate MPM material are pushed out by a fixed fraction of the grid
        spacing <em>per frame</em>, and their normal velocity is reflected with a stacked factor. MPM
        never sees SPH at all.</td>
        <td class="notes">Fluid slowly pops and jitters its way <em>out</em> of concave MPM pockets while
        resting in them. A water jet cannot move a sand pile. The same scene behaves differently at a
        different timestep or grid resolution.</td>
        <td class="notes">Two independent defects. (a) The correction <em>teleported</em> position: it
        was dt-independent and scaled with the grid spacing, so depenetration speed tracked dx and frame
        rate rather than time. The reflection factor summed to 1.05 + 1.30 + 0.75 = 3.10; since k = 1
        kills the normal velocity and k = 2 reflects it perfectly, k = 3.10 <em>reverses</em> it at
        about 2.1&#215; speed &#8212; resting contact injected kinetic energy. (b) The coupling was
        strictly one-way: SPH felt MPM as an immovable static collider, MPM felt nothing, and momentum
        was created from nothing every frame.</td>
      </tr>
      <tr>
        <td class="name">Coupling v2<br><span class="b yellow">selectable</span></td>
        <td>A <strong>passive</strong> response: k = 1 + e with e in [0,1], so it can never add energy,
        and a rate-based recovery in 1/s so behaviour is timestep-independent. Then the loop closes: the
        SPH force pass records the exact impulse each particle received from the contact and MPM receives
        its negative.</td>
        <td class="notes">Selectable, still off by default pending live calibration.</td>
        <td class="notes">Two hard constraints shaped it, and both are characteristic of this codebase.
        The SPH force shader sits <em>at</em> the 16-SSBO cap so it cannot bind the grid &#8212; the
        reaction rides an <code>imageBuffer</code> instead, which also avoids duplicating contact logic
        in a second shader that could drift. And SPH runs <em>after</em> the whole MPM step, which the
        clear pass wipes, so the impulse lands in persistent reaction grids and is consumed by the
        <em>next</em> step: a documented one-frame latency, the same order of accuracy as the rest of the
        explicit scheme.</td>
      </tr>
    </table>

    <div class="grid-2">
      <div class="panel">
        <h3>Why v2 is the right model</h3>
        <p>Restitution above 1 is not a tuning choice, it is a sign error in disguise: any resting
        contact with k &gt; 2 is a pump. Clamping k to 1 + e with e &#8804; 1 makes the response passive
        by construction, which is a property rather than a setting &#8212; you cannot tune your way back
        into the bug. Making recovery a rate in 1/s rather than a fraction of dx per frame is the same
        kind of change: it removes dx and frame rate from a physical quantity's units.</p>
        <p>The back-reaction is the part that makes it a coupling rather than a boundary condition.
        Recording the impulse <em>at the one site where the contact force is actually known</em>, rather
        than recomputing it in the scatter pass, is a deliberate anti-drift decision worth copying.</p>
        <p>The verification is the most instructive part of this entry. Forcing it on with a temporary
        GPU readback caught a bug that a clean build and a no-crash run would both have missed: the
        consume-and-zero sat <em>after</em> grid_op's empty-cell early-out, but SPH deposits with
        B-spline weights and therefore reaches nodes carrying no MPM mass. Those cells were never
        cleared and the fixed-point accumulator climbed monotonically, 0.6 to 126 over 25&#160;s
        &#8212; int32 wraparound in roughly seven minutes, and a phantom kick of minutes-worth of stale
        impulse for anything that later drifted in. Moving the read and clear above the early-out made
        it flat: about 140 cells at about 0.18 peak impulse, steady over 30&#160;s.</p>
      </div>
      <div class="panel">
        <h3>What is still wrong</h3>
        <ul class="bullet-list">
          <li><strong>It is not the default</strong>, because the live A/B is the calibration step and it
          has not been run: does a water jet now visibly move a sand pile, and does fluid stop climbing
          out of pockets?</li>
          <li><strong>One frame of latency</strong> on the back-reaction, by construction. Acceptable in
          an explicit scheme, but it means action and reaction are not simultaneous.</li>
          <li><strong>The applied velocity change is clamped to one cell per step</strong> so a
          near-empty node cannot produce an unbounded kick. That is a safety clamp, i.e. exactly the kind
          of compensator this page is generally suspicious of.</li>
          <li><strong>It is still a penalty contact, not a unified pressure solve.</strong> The
          relationship map's own wording remains accurate: soft coupling for contact and thermal
          exchange. Two-way momentum makes it honest, not unified.</li>
          <li><strong>SDF geometry still receives no momentum back</strong> from either solver. That
          one-way arrow is a separate, still-open item.</li>
        </ul>
      </div>
    </div>

    <div class="note">
      <p><strong>When you would still pick Legacy.</strong> Default-off here is enforced structurally
      &#8212; the diff across every touched shader and source is 111 lines added and <em>zero</em>
      removed, with the legacy contact block reproduced verbatim in the else branch &#8212; so Legacy is
      byte-identical and every existing scene is unaffected. Keep it for any scene where SPH is meant to
      act as a decorative fluid over static terrain and you do not want a water jet redistributing your
      carefully placed sand. Switch to v2 when the interaction is the point.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="callout">
      <p><strong>The A/B to run.</strong> Two tests, one per defect. (1) <em>Energy:</em> rest a pool of
      SPH fluid in a concave MPM bowl and leave it alone. On Legacy it jitters and slowly climbs out; on
      v2 it sits. (2) <em>Momentum:</em> aim a water jet at a loose sand pile. On Legacy the sand does
      not move at all no matter how hard you push, because MPM never receives the force; on v2 the pile
      erodes. Then change the timestep or grid resolution and repeat test 1: Legacy's depenetration speed
      changes with both, v2's does not.</p>
    </div>
  </section>
