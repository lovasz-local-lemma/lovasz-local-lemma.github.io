Going deeper

The first page covered the spine of the engine: shape, modes, sound. This one covers the parts that sit around it — how the sound field in the air is drawn, what the air trapped inside an instrument does, and what changes when you leave flat 2D behind. Same rule as before: every demo computes for real.

01Seeing sound in the air

The mesh tells you how the object moves. The next question is what that motion does to the air around it — and unlike the object, the air is not meshed at all.

Every point on the surface is a little speaker

Christiaan Huygens had the idea in 1678: treat every point of a disturbance as the source of its own expanding ripple, then add all the ripples up. Whatever pattern emerges is the wave. It sounds too simple to work, and it works remarkably well.

So the viewer takes the boundary of the shape, notes how fast each boundary point is moving outward, and treats each one as a tiny source. For any pixel of empty space it adds up the contribution of every source — delayed by how long the sound took to travel there.

pressure at a point = SUM over boundary points of (how hard it pushes) × (a ripple delayed by distance/speed) × (spreading) × (facing you?) Four factors. The delay is what creates interference; the "facing you" factor stops the back of the surface from radiating forward through the material.

Interference does the rest. Two sources whose ripples arrive in step reinforce; arrive half a cycle apart and they cancel. That is the entire origin of the lobes, beams and quiet zones you see in the medium view.

A row of sources along the bottom, each emitting the same tone. The field is computed by summing every source at every pixel — exactly the calculation the viewer does on the GPU. Widen the array or raise the frequency and watch a smooth bubble collapse into sharp beams.

—

Set curvature away from zero and the sources delay each other into a lens: the beam focuses to a point. That is a phased array, and it is the same maths.

The trick that makes all the other views possible

Here is the neat part. If everything is ringing at one frequency, then every point in the air is doing the same thing: going round and round a circle at that frequency. The only thing that differs from point to point is how big its circle is and where on the circle it currently is.

Two numbers per point. Size and angle. And once you store those two numbers, you no longer need to store time at all — you can rebuild the pressure at any instant, past or future, instantly. That pair is called a phasor.

pressure(x, t) = size(x) × cos( omega·t + angle(x) ) Store size and angle once; get every moment for free. This is why the viewer can show a frozen interference pattern, or a whole slab of time, at no extra cost.
DEMO

One phasor, every instant

Left: a point in the air, drawn as an arrow going round a circle. Right: the pressure that arrow traces out over time. The arrow is the stored data; the wave is what you hear. Nothing is being simulated forward — the wave is read straight off the rotation.

Drag "angle" and the whole wave slides in time without changing shape. That is all a phase is: a head start.

02Nine ways to look at the same field

Once the field is stored as phasors, you can ask it questions that a plain animation cannot answer. The viewer's Field Lab offers nine; here is what each one means.

The animated blue-and-red view is the obvious one, but it is also the least informative: everything is moving, so nothing stands still long enough to be read. The other views trade the animation for insight.

The strangest one: places where phase gives up

If the size of a point's circle shrinks to exactly zero, then its angle is meaningless — there is no direction to point. These are phase singularities, and they are not rare accidents. In any field made of a few interfering waves they appear naturally, as isolated points where all the colours of the phase wheel meet.

Circle one and the phase runs through a full turn. Energy does not just sit there either: it orbits the singularity. That is what the Vorticity view finds — genuine whirlpools in the flow of sound energy. They are a real, studied phenomenon (acoustic vortices), not a rendering artifact.

A note on honesty: this whole picture assumes one single frequency, and ignores reflections, obstacles and the object getting in the way of its own sound. It is a intuition-builder, not an acoustics solver. The real engine says the same thing in its own documentation.

03Trapped air: the cavity

A guitar is not a vibrating plate. It is a vibrating plate wrapped around a box of air — and that air is an instrument in its own right.

Air is a spring made of nothing

Blow across a bottle and you get a note. Nothing solid is vibrating: the plug of air in the neck is sloshing in and out, and the air in the body is being squashed and released. The plug is the mass, the trapped body of air is the spring. It is the simplest oscillator there is, made purely of air.

bigger body → softer spring → lower note longer neck → heavier plug → lower note This is a Helmholtz resonator. Drink from the bottle, the body gets bigger, the note drops — you have heard this experiment many times.

Bigger cavities have modes, just like plates

Once the box is large compared to the wavelength, it stops being one simple oscillator and starts having standing waves — the same story as the string, but in pressure instead of displacement. A closed box of length L has a lowest air mode at roughly speed of sound / 2L, then multiples of it.

The engine handles this with the same machinery as the structure. It meshes the air, builds a stiffness and a mass matrix for pressure instead of displacement, and solves the very same eigenproblem. Air modes come out looking exactly like string modes — because mathematically they are.

When the two systems talk to each other

Now the interesting bit. The wooden top pushes the air; the pressurised air pushes back on the wooden top. Neither is independent any more — they are coupled, and you have to solve them together.

And when you do, something familiar happens. If you tune the air resonance close to a plate resonance, the two do not simply coincide. They repel: one moves down, one moves up, and both become a blend of wood-motion and air-motion. It is the avoided crossing from the first page, turning up again in a completely different guise.

This is instrument design, not trivia. Luthiers deliberately place the guitar's air resonance relative to the top-plate resonance, because the split pair is what fills out the low end. Move the soundhole and you move the air mode — and the coupling changes the whole bottom octave of the instrument.

04Leaving flat 2D behind

Everything so far assumed a flat sheet moving in its own plane. Real objects are curved, thick, and three-dimensional — and each step up costs something.

What "degrees of freedom" really buys you

The size of the problem is set by how many numbers you need per node to describe the motion. That number is the difference between a membrane and a bell.

ModelNumbers per nodeWhat it can doCost
Membrane2 — slide in x, ya drum skin; stretches but cannot bendcheapest
Plate3 — lift, plus two tiltsproper bending stiffness; a cymbal, a barmodest
Shell5 — slide, lift, and tiltcurvature, which couples stretching to bendinghigher
Solid3 — but every node is in 3Dreal volume; thick, chunky objectsfar more nodes

The jump to a solid is brutal not because 3 is a big number but because filling a volume with elements needs vastly more of them than covering a surface. Double the detail on a 2D mesh and you get four times the nodes; do it in 3D and you get eight. The eigensolve grows faster still. That is why the 3D solids in the app are coarse and marked experimental.

Why curving a sheet makes it stiff

Take a flat sheet of paper and it flops. Curve it slightly — a taco, a bent business card — and suddenly it holds itself up. Nothing about the material changed.

The reason: on a curved surface you cannot bend without also stretching. Bending is easy, stretching is enormously harder, and curvature welds the two together so the easy motion has to drag the hard one along. That coupling is exactly what a shell model adds over a plate, and it is why a curved cymbal rings so differently from a flat disc of the same metal.

This is why a curved shell needs five numbers per node: the in-plane sliding and the out-of-plane lifting can no longer be treated as separate problems.

05Time as an extra dimension

The last idea is the one that sounds most abstract and turns out to be the most visual: stop animating time, and draw it as an axis instead.

An animation shows you one instant, then throws it away. If instead you stack every instant into a solid block — two dimensions of space, one of time — you can see the whole history at once. A photograph of a wave becomes a sculpture of a wave.

What was moving becomes something you can read off a shape:

A standing wave

becomes a set of straight vertical columns — the still points never move, so they carve stationary lines through the whole block.

A travelling wave

becomes a slanted sheet. The slope of the slant is the wave speed: shallow means fast.

A decaying note

becomes a block that fades along its length, so you can see the decay envelope directly rather than waiting for it.

Two frequencies

produce a beating pattern that shows up as regular bulges up the time axis — obvious in the block, easy to miss live.

And because the field is stored as phasors, this costs nothing. The viewer does not record frames; it simply evaluates the same two stored numbers at whatever time each layer needs. Time becomes a coordinate you can look along rather than a thing you wait for.

DEMO

A slab of time

The same 1D wave shown two ways: animated on the left, and stacked into a space-time block on the right (space across, time downward). Switch between a standing and a travelling wave and watch the block change character completely.

—

Standing: vertical stripes, fixed nodes. Travelling: diagonal stripes whose slope is the speed. Same maths, completely different picture.

Where this shows up in the app: the Field Lab's "Spacetime" view builds exactly this block out of the live phasor field, with sliders for how much time to include and how far to tilt the stack. It is the one view that shows you history rather than a moment.

06Why the real thing is harder than this page

Everything you have played with so far quietly cheated. Not in the physics — the equations are right — but in the shapes. And that one cheat is the entire reason finite elements exist.

The cheat

Every demo here used a string, a rectangle, or a disc. Those three are not a random selection: they are essentially the complete list of shapes whose modes can be written down as a formula. A string gives sines. A rectangle gives products of sines. A disc gives Bessel functions. Pick literally anything else — a guitar body, a bell, a bar with a notch filed in it, a rectangle with a hole — and no formula exists. Not "is hard to find". Does not exist.

That is the wall. And the way through it is to stop looking for a formula and start approximating: chop the shape into thousands of pieces so simple that each one does have a formula, then let the computer stitch them back together. That is the finite element method, and it is why this project has one.

DEMO

Where the formulas run out

Drag the slider to deform a perfect circle. At zero, the modes are known exactly — textbook Bessel functions. Move it even slightly and every closed form evaporates, while the meshing approach carries on without noticing.

Why the solver had to change

The eigensolver on the first page is real, and it is genuinely the algorithm Jacobi published in 1846. It is also the wrong tool at scale. It works on a dense matrix and costs roughly the cube of the size: 36 unknowns is instant, 600 is a pause, and a few thousand is hopeless — and it computes every mode when we only ever wanted the lowest few dozen.

The engine therefore does three things differently. It keeps the matrices sparse, since a node only touches its neighbours and storing the zeros would be absurd. It asks only for the lowest modes, using a shift that turns the smallest, most awkward frequencies into the largest and easiest ones. And it pushes the dense work onto the GPU when the problem is small enough to fit.

None of this changes the story you already know. Shape becomes matrices, matrices become modes, modes become sound. Everything on the first page is still exactly what the engine does — the difference is that it does it for shapes nobody has a formula for, fast enough that you can drag a slider and hear the result change.
