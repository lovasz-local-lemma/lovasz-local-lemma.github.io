<section id="recipes">
  <h2><span class="num">01</span> Recipes</h2>

  <p>
    Concrete walkthroughs. Each one assumes you've started the viewer and
    are in Play mode. Step counts in the headers are rough — your hands
    move faster than reading does.
  </p>

  <div class="recipe" id="r-first">
    <div class="recipe-head">
      <div class="title">First instrument: tune 8 notes</div>
      <div class="meta">~ 5 min</div>
    </div>
    <ol>
      <li>Toolbar: switch from <strong>Play</strong> to <strong>Create</strong>.</li>
      <li>Pick a shape from the <strong>Component</strong> combo. Start with <code>Box-2s</code> — well-behaved, gives clean fundamentals.</li>
      <li>Drag <strong>Oct:</strong> to pick the octave you want (e.g. <code>C4</code> for middle C).</li>
      <li>Click the <strong>C</strong> note button — that's the first target.</li>
      <li>Click <strong>Auto</strong> to make the tuner auto-strike between iterations.</li>
      <li>Click <strong>Fine-W</strong>. The strike button glows red — strike the shape once. The tuner then iterates on its own.</li>
      <li>When Fine-W converges, it auto-advances to D, then E, F, G, A, B, C&prime;.</li>
      <li>When all 8 notes are green, click <strong>Save</strong>, then switch to <strong>Test</strong> to play your instrument.</li>
    </ol>
    <div class="why">
      Fine-W binary-searches the half-width until the fundamental matches the
      target. <code>Auto</code> automates strike + advance so you can do all
      8 notes hands-free. HF acceleration (on by default) typically converges
      each note in 2–3 solves.
    </div>
  </div>

  <div class="recipe" id="r-discover">
    <div class="recipe-head">
      <div class="title">Explore: see what notes a shape produces</div>
      <div class="meta">~ 3 min</div>
    </div>
    <ol>
      <li>Switch to <strong>Discover</strong>. The chromatic grid appears in the right panel.</li>
      <li>Set <strong>Lo / Hi</strong> octave drag-ints to span the range you care about (default C0–C9).</li>
      <li>Drag <strong>W / H / Shell</strong> sliders. Strike the shape after each change.</li>
      <li>If the detected freq lands within <strong>threshold</strong> cents of a chromatic note, the cell auto-fills.</li>
      <li>For bulk coverage, click <strong>Auto Explore</strong>: pick <em>1D</em>, <em>Size</em>, or <em>2D</em>, then how many steps per axis (2–16). The tuner sweeps the bracket and records every clean hit.</li>
      <li>Hover a filled cell to see <code>W</code>, <code>H</code>, freq, and cents-to-note. Click any filled cell to load and play that shape.</li>
    </ol>
    <div class="why">
      Discover is shape-first: it doesn't try to hit a specific note, it
      records whatever notes happen to fall within threshold of a real
      chromatic. Pair the chromatic grid with the <em>scatter</em> view on
      the right — the latter shows the (W, H) coordinates of every clean hit.
    </div>
  </div>

  <div class="recipe" id="r-refine">
    <div class="recipe-head">
      <div class="title">Refine a discovered note to ±5 cents</div>
      <div class="meta">~ 30 s</div>
    </div>
    <ol>
      <li>Discover or Build a few notes first (see previous recipe).</li>
      <li>Switch to <strong>Refine</strong> mode. Same grid view, but cells are now <em>clickable</em> and start a Fine-W session for that cell's chromatic freq.</li>
      <li>Click any filled cell — the shape loads at its saved (W, H), Fine-W kicks in.</li>
      <li>When it converges, the cell updates with the refined values; the displayed cents tightens up.</li>
    </ol>
    <div class="why">
      Refine reuses Fine-W per cell, targeting the chromatic Hz the cell
      represents. With HF acceleration this typically takes 1–3 iterations
      because the starting shape is already close.
    </div>
  </div>

  <div class="recipe" id="r-bayes">
    <div class="recipe-head">
      <div class="title">When Bayes-W beats Fine-W</div>
      <div class="meta">~ 1 min</div>
    </div>
    <p>Use <strong>Bayes-W</strong> in these situations:</p>
    <ul>
      <li>Fine-W keeps "giving up" with a suboptimal-best message — the cost surface has a local minimum the binary search keeps falling into.</li>
      <li>The shape has multiple plausible modes near the target, and the loudest changes with W (which confuses Fine-W's bisection).</li>
      <li>You want to <em>map</em> the W-axis as a side effect; Bayes-W's 8 LHS seeds give you a near-uniform survey for free.</li>
    </ul>
    <ol>
      <li>In Create / Refine mode, instead of Fine-W click <strong>Bayes-W</strong>.</li>
      <li>Watch the GP heatmap in the Experimental tab populate. Green = low |cents| (good).</li>
      <li>Convergence: typically 8–15 samples. Falls back to "budget exhausted" with best-so-far if it can't get under threshold.</li>
    </ol>
    <div class="why">
      Bayes-W draws 8 Latin-hypercube seeds first, then Expected-Improvement
      picks the next sample based on the GP surrogate of the cost surface.
      Handles multi-modal cost surfaces that bisection gets stuck on.
    </div>
  </div>

  <div class="recipe" id="r-tension">
    <div class="recipe-head">
      <div class="title">Tune a string with realistic tension</div>
      <div class="meta">~ 2 min</div>
    </div>
    <ol>
      <li>Create mode → Component combo → <strong>String</strong>.</li>
      <li>Set <strong>Width</strong> = 0.15 (≈ 30 cm half-length).</li>
      <li>Set <strong>Height</strong> = 0.05 (controls beam cross-section: <code>beam_w = H · 0.01</code> = 0.5 mm, realistic).</li>
      <li>Open the <strong>post_process</strong> panel. Drag <strong>String tension</strong> from 0 to ~100 N. The shape re-solves on release.</li>
      <li>Strike it. At 100 N the fundamental should land around 80–150 Hz depending on material.</li>
      <li>Now run Fine-W on a chromatic target. Width still controls fundamental (longer = lower); tension shifts the whole spectrum.</li>
    </ol>
    <div class="why">
      Adding tension activates the geometric-stiffness term <code>K_geom = T/L · ...</code>
      on every beam edge. The fundamental moves toward
      <code>(1/2L)·√(T/μ)</code> — pure-string physics. Without tension the
      string only has bending stiffness, which makes for a stiff piano-wire-
      without-tension sound rather than a guitar string.
    </div>
  </div>

  <div class="recipe" id="r-cavity">
    <div class="recipe-head">
      <div class="title">Build a resonance-cavity instrument</div>
      <div class="meta">~ 3 min</div>
    </div>
    <ol>
      <li>Create mode → Component combo → <strong>Strung</strong>. You get a hard-frame / soft-panel cavity with a steel string + bridge above the top panel. Material legend shows three regions.</li>
      <li>Open <strong>post_process</strong>. Set <strong>String tension</strong> to 80 N.</li>
      <li>Strike the string. The top panel rings via the bridge — soft panels radiate stronger than the hard frame.</li>
      <li>Now go to the <strong>Experimental</strong> tab → <strong>Air cavity</strong>. Toggle on, set freq ≈ 120 Hz, Q ≈ 12, gain ≈ 0.6.</li>
      <li>Strike again. The bandpass adds a body resonance around 120 Hz — the audible "boom" of a small cavity.</li>
    </ol>
    <div class="why">
      Joint modes between string, frame, and soft panels do most of the
      work — the frame defines the structural skeleton, the panels radiate.
      The air-cavity bandpass approximates the Helmholtz resonance the
      enclosed air would have if we modelled it as a separate FEM domain
      (currently lumped into a single parallel resonator).
    </div>
  </div>

  <div class="recipe" id="r-mass">
    <div class="recipe-head">
      <div class="title">Mass-spring scout → FEM commit</div>
      <div class="meta">~ 30 s</div>
    </div>
    <ol>
      <li>Create mode. Open the <strong>Experimental</strong> tab.</li>
      <li>Drag <strong>Width</strong> or <strong>Height</strong> sliders. The mass-spring preview thumbnails update in microseconds — every mode shape recomputes live.</li>
      <li>When you see a shape with the mode distribution you want, <em>release</em> the slider. That fires the full FEM solve.</li>
      <li>FEM thumbnails appear next to the mass-spring ones (A/B view). Compare topology directly.</li>
    </ol>
    <div class="why">
      The mass-spring lattice runs Jacobi eigensolve on a 10×6 grid (~96 DOFs)
      in microseconds. Absolute frequencies drift 2–5× from FEM, but mode
      shape <em>ordering</em> matches. Use it to navigate parameter space at
      cursor-speed, commit to FEM only when the topology looks right.
    </div>
  </div>

  <div class="recipe" id="r-manual">
    <div class="recipe-head">
      <div class="title">Manual-W: track a specific overtone</div>
      <div class="meta">~ 1 min</div>
    </div>
    <ol>
      <li>Create or Refine mode. Strike the shape so the spectrum has visible bars.</li>
      <li><em>Left-click</em> the spectrum bar of the mode you want to tune. A glowing "Mode N pinned" banner appears.</li>
      <li>Click <strong>Manual-W</strong>. The binary search now targets the pinned mode's frequency, not the fundamental.</li>
      <li>As the shape changes, the white arrow follows the pinned mode automatically (via MAC mode-shape correlation). The arrow's tint colour-codes confidence: green ≥ 0.60, yellow ≥ 0.30, red below.</li>
      <li>Red arrow = mode identity changed too much. Re-click a bar to re-pin.</li>
      <li><em>Right-click</em> in the spectrum to clear the pin. Manual-W then falls through to fundamental tracking (same as Fine-W).</li>
    </ol>
    <div class="why">
      Bell-like shapes often have overtones the user finds more "in tune"
      than the fundamental. MAC tracks the mode you picked via eigenvector
      correlation, not frequency proximity — so it survives mode crossings
      that pure-frequency tracking would lose.
    </div>
  </div>
</section>

  <h2><span class="num">02</span> Keyboard shortcuts</h2>

  <h3>Global</h3>
  <table>
    <tr><th style="width:140px">Key</th><th>What</th></tr>
    <tr><td><kbd>Esc</kbd></td>
        <td>Cascade-cancel: fine-tune → draw-mode → create/test → no-op. Held doesn't double-fire (edge-detected).</td></tr>
    <tr><td><kbd>Space</kbd></td>
        <td>Clear all modal energy (silence). Useful when something rings forever.</td></tr>
    <tr><td><kbd>D</kbd></td>
        <td>Toggle polygon-draw mode. Click vertices, press <kbd>Enter</kbd> to triangulate. Disabled while solving.</td></tr>
    <tr><td><kbd>S</kbd></td>
        <td>Cycle slow-motion: realtime → 20× slow → 200× → 5000× → realtime.</td></tr>
    <tr><td><kbd>C</kbd></td>
        <td>Toggle Chladni mode — viewport shows the nodal pattern of the selected mode instead of live oscillation.</td></tr>
    <tr><td><kbd>V</kbd></td>
        <td>Toggle 2D / 3D view of the mesh.</td></tr>
    <tr><td><kbd>M</kbd></td>
        <td>Toggle medium-propagation visualization (acoustic waves around the shape).</td></tr>
    <tr><td><kbd>P</kbd></td>
        <td>Toggle plate-bending mode (DKT elements). Forces a re-solve.</td></tr>
    <tr><td><kbd>B</kbd></td>
        <td>Toggle boundary-beam stiffeners along the mesh outline.</td></tr>
    <tr><td><kbd>Home</kbd></td>
        <td>Reset camera (zoom = 1, pan = 0, 3D orbit reset).</td></tr>
  </table>

<section id="statemachine">
  <h2><span class="num">03</span> Fine-tune state machine</h2>

  <p>
    Every fine-tune mode (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9) shares the same
    <code>fine_phase_</code> state machine. The pick-next logic varies per
    mode; the phase transitions are universal.
  </p>

  <div class="state-diagram">  <span class="label">fine_mode_ = 0</span>  (idle)
       │
       │  user clicks Fine-W / Bayes-W / etc.
       <span class="arrow">▼</span>
  <span class="label">fine_mode_ = M</span>, <span class="label">fine_phase_ = 1</span>   (WAIT for strike)
       │
       │  user strikes the shape  ─ or ─  auto-skip if Auto mode + has prior strike
       <span class="arrow">▼</span>
  <span class="label">fine_phase_ = 2</span>   (build component + start solve)
       │
       │  solve_worker runs in background
       <span class="arrow">▼</span>
  <span class="label">fine_phase_ = 3</span>   (wait for audio settle, ~20 frames)
       │
       <span class="arrow">▼</span>
  <span class="label">fine_phase_ = 4</span>   (READ detected freq, decide next)
       │
       ├──── |cents| &lt; threshold ─── <span class="term">CONVERGED → fine_mode_ = 0</span>
       ├──── iter cap exceeded   ─── <span class="term">SUBOPTIMAL → fine_mode_ = 0</span>
       └──── otherwise:
              pick next param via mode-specific picker
              update tuning_width_ / tuning_height_
              fine_phase_ = 2  (back to solve)
</div>

  <p>
    The <strong>pickers</strong> vary by mode:
  </p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Mode 1, 6, 9</strong>: bisection mid-point, or HF Newton step when available.</li>
    <li><strong>Mode 2</strong>: bisection on a uniform scale factor.</li>
    <li><strong>Mode 3</strong>: alternating W/H bisections per round.</li>
    <li><strong>Mode 4</strong>: adaptive sampler scores bracket / near-best / gap regions, picks highest.</li>
    <li><strong>Mode 5</strong>: sweep then per-bracket bisection.</li>
    <li><strong>Mode 7, 8</strong>: GP fit + Expected-Improvement argmax on a fine grid.</li>
  </ul>
</section>

<section id="dataflow">
  <h2><span class="num">04</span> Threading &amp; data flow</h2>

  <p>
    Three threads operate concurrently. Mutexes guard the small set of shared
    state; the rest is single-owner.
  </p>

  <h3>Render thread (60 Hz)</h3>
  <ol>
    <li>Process input (keyboard, mouse).</li>
    <li>Drain solve worker result if ready: <code>modal_res_ = solve_worker_.take_result()</code>.</li>
    <li>Apply per-frame state pushes (drift, damping, sympathetic config, gravity).</li>
    <li>Read displacements via <code>modal_get_displacements</code>.</li>
    <li>Write displacement VBO → draw mesh (OpenGL).</li>
    <li>ImGui begin → draw_layout (tabs, panels, viewport) → end.</li>
    <li>Swap buffers.</li>
  </ol>

  <h3>Solve worker (background thread)</h3>
  <ol>
    <li>Started by <code>solve_worker_.start(modal_obj_, num_modes)</code> from the render thread.</li>
    <li>Runs <code>modal_solve</code>: assemble K / M → eigensolve → build ModalResult.</li>
    <li>Result picked up by the next render frame via <code>is_done()</code>.</li>
    <li>Joined before any destroy of <code>modal_obj_</code> / <code>modal_res_</code> to avoid use-after-free.</li>
  </ol>

  <h3>Audio callback (PortAudio, ~5 ms blocks)</h3>
  <ol>
    <li>Drain strike events from libmodal's strike_buf, project onto modes via <code>compute_strike_impulse</code>.</li>
    <li>Run GPU synthesis (CUDA kernel) → PCM samples per mode → sum.</li>
    <li>Apply air-cavity bandpass (if enabled).</li>
    <li>Run noise gate (HP-filter envelope, smoothstep).</li>
    <li>Post-process: gain → low-pass → soft-clip → auto-normalize.</li>
    <li>Write to PortAudio output buffer.</li>
  </ol>

  <p>
    <strong>Strike flow</strong>: a click in the viewport calls
    <code>modal_strike(res, x, y, mag)</code>. That pushes to the audio
    strike_buf (drained by the audio callback) AND immediately injects into
    <code>vis_impulses</code> for visualisation (no audio latency).
  </p>
</section>

<!-- 6. GLOSSARY                                                    -->
<!-- ============================================================== -->
<section id="glossary">
  <h2><span class="num">06</span> Glossary</h2>

  <div class="glossary">
  <dl>
    <dt>Mode</dt><dd>A standing-wave pattern of vibration. The shape vibrates as a sum of decoupled modes; each has a frequency, a shape, and a decay rate.</dd>
    <dt>Eigenmode</dt><dd>Same thing as a mode. The "eigen" emphasises that it's the solution to the generalised eigenvalue problem <code>K φ = λ M φ</code>.</dd>
    <dt>K</dt><dd>Stiffness matrix. Encodes how much force a unit displacement at each DOF generates. Symmetric, sparse, positive-semi-definite (3 rigid-body modes give it a 3-dim nullspace before BCs).</dd>
    <dt>M</dt><dd>Mass matrix. Inertia per DOF. Diagonal-ish; we use the consistent form.</dd>
    <dt>ω (omega)</dt><dd>Angular frequency in rad/s. The audible frequency in Hz is <code>ω / (2π)</code>.</dd>
    <dt>ζ (zeta)</dt><dd>Damping ratio. Decay rate of a mode is <code>ζ · ω</code>; the e-folding time is <code>1 / (ζ·ω)</code>.</dd>
    <dt>α (alpha)</dt><dd>Radiation efficiency per mode. Computed from the modal velocity normal to the boundary and the medium's impedance. Controls how loud each mode is in the air.</dd>
    <dt>φ (phi)</dt><dd>Eigenvector. The mode's shape, stored as displacement per DOF. M-normalised so <code>φᵀ M φ = 1</code>.</dd>
    <dt>CST</dt><dd>Constant-Strain Triangle. The simplest 2D plane-stress finite element. 3 nodes × 2 DOFs = 6 DOFs per element.</dd>
    <dt>DKT</dt><dd>Discrete Kirchhoff Triangle. Plate-bending element with 3 DOFs/node (w, θx, θy). Produces out-of-plane modes.</dd>
    <dt>MAC</dt><dd>Modal Assurance Criterion. <code>|φ_a · φ_b|² / ((φ_a · φ_a)(φ_b · φ_b))</code>. 1 = identical shape, 0 = orthogonal. We use it for mode tracking across W changes in Manual-W.</dd>
    <dt>HF</dt><dd>Hellmann-Feynman theorem. Says <code>dλ/dp = φᵀ(dK/dp − λ dM/dp)φ</code> for an eigenpair. Gives us <code>df/dW</code> without doing a new eigensolve, used for Newton steps in Fine-W / Manual-W.</dd>
    <dt>K_geom</dt><dd>Geometric stiffness. Extra stiffness from pre-stress (tension, compression, gravity-induced strain). For a tensioned beam, scales as <code>T/L</code> and stiffens the transverse motion.</dd>
    <dt>GP / EI</dt><dd>Gaussian Process / Expected Improvement. The GP fits a surrogate over (W, H) → |cents|; EI picks the next sample by trading off predicted improvement vs uncertainty. Drives Bayes-W and Bayes-WH.</dd>
    <dt>LHS</dt><dd>Latin Hypercube Sampling. Better space coverage than uniform random for small sample counts. Used to seed Bayes-W / Bayes-WH before EI takes over.</dd>
    <dt>Lanczos</dt><dd>The sparse iterative eigensolver we use. Computes the lowest N eigenvalues of <code>(K, M)</code> without dense factorisation. Cheap for small N.</dd>
    <dt>Rayleigh quotient</dt><dd><code>R(x) = (xᵀ K x) / (xᵀ M x)</code>. Equals the eigenvalue when x is the eigenvector; stationary at eigenvectors. Used internally by HF for second-order-accurate FD of dλ/dW.</dd>
    <dt>Strike impulse</dt><dd>Per-mode amplitude added when you hit the shape. Computed as the eigenvector's value at the nearest node, projected onto the strike direction (boundary normal for boundary nodes; +Y for interior).</dd>
    <dt>Helmholtz mode</dt><dd>The lowest acoustic mode of an enclosed air volume connected to outside via a small opening. Has a simple closed form: <code>f = (c/2π)·√(A/(V·L))</code>. We currently model this as a single bandpass; full cavity-FEM is planned.</dd>
    <dt>Boundary clamp / fix_level</dt><dd>How many boundary nodes are pinned to zero displacement. 0 = free-free (rigid-body modes appear), 1–3 = partial, 4 = clamped all-around (membrane-only modes).</dd>
  </dl>
  </div>
</section>

<section id="troubleshooting">
  <h2><span class="num">07</span> Troubleshooting</h2>

  <div class="faq">
    <div class="q">Q · My instrument is silent / very quiet after a strike.</div>
    <div class="a">
      Check the noise gate: post_process panel, gate thresholds. If your shape
      produces a sub-bass-only mode, the gate's 80 Hz HP filter rejects it
      (intentionally — see commit history). Either raise the mode into audible
      range with the freq-drift slider, or temporarily disable the gate.
    </div>
  </div>

  <div class="faq">
    <div class="q">Q · A single mode keeps ringing forever after I strike.</div>
    <div class="a">
      Add damping: post_process panel → <strong>Damping</strong> slider. Pushed
      to libmodal in real time. For a hard cutoff, the <strong>Search timeout</strong>
      slider clears all modal energy N seconds after the last strike — but
      only during fine-tune sessions (Play / Test get infinite sustain by design).
    </div>
  </div>

  <div class="faq">
    <div class="q">Q · Fine-W keeps "giving up after N retries; best ±12 cents".</div>
    <div class="a">
      The bracket is converging to a non-target mode. Three things to try:
      (1) Switch to <strong>Bayes-W</strong> — its GP surrogate handles
      multi-modal cost surfaces. (2) Use <strong>Manual-W</strong>: click the
      spectrum bar of the mode you actually want. (3) Use
      <strong>Filter-W</strong> and dial in a decay window that excludes
      whichever mode is misleading the search.
    </div>
  </div>

  <div class="faq">
    <div class="q">Q · The viewport says "Solve failed (degenerate mesh)".</div>
    <div class="a">
      The triangulator produced a near-zero-area element, usually because
      W or H was pushed extreme. App auto-recovers to the last known-good
      W/H. Common cause: Fine-W+ adaptive sampler exploring a corner of the
      bracket. Lower the budget, or use Bayes-W which spaces samples better.
    </div>
  </div>

  <div class="faq">
    <div class="q">Q · The note label in Test mode says "C4" but the shape sounds like D.</div>
    <div class="a">
      Two possibilities. (1) The freq-drift slider is non-zero — playback is
      shifted by <code>2^(s/12)</code>. Either reset drift to 0 or enable
      <strong>comp</strong> next to it so future fine-tune sessions
      pre-compensate. (2) The instrument was tuned with the <em>flexible</em>
      toggle on; the saved freqs are correct but the chromatic labels reflect
      a relative octave, not absolute. Save with flexible off if you want
      absolute pitch labels.
    </div>
  </div>

  <div class="faq">
    <div class="q">Q · MAC arrow turns red after a few tune iterations.</div>
    <div class="a">
      The pinned mode's shape changed too much for the reference snapshot to
      match. Re-click the bar in the spectrum to re-pin (captures a new MAC
      reference). For very-wide tuning ranges, expect to re-pin once or twice
      mid-session.
    </div>
  </div>

  <div class="faq">
    <div class="q">Q · GP heatmap in Bayes-WH looks all-red / all-green.</div>
    <div class="a">
      Normal early in the session — the heatmap auto-scales lo/hi from the
      grid predictions, so before EI has any data the predictions are nearly
      uniform. After 4–6 samples you'll see structure emerge.
    </div>
  </div>

  <div class="faq">
    <div class="q">Q · I changed material but the mass-spring preview doesn't update.</div>
    <div class="a">
      It does, but the change is subtle if you only nudged E or rho slightly.
      The preview reads E and rho from the material panel each frame. For
      multi-material scenes the material panel is disabled and the preview
      shows the default mat — change comp_type or edit obj-&gt;mat directly
      to affect it.
    </div>
  </div>

  <div class="faq">
    <div class="q">Q · Audio cuts out for ~1 second when I change shape.</div>
    <div class="a">
      Expected: shape changes trigger a re-solve, which destroys the audio
      stream and rebuilds. There's an unavoidable gap of ~100 ms while the
      solver runs (longer for high mesh resolution). Lower mesh resolution
      in the Physics tab if the gap is too disruptive.
    </div>
  </div>

  <div class="faq">
    <div class="q">Q · The strung preset sounds dull / muddy regardless of tension.</div>
    <div class="a">
      The string's beam material defaults to whatever obj-&gt;mat was set to
      before the multi-material palette took over. With the material panel
      disabled, you can't edit obj-&gt;mat through the UI. Workaround: pick a
      stiffer single-material preset (e.g. Box-Hard) first, set material to
      Steel, then switch to Strung — obj-&gt;mat persists. Per-beam material
      assignment is the planned proper fix.
    </div>
  </div>
</section>

<!-- 8. BASICS: MODAL ANALYSIS FROM FIRST PRINCIPLES                -->
<!-- ============================================================== -->
<section id="math">
  <h2><span class="num">08</span> Basics: modal analysis from first principles</h2>

  <p>
    This section builds up from the simplest example (a plucked guitar
    string) to the framework the rest of the viewer uses. No prior
    knowledge of FEM or eigenvalues assumed — equations introduced as
    they become necessary, never before.
  </p>

  <h3>8.1 · What is modal analysis?</h3>

  <p>
    Pluck a guitar string. You hear a pitch (the fundamental), plus quieter
    higher pitches (overtones). The string isn't vibrating in just one
    shape — it's vibrating in many shapes at once, each with its own
    frequency. Each of those independent vibration patterns is a
    <strong>mode</strong>, and the observation that any motion of the
    string can be written as a sum of them is the heart of modal analysis.
  </p>

  <p>
    Why is this useful? Because each mode evolves independently. The
    fundamental does its thing, the second overtone does its thing, and
    they don't interfere. So if we can find the modes once and know how
    hard each one was struck, we can compute the entire future of the
    vibrating object by summing analytical sine waves — no iterative
    physics simulation needed.
  </p>

  <div class="note">
    <strong>Compared to other audio approaches:</strong>
    <em>Recorded samples</em> sound real but don't respond to where you
    strike. <em>Physical simulation</em> (time-stepping the wave equation)
    responds correctly but costs millions of operations per audio sample.
    <em>Modal synthesis</em> is the middle ground: one expensive solve
    upfront, then cheap sums forever after.
  </div>

  <h3>8.2 · Eigenmodes of a 1D string</h3>

  <p>
    The simplest case. A string of length <em>L</em>, tension <em>T</em>,
    and mass per length <em>μ</em>, pinned at both ends, has displacement
    <em>v(x, t)</em> obeying the wave equation:
  </p>
  <div class="eq">μ ∂²v/∂t² = T ∂²v/∂x²,   v(0, t) = v(L, t) = 0</div>

  <p>
    Try a separated-variables solution <code>v(x, t) = φ(x) · sin(ωt)</code>.
    Substituting gives
  </p>
  <div class="eq">T φ''(x) = −μ ω² φ(x)</div>
  <p>
    plus the boundary conditions <code>φ(0) = φ(L) = 0</code>. The only
    solutions are
  </p>
  <div class="eq">φ_n(x) = sin(n π x / L),   ω_n = (n π / L) √(T/μ),   n = 1, 2, 3, …</div>

  <p>
    Each <code>φ_n</code> is a <strong>mode shape</strong>; each
    <code>ω_n</code> is its <strong>natural angular frequency</strong>. The
    pair <code>(φ_n, ω_n)</code> is called an <em>eigenmode</em> because
    the equation above is an eigenvalue problem in disguise: <code>φ_n</code>
    is an eigenvector of the operator <code>−(T/μ) ∂²/∂x²</code> with
    eigenvalue <code>ω_n²</code>.
  </p>

  <p>
    The first three modes look like:
  </p>

  <div class="state-diagram">  <span class="label">mode 1</span> (fundamental, f₁ = (1/2L)√(T/μ)):

      ●━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━●   ← endpoints pinned
       \                      /
        \                    /
         '─────────────────'      ← single bulge

  <span class="label">mode 2</span> (1st overtone, f₂ = 2 f₁):

      ●━━━━━━━●━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━●   ← node in the middle
       \     / \              /
        \   /   \            /
         '─'     '──────────'

  <span class="label">mode 3</span> (2nd overtone, f₃ = 3 f₁):

      ●━━●━━━━━━━━●━━━━━━━━●━━━━●  ← two interior nodes
       \/         /\       /\   /
       /\        /  \     /  \ /
      ●  '──────'    '───'    '
</div>

  <p>
    Three things to notice that generalise to all modes, in any geometry:
  </p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Each mode has its own frequency.</strong> Higher modes oscillate faster.</li>
    <li><strong>Each mode has a fixed shape.</strong> Mode 3 always vibrates with two interior zeros (nodes); it doesn't morph into mode 2.</li>
    <li><strong>Different modes don't interfere.</strong> A plucked string's motion is exactly the sum of every mode's contribution, each evolving independently in time.</li>
  </ul>

  <h3>8.3 · Striking, sound, and decay</h3>

  <p>
    A real strike is a transient: a brief force impulse at one position
    on the string. The string's response is the sum of mode contributions,
    each scaled by how much the strike "looked like" that mode:
  </p>
  <div class="eq">v(x, t) = Σₙ A_n · φ_n(x) · sin(ω_n t)</div>

  <p>
    Where <code>A_n</code> is the projection of the strike onto mode
    <em>n</em> — essentially, <em>"how much does mode n's shape match where
    I struck?"</em>. Strike at the middle of the string (<code>x = L/2</code>):
    odd modes (1, 3, 5, …) have a peak there, so they ring loud; even
    modes (2, 4, …) have a zero there, so they ring not at all. This is
    why striking different parts of an instrument changes its timbre.
  </p>

  <p>
    Real materials dissipate energy. We model that with a damping ratio
    <code>ζ_n</code> per mode, giving an exponential envelope:
  </p>
  <div class="eq">v(x, t) = Σₙ A_n · φ_n(x) · exp(−ζ_n ω_n t) · sin(ω_n √(1−ζ_n²) t)</div>

  <p>
    Two consequences worth absorbing:
  </p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Higher modes decay faster</strong> at the same <code>ζ</code>, because the exponent <code>ζ ω t</code> grows quicker with <code>ω</code>. That's why a struck bell's bright initial timbre mellows out over time — the overtones die first.</li>
    <li><strong>Decay time is <code>1 / (ζ ω)</code></strong>. For <code>ζ = 0.01</code> and an audible 440 Hz mode (<code>ω ≈ 2765 rad/s</code>), that's ~36 ms — a percussive sound. For <code>ζ = 0.0001</code> it's 3.6 seconds — a bell.</li>
  </ul>

  <p>
    To turn vibration into <em>sound</em>, every mode shape radiates into
    the surrounding air with an efficiency <code>α_n</code> that depends
    on its shape and the medium. The final audio signal is the radiated
    pressure at the listener position; in the viewer we approximate this
    as a weighted sum where each mode's contribution scales by
    <code>α_n</code>:
  </p>
  <div class="eq">p(t) = Σₙ α_n · A_n · exp(−ζ_n ω_n t) · sin(ω_dn t)</div>

  <p>
    <code>α_n</code> is computed from the modal velocity normal to the
    boundary and the medium's impedance. This is why setting the
    Acoustic Medium to vacuum-like properties silences the output — the
    structure still vibrates, it just doesn't radiate.
  </p>

  <h3>8.4 · Going from 1D to 2D shapes</h3>

  <p>
    A 1D string has modes with a single index <em>n</em>: shape number 1,
    2, 3, …, ordered by frequency. A 2D shape has modes indexed by
    <em>two</em> numbers in the simplest case (number of bulges along x,
    number along y), and ordered modes start to mix the two indices.
    For a rectangular plate fixed at the edges:
  </p>

  <div class="state-diagram">  <span class="label">mode (1,1)</span>          <span class="label">mode (2,1)</span>          <span class="label">mode (1,2)</span>          <span class="label">mode (2,2)</span>

  ┌──────────────┐    ┌──────────────┐    ┌──────────────┐    ┌──────────────┐
  │              │    │       │      │    │              │    │       │      │
  │   (single    │    │       │      │    │ ─────────── │    │       │      │
  │    bulge)    │    │  one  │ node │    │              │    │  + ─── ─── + │
  │              │    │       │      │    │     node     │    │       │      │
  │              │    │       │      │    │              │    │ ─── + + ─── │
  └──────────────┘    └──────────────┘    └──────────────┘    └──────────────┘
   fundamental         vertical node line   horizontal node     crossed nodes
</div>

  <p>
    The patterns of zero displacement are <em>nodal lines</em>; for higher
    modes they form intricate curves. In Chladni's classical experiment
    (the demo this viewer's Chladni-mode <kbd>C</kbd> toggle is named for),
    sand sprinkled on a plate gathers along these nodal lines because
    they're the only places that don't shake the sand off.
  </p>

  <p>
    For arbitrary shapes (not just rectangles), the modes can't be written
    as products of 1D sines — they're whatever shape comes out of solving
    the 2D wave equation with the actual boundary. Closed-form solutions
    rarely exist. We need a numerical method: FEM.
  </p>

  <h3>8.5 · FEM: from continuous PDE to a matrix problem</h3>

  <p>
    The 2D wave equation we want to solve is
  </p>
  <div class="eq">ρ ∂²u/∂t² = ∇·(E ∇u)</div>
  <p>
    in some 2D region, with material parameters <em>E</em> (stiffness),
    <em>ρ</em> (density), and boundary conditions on the region's edge.
    Three steps turn this into something a computer can chew on:
  </p>

  <h4>Step 1: mesh the shape</h4>
  <p>
    Cut the 2D region into a triangulation. Each triangle has three nodes
    at its corners. The displacement field <em>u(x, t)</em> is approximated
    as <em>linear interpolation</em> between the displacement values at
    those nodes. So instead of a continuous unknown function we have a
    finite list of unknowns: <code>u_i(t)</code>, one per node DOF.
  </p>
  <p>
    Triangulation in this viewer is done with shewchuk's Triangle library,
    invoked via <code>mesh::triangulate</code> in
    <code>libmodal/src/mesh/triangulator.cpp</code>.
  </p>

  <h4>Step 2: assemble K and M</h4>
  <p>
    For each triangle, you can derive (from the wave equation + linear
    interpolation) a small <strong>element stiffness matrix</strong>
    <code>K_e</code> (6×6 for the 2D CST element — 3 nodes × 2 DOFs) and
    an <strong>element mass matrix</strong> <code>M_e</code>. The full
    system is built by summing contributions from every element into
    global sparse matrices:
  </p>
  <div class="eq">M ü + K u = f_external</div>
  <p>
    <code>K</code> encodes how much force each unit displacement produces;
    <code>M</code> encodes inertia. Both are sparse because each node only
    couples to its triangle neighbours.
  </p>
  <p>
    Assembly happens in <code>libmodal/src/fem/assembler.cpp</code> (pure
    2D CST) and <code>assembler_mixed.cpp</code> (CST + beam elements for
    strings). The element matrices themselves come from
    <code>element_cst.cpp</code> and <code>element_beam.cpp</code>.
  </p>

  <h4>Step 3: eigensolve</h4>
  <p>
    For <strong>free vibration</strong> (no external force, <code>f = 0</code>)
    we look for solutions of the form <code>u(t) = φ · sin(ωt)</code>.
    Substituting into <code>M ü + K u = 0</code> gives
  </p>
  <div class="eq">−ω² M φ · sin(ωt) + K φ · sin(ωt) = 0</div>
  <p>
    Dropping the time factor leaves the <strong>generalised eigenvalue
    problem</strong>:
  </p>
  <div class="eq">K φ = ω² M φ</div>
  <p>
    Solve this and you get a discrete set of eigenpairs
    <code>(ω_k, φ_k)</code> — exactly the modes from §8.2, but for an
    arbitrary 2D geometry instead of a 1D string. We solve typically
    24 – 120 of the lowest modes using sparse Lanczos iteration in
    <code>libmodal/src/solver/eigensolve_sparse.cpp</code>.
  </p>

  <div class="note warn">
    <strong>Why generalised, not standard?</strong> Because <code>M</code>
    isn't the identity. A standard eigenvalue problem looks like
    <code>A φ = λ φ</code> — what we have is <code>K φ = ω² M φ</code>,
    a generalised eigenvalue problem. It's still solvable cheaply because
    <code>M</code> is positive-definite, so we can reduce to standard
    form via Cholesky factorisation of M, but the algorithm is slightly
    different from the basic power method.
  </div>

  <h3>8.6 · How a click becomes a sound</h3>

  <p>
    End-to-end trace of what happens when you click the viewport. This is
    the complete pipeline; every step touches a specific file in the
    codebase.
  </p>

  <div class="state-diagram">  <span class="label">User clicks viewport at (sx, sy)</span> [<span class="arrow">app.cpp · on_mouse_click</span>]
       │
       │  screen → world coords via the camera's zoom/pan
       <span class="arrow">▼</span>
  <span class="label">modal_strike(res, x, y, magnitude)</span> [<span class="arrow">modal_api.cpp</span>]
       │
       ├─── push StrikeEvent onto strike_buf  (audio path; lock-free queue)
       │
       └─── compute_strike_impulse(mesh, mode_shapes, x, y, f_impulse)
              │
              │  for each mode k:
              │    f_impulse[k] = φ_k(nearest_node) · strike_direction
              │
              ├─── apply_sympathetic_coupling (if enabled)
              │
              └─── for each mode: vis_impulses[k].add(f_impulse[k] * mag)
                        (visual path; ring buffer for analytical decay)

  <span class="label">VISUAL THREAD</span> (60 Hz, in render() loop)
       │
       ├─── modal_get_displacements(res, disp, t)
       │      for each node n:
       │        disp[n] = Σₖ φ_k(n) · vis_impulses[k].eval(ω_k, ζ_k, t)
       │
       ├─── apply gravity sag (if enabled)
       │
       └─── write disp to OpenGL VBO → draw mesh

  <span class="label">AUDIO THREAD</span> (PortAudio callback, ~5 ms blocks)
       │
       ├─── drain strike_buf into mode_states (apply impulses to A, t0)
       │
       ├─── upload mode_states to GPU (cudaMemcpyAsync)
       │
       ├─── <span class="label">CUDA kernel</span> [<span class="arrow">synthesis.cu</span>]
       │      for each PCM sample s in this block:
       │        t = t_start + s · dt
       │        pcm[s] = Σₖ α_k · A_k · exp(−ζ_k ω_k (t−t0_k))
       │                     · sin(ω_k √(1−ζ_k²) (t−t0_k))
       │
       ├─── air-cavity bandpass (if enabled) — parallel SVF
       │
       ├─── noise gate (HP-filter amplitude envelope)
       │
       ├─── post-process: gain → LP → soft-clip → auto-normalize
       │
       └─── write to PortAudio buffer → speaker
</div>

  <p>
    Three details that surprise newcomers:
  </p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Visual and audio are decoupled.</strong> They share strike
        events but run on different threads with different time bases. Visual
        uses scaled time (<kbd>S</kbd> key cycles slow-motion), audio always
        runs at real time. Each path stores its own copy of recent impulses.</li>
    <li><strong>Audio synthesis is analytical, not simulated.</strong> The
        CUDA kernel computes the exact sum
        <code>Σ α_k A_k exp(−ζω t) sin(ω_d t)</code> at every PCM sample,
        with no time-stepping. No accumulated error, no stability concerns,
        and trivially parallel across samples.</li>
    <li><strong>The strike acts on one node.</strong> We snap the world
        click to the nearest mesh node, then project that node's eigenvector
        entry onto the strike direction (outward boundary normal for
        boundary nodes, +Y for interior). The whole strike-to-modes
        projection lives in <code>compute_strike_impulse</code>.</li>
  </ul>

  <h3>8.7 · Why the same machinery handles strings, plates, and bells</h3>

  <p>
    Everything in §8.5 and §8.6 was general. The viewer has different
    <em>elements</em> (CST triangle, Bernoulli beam, DKT plate) but each
    one just contributes its own little <code>K_e</code> and <code>M_e</code>
    block during assembly. After assembly the eigenvalue problem looks
    identical regardless of which elements were used. Same Lanczos solve,
    same modal sum for synthesis.
  </p>

  <p>
    That's why adding tension to strings (§7) was a small change: tension
    contributes an extra term <code>K_geom</code> to the beam element's
    <code>K_e</code>. Everything downstream — eigensolve, MAC, HF
    derivatives, audio synthesis — is unchanged. The element layer is
    where the physics lives; everything above it operates on abstract
    sparse matrices.
  </p>

  <p>
    Multi-material support (§5) works the same way: each triangle's
    element matrix is computed with its own material, then the assembler
    sums all elements into a single global <code>K</code> and <code>M</code>.
    The eigensolver doesn't know or care that the material varies — it
    just sees a different sparse pattern of stiffness values.
  </p>

  <h3>8.8 · Deeper: Hellmann-Feynman, MAC, geometric stiffness</h3>

  <p>
    With the basics in place, the three "advanced" results in the rest of
    the viewer become short corollaries.
  </p>

  <h4>Why Hellmann-Feynman is cheap</h4>
  <p>
    Conventional derivative <code>df/dW</code>: change W, redo the entire
    eigensolve, finite-difference. Cost: one extra eigensolve per
    derivative.
  </p>
  <p>
    Hellmann-Feynman insight: for an eigenpair <code>(λ, φ)</code> of
    <code>K φ = λ M φ</code> with M-normalisation <code>φᵀ M φ = 1</code>,
  </p>
  <div class="eq">dλ/dp = φᵀ (dK/dp − λ dM/dp) φ</div>
  <p>
    No new eigensolve. Cost: one matrix re-assembly at perturbed W (much
    cheaper than eigendecomposition) plus a quadratic-form evaluation.
    Total ~5% of an eigensolve. We use this for Newton-step Fine-W /
    Manual-W via the Rayleigh-quotient formulation, which handles
    M-normalisation automatically (see <a href="features.html#tuning-overview">features §3</a>).
  </p>

  <h4>MAC mode tracking</h4>
  <p>
    Two eigenvectors <code>φ_a</code> and <code>φ_b</code> from different
    solves are "the same mode" if their shapes correlate strongly. The
    Modal Assurance Criterion measures this with a single scalar in
    <code>[0, 1]</code>:
  </p>
  <div class="eq">MAC(φ_a, φ_b) = |φ_a · φ_b|² / ((φ_a · φ_a)(φ_b · φ_b))</div>
  <p>
    The square handles sign flips (eigenvectors are only defined up to
    sign). Above 0.6 means "clearly the same mode topology"; below 0.3
    means the modes diverged — the geometry change crossed a mode-identity
    boundary and the tracker needs a fresh reference. Used by Manual-W
    to auto-retrack the pinned mode across W changes.
  </p>

  <h4>Geometric stiffness from tension</h4>
  <p>
    A taut string's pitch comes from tension, not bending. Why? The
    transverse equation for a beam under axial tension <em>T</em> is
  </p>
  <div class="eq">EI ∂⁴v/∂x⁴ − T ∂²v/∂x² + μ ∂²v/∂t² = 0</div>
  <p>
    When <code>T · L² ≫ EI</code> the bending term <code>EI ∂⁴v/∂x⁴</code>
    is negligible and the equation collapses to the pure-string wave
    equation from §8.2. Frequencies follow <code>f_n = (n/2L) · √(T/μ)</code>.
  </p>
  <p>
    In FEM terms, the tension contributes a <em>geometric stiffness</em>
    matrix <code>K_geom = T/L · [transverse-block]</code> that adds to the
    usual bending stiffness:
  </p>
  <div class="eq">K_total = K_bending + K_geom</div>
  <p>
    High tension → geometric term dominates → string-like behaviour.
    Zero tension → pure bending beam. Same eigensolve handles both
    regimes because the only thing that changed is the values in
    <code>K</code>.
  </p>
