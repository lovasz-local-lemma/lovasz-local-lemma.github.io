The idea behind it

My first idea was to turn a real-time 3D render into a Rive animation. A texture could carry the finished image; I wanted to see how much could instead become reusable geometry, animation keys and eventually NPR contours.

That question kept opening other doors: use Rive to drive a live GPU renderer, implement its drawing interface, add new curves and soft fields, then build editing and content-aware effects around them. A photon-mapping and time-of-flight system gave me richer scenes to try exporting. Some directions became useful tools; others were interesting enough to follow simply as graphics problems.

Working intuition An interesting possibility is a reason to begin; a working result lets me see where it leads.

Deliberate scope Standalone .riv carries supported drawing and authored animation. Rive-driven WebGL and hybrid output include a viewer. Extended curves and splats can remain native or be approximated. Signed GPU Canvas studies are separately authored; general desktop conversion is not yet available.

01 · The original question · a 3D scene inside a 2D animation

Rendered glass. Delivered as Rive.

I began with the ambition of turning any real-time 3D render into .riv. The straightforward image route is to render each frame and display it as a texture in Rive. Image drawing made that a useful starting point, but I was more interested in what could survive as drawing structure and animation.

The next idea was to analyze the scene: which shapes persist, which properties move, and which appearance really needs an image? Reuse that structure and encode its changes as keys. Depending on how much of the scene you want to preserve, a seemingly simple exporter becomes a substantial rendering problem.

Start with a working result below. The native renderer packages an authored glass-and-lighting orbit into an ordinary .riv, which plays through the official Rive runtime. It mixes drawing structure with sampled appearance: the view-dependent glass still uses baked images. The next panel opens up those layers.

An ambition explored through working examples

The goal was general conversion. The implemented routes each have a defined scope: an authored orbit, projected geometry, a chosen NPR drawing or a retained scene with its own player.

02 · Inside the exported drawing

3D motion becomes 2D keys.

Description · at a glance

A keyed animation can reuse a shape across many frames, instead of storing the whole screen again. This layered export keys projected vertices, colours and image-mesh coordinates. Turn layers off and follow their tracks: geometry and appearance have different jobs, and only the dependencies that need images pay that cost.

Glass appearance is a separate dependency: this sphere stores 240 cropped refraction images, including three-band dispersion. The receiver carries a baked light texture, while contours and the foil accent are keyed geometry and colour. The diagram explains a companion artifact built with the same Rive property vocabulary; it is not a claim that the native 600-angle file is vector-only.

ExplanationGeometry keys, appearance samples and paint orderWhy one export contains several kinds of stored information.＋

A two-dimensional player composites a draw order rather than solving arbitrary per-pixel scene depth. As a camera orbits, faces may change their relative painter order. One shared mesh in one fixed position in the tree cannot necessarily represent every pose, even if its vertices can be animated perfectly.

The slot-based export distributes reused faces among an order-compatible sequence. Stable identity, matching topology, color, winding and placement constraints determine what can fold. If those conditions fail, the exporter falls back to another tier rather than silently reorder the image. Rings or other primitives that switch between an ellipse and multiple clipped arcs can still require pose-specific representations.

The new export preflight refuses reuse when ordered texture coordinates, mesh topology or pose identity differ. Refusal also propagates across overlapping ordered slots, preventing a foldable foreground mesh from moving behind a neighboring fallback mesh. Asset pruning then removes only images no final drawable references.

Scene3D mesh pose keys currently use HOLD interpolation. This preserves the authored pose snapshots; it is not a continuous camera solver. Smooth interpolation of projected vertices cannot reliably interpolate perspective, changing visibility or paint order. Compact parameter motion such as a gradient sweep is a different and more favorable keyed case.

The cube study makes this conversion concrete. Perspective projection is sampled into scalar animation tracks at 60 fps, and the official Rive runtime plays those keys. The host reads back the selected vertex’s position for the curve readout; it does not reposition the drawing. This preserves a smooth authored orbit while making the remaining limitation visible: a new camera projection requires new keys.

The richer keyed fixture deliberately separates shape from appearance. A circle’s projected contour is keyed as geometry; a photon-baked receiver is carried by an image mesh; the foil sticker has color tracks; glass uses sampled embedded images. Adding layers exposes the corresponding track families. This illustrates the same geometry-versus-appearance tradeoff as the larger native orbit without reusing images from an incompatible camera.

Once projection is back in ordinary Rive, the drawing can change without GPU Canvas. The follow-up replaces sampled glass and textured surfaces with engraved curves or cut-paper tones. The same projected sphere silhouette, layer controls and 60 fps orbit remain keyed Rive geometry. These authored vector styles retain a scene’s motion while choosing a different appearance; they do not need the original shader or a live refraction solve.

ExplanationReuse is constrained by topology and orderA slot can change values only when it still represents the same drawable structure.＋

An animation exporter can fold corresponding objects across sampled poses and key the properties that change. Correspondence requires compatible structure: path topology, material kind, image binding and ordering cannot be treated as arbitrary numeric channels. If a shape changes its role or topology, forcing it into an old slot can create a small file that draws the wrong scene.

Painter order is similarly structural. Rive’s serialized child ordering must be matched to the intended paint stream; reversing the relationship can produce a perfectly readable file with incorrect occlusion. The reduction reported in the existing fixture counts drawable nodes separately from all serialized components because additional keyed properties can increase the latter even as the visible tree shrinks.

Orthographic projection supplies useful geometric simplifications, including circles projected as ellipses at a fixed pose. It does not make arbitrary temporal interpolation exact. Between authored keys, the player follows the supported animation channels, so the sampled pose domain and interpolation error remain part of the export contract.

A documented 152-pose fixture reduced the drawable tree from 70,119 to 1,579 while making the file about 15 percent smaller. Total serialized objects increased because keyed properties have their own objects. This is a useful concrete result precisely because it distinguishes drawable reuse from universal compression, and because short sequences may not repay the keying overhead.

03 · Isocurves · from appearance to editable geometry

Going deeper: turn shading into paths.

Description · at a glance

The furthest I pushed the drawing route was to start with non-photorealistic rendering, then turn the result into isocurves. A few well-chosen tone regions can preserve a surprising amount of the scene while giving it a new visual character. This was worth exploring as a graphics problem even where the practical answer would simply be a texture.

Sample the shaded field, choose luminance levels, then join their crossings into closed contours. Nested filled regions approximate the tone with ordinary Rive paths. The native recording shows the drawing style, the schematic exposes the conversion, and Iso Studio lets you relight and export a scene. The exported drawing retains the selected projection.

More detail produces more contours, control points, overlap and changing topology. Key reuse helps when the structure persists; it cannot make every moving shadow or newly visible region cheap. These examples work well at selected scene and style budgets, with visible approximation when pushed further.

ExplanationThe native Iso, Hatch and Inkline routesDifferent drawing languages retain different parts of the same field.＋

The live isocurve pipeline below samples a lit scene, extracts closed tone boundaries and draws them with official Rive. Move the light or camera and the paths change. The fitted-ellipse and photon-relief studies offer two other ways to turn appearance into actual geometry.

This is also a study of how to design for Rive’s drawing budget. Tone bands, coherent strokes and a small set of soft marks can preserve the structure of a scene with different costs and editing possibilities. The aim is to find useful vector encodings and measure their error and workload. A shader can supply the lighting field while the final contours remain ordinary Rive geometry.

The preset “* Ink and tone: the drawing, analytic” combines Hatch and Inkline on the same built-in scene. Hatch starts in Flow / Gradient with coats off, so tone is carried by strokes; Inkline adds creases, object contours, sphere terminators and shadow boundaries as separate line classes. This route works from the scene and analytic illumination approximation, rather than vectorizing the final color image.

The Hatch node now exposes eleven patterns: Lines, Dots, Scribble, Relief, Oneline, Ink spiral, Stained glass, Flow, Hilbert, Isolines and Adaptive. The shared colour and normalize-tone controls allow the drawing language to change without replacing the underlying tone source. This is a more focused demonstration than cycling unrelated screen filters.

The native Iso node already emits ordinary Rive-compatible paths and works with both official and enhanced renderers. It is not desktop-only by design. This browser counterpart demonstrates the conversion; the full native graph, gradient/feather fitting and depth-of-field extensions remain separate.

Illum, Hatch, Brush and Cel use an analytic direct-light/shadow construction for the supported scene geometry. Exact subproblems such as a boundary or Voronoi clip do not make the entire image an exact global-illumination solution. Repaint is explicitly a fit, and its Rive soft-lobe appearance remains an approximation.

Theory / mathematicsTone becomes coverage, width or path lengthA vector counterpart needs a geometric encoding of darkness.＋

A screen shader can change the color of every pixel. A vector recipe instead encodes the sampled illumination in geometry: dot radius, stroke width, the number of crossing families, or the length of a wavy path. The halftone route makes radius proportional to the square root of darkness so dot area, rather than radius, varies linearly with its tone signal. This is a chosen coverage law; the square lattice and maximum circular footprint still limit the coverage range.

Other recipes preserve their own invariants. Ink spiral width is capped below one turn’s spacing to prevent neighboring ribbons from overlapping into even-odd holes. Relief rows are emitted in an ordered stack and sample the baseline field that defines their displacement. These details are what turn a suggestive filter into a valid drawable with predictable fill and ordering behavior.

The surface versions anchor their construction to face coordinates and the relevant baked or analytic illumination. That attachment is what keeps marks from sliding across a wall during a camera orbit. Pixel-level and surface-level styles may resemble one another while retaining different motion behavior.

04 · From shading contours to moving wavefronts

What about time of flight?

If you have explored my other rendering projects, you may already have met time of flight (ToF). Instead of showing only a steady-state image, with all arrival times combined, we separate light by how long it has travelled. Follow a short pulse in simulated slow motion and light appears to ripple outward, reaching different parts of the scene at different times.

In a participating medium, such as fog, scattering makes the pulse visible between surfaces as well as when it reaches them. The first recording shows this time-resolved view: watch the light move through the room and illuminate its surfaces as the clock advances.

A contour can describe more than brightness. What if the quantity we draw is the time light takes to arrive? The analytical-wavefront experiment in the second recording constructs fronts of equal optical travel time, then projects their geometry into the drawing. Advancing the clock makes the front move through the scene.

This extends the same question behind the isocurves: can a rendering result become meaningful geometry rather than just another image? Here the geometry describes light in flight. The native construction handles selected geometric-optics paths, including supported specular branches; it does not model diffraction. Later, the transport section separates these fronts from time-gated photon beams and explains what each representation retains.

05 · Another route · let Rive drive the renderer

Two export formats. Five instruments to explore.

Description · at a glance

There was another way to connect the two worlds: use .riv as an intermediate layer for animation and interaction, and let a WebGL or WebGPU host do the rendering. The drawing no longer has to approximate every shaded detail as a vector shape.

While I was working on this, Rive released GPU Canvas. I was a little disappointed that I might be reinventing the wheel, but also excited: we were exploring a very similar idea about how to push Rive further. I then tried both formats, with computation inside the document or in a host alongside it.

This project supports two delivery formats for these visual instruments: a GPU Canvas .riv containing the shader behavior, and an ordinary .riv paired with a JavaScript/GPU host. Four studies are available in both forms: compound materials, wavelength-dependent refraction, a playable membrane and a liquid lens. Prismatic Garden adds a fifth instrument using the host format, with a movable prism and time sections through a Julia field.

The next chapter shows a concrete reason to keep the host route: persistent fog and photon accumulation implemented with compute shaders. This is an API distinction in the current implementation, rather than a claim that RIVX is a better replacement for Rive.

Both formats are supported

Description · at a glance

The shared operation is a change of representation. Start with a scene, animation or measured field; retain the information needed by the intended interaction; then express its appearance as pixels, geometric marks or a mixture. The shader route evaluates a field directly. The vector route constructs curves, strokes, fills or textured meshes from that field. Neither result makes the other unnecessary.

The interesting mathematics lives at those boundaries: projection turns 3D motion into 2D keys; covariance turns a spatial Gaussian into a screen footprint; contours turn a scalar field into a drawing; a temporal kernel selects an optical path-length shell; compositing determines whether exported opacity keys reproduce the intended mixture. Theory / mathematicsIntegrate a moving spot over a segmentThe error-function beam is an analytic motion-blur primitive.＋

The vector-beam renderer treats each straight segment as the path of a Gaussian spot. Instead of sampling many intermediate positions, it evaluates the Gaussian’s line integral: an error-function difference along the segment and a Gaussian across it. The implementation normalizes by segment length for its fixed traversal-energy convention, so long, fast jumps become dimmer.

This is analytic for a constant-width Gaussian moving uniformly along a straight segment. Curved motion is approximated by the chosen segment chain, and changing speed or width within a segment would require a different integral or subdivision. The glow and tone mapping applied afterward are display operations.

This beam belongs to the contour/phosphor laboratory. A photon beam in the transport laboratory carries a sampled light path and an estimator measure. They share a useful line geometry but do not share a physical interpretation merely because both are called beams.

The native Scene3D_new scope exporter also bakes the currently captured contour geometry into a stock-Rive animation. It animates a gradient-stop sweep at 60 fps over static feathered stroke legs. ExplanationThe native depth renderer and a painted document solve visibility differentlyActual sphere depth matters at intersections; a projected disc is insufficient.＋

The custom Scene3D ZBuffer route is a dedicated rendering path rather than an accidental property of the 2D shape renderer. Theory / mathematicsA photon stamp conserves its discrete deposited quantityKernel radius redistributes a deposit instead of silently changing its total.＋

The lightmap route maps a photon deposit to a face chart and evaluates a Gaussian-like kernel at nearby texels. Its normalization uses the weights of the texels actually retained on that face. A kernel clipped by a wall edge is renormalized over those kept samples, so its discrete integral remains the deposited quantity divided by the launched-path population.

The equation is a bookkeeping invariant, not a proof that every smoothed estimate is unbiased. Kernel bandwidth, finite photons, face boundaries and the selected estimator still determine the spatial error. Normalizing a truncated stamp preserves its mass while changing its shape.

The current baker requires a supported unrotated room box. Chart conversion helpers can describe rotated coordinates, but the deposit route explicitly rejects a rotated room rather than silently stamping with an incompatible inverse. Linear accumulation also precedes exposure, tone mapping and display dither. A tone-mapped image is not the array on which conservation should be tested.

Theory / mathematicsSweeping a random variable requires its densityA sphere releases two angular variables; a ring releases one conditional variable.＋

Replacing a sampled point by a swept primitive integrates over variables that the path originally sampled. A spherical sweep varies the full direction; a ring holds the polar angle and varies azimuth. A uniform azimuth ring is therefore not generally the same integral as uniform spherical emission. The normalization has to follow the released variables and the angular lobe at the vertex being left.

The implementation expresses a full-sphere angular law as a density relative to the uniform sphere. Diffuse release, a participating-medium phase function and a glossy lobe have different support and weights. The photon payload already includes the factors assigned to the sampled anchor path, so applying reflectance a second time at the display primitive would double count it.

Delta events are a separate case. An ideal mirror or refraction event is not a smooth angular density that can be released into a continuous sphere merely for a nicer picture. The declared capability table records such restrictions, but the registry is not a complete automatic proof or dispatcher for every combination of current display options.

Theory / mathematicsA finite spherical band has an exact angular massChanging band count should refine geometry rather than introduce a predictable brightness gain.＋

25 · Temporal lightmap export

A dissolve is an equation, not two opacity sliders

Description · at a glance

Later export work carries changing wall illumination using a finite set of baked time slices. The images can switch at slice boundaries or be stacked and animated through opacity. This supersedes the older limitation that exported walls always remain at their settled illumination. It does not provide arbitrary animated pixels: the file reuses the slice images that were baked.

Source-over composition makes a naïve symmetric crossfade incorrect. Two half-transparent layers do not produce one fully covered wall; at their midpoint some background remains visible, producing a periodic dip. The exporter therefore derives opacity schedules from the desired visible weights of the ordered stack. This is a small mathematical detail with a conspicuous visual consequence.

Theory / mathematicsSolve the opacity schedule for the desired weightsIndependent complementary alpha sliders do not form a normalized crossfade.＋

For source-over compositing, a layer is attenuated by every layer above it. Its contribution is therefore not simply its own alpha. If two layers are both faded with complementary alpha, the lower one is additionally attenuated by the upper one and the resulting weights no longer sum to one over a transparent background.

A normalized two-slice blend can keep the lower slice opaque while the upper slice takes the desired interpolation weight. More general slice stacks must solve their alpha schedule in the actual paint order. This is an encoding problem before it is an animation-polish problem.

The quantities being blended also need care. Linear light can be accumulated before tone mapping, while a blend of two already tone-mapped PNGs is a display interpolation. The integrated lightmap includes terms that are not necessarily present in each shipped transient slice, so summing exported images is not an independent conservation test of the integrated estimator.

The validation reads back which image assets the imported animation actually references. Merely counting embedded slices would miss a file that stores every image but points every pose at the first one. Export correctness is tested at the behavior the user sees.

The project joins three kinds of work: authoring richer scenes, extending how they are drawn, and delivering the result through an appropriate player. The scene may contain 3D motion, light transport, soft fields or temporal effects. The presentation may keep live computation, express it as Rive curves and regions, or bake selected dependencies into images and keys. Choosing that boundary is the common task across the platform.

The work began with a 3D Rive scripting prototype: more convincing than expected, but expensive when many samples and drawing updates ran through the script layer. It led to two complementary tools: a desktop authoring studio and an enhanced drawing backend. Returning the result to ordinary Rive then became a representation problem: reuse projected motion as keys, reconstruct illumination with surfaces or curves, and express soft fields with controlled marks.

GPU Canvas adds another way to explore these ideas, with live shader evaluation inside the document. The ordinary-vector experiments keep asking a different question: how much of a shaded scene can be expressed through paths, fills and animation? Following these questions produced several working forms: ordinary .riv exports, signed GPU Canvas instruments and specialized browser viewers. Each preserves a different part of the scene. Their differences are part of what the project explores.

ExplanationKeep the behavior that the audience actually needsA live document preserves interaction; a portable animation preserves a selected performance.＋

The same illuminated room can be stored as six surface images, a collection of Gaussian primitives, a sequence of projected vector poses, or a retained 3D record. These alternatives differ in what can still change after export. Moving the camera over a diffuse lightmap reuses surface illumination; moving around an already projected drawing cannot reveal a newly visible surface. A mirror image depends on the eye position even when the room and light remain fixed.

The native application is the authoring and research workbench. Ordinary .riv files carry a representable drawing or authored animation. A diagram may need controllable contours; a product page may need a small authored orbit; a rendering study may need free camera and time inspection.
