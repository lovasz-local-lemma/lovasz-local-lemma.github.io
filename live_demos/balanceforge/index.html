Why this dodger works ↗

Forecast → measure → rank. The supplied physics model predicts nine candidate headings for 1.2 s. A small trained network scores their clearance, wall margin and motion. It replans every 0.05 s.

A temporal CNN would learn motion and useful features from successive images. Here, velocity and a physical forecast are supplied explicitly: learning only has to rank the alternatives. The history thumbnails are explanatory, not network inputs.

Removing velocity reduces fresh-test survival from 33/40 to 0/40. That supports the importance of motion information; it does not show that a properly trained temporal CNN would be worse.

Course rules & obstacles ↗

The ceiling can be touched. It is a passive rebound surface, not a touch-to-lose zone. The attempt ends when the ball sinks, escapes the course or runs out of time.

  • Two ramps: change the launch and landing angles.
  • Movable bumper: exchanges momentum with the ball.
  • Low beam: limits high arcs; a rebound changes speed and direction.
  • Single strike: after impact, the club freezes and the ball continues under physics.

The policy observes ball and hole state, not an arbitrary obstacle map. It is trained for this particular layout. Watch actual sinks; a high fitness is not a success percentage.

Why the first word works better ↗

Acquire first, then refine. The previous search ignored the poor first loop and kept a saturated cart/joint network. This controller learns from neutral commands and is scored on the entire first word.

We tested fixed and shrinking tolerance with the same starts and training budget. Both worked; the selected shrinking run kept every complete loop's mean error below 20 px on 40 fresh nearby starts. That is approximate tracing, not exact handwriting.

The word and future-target cues are known. Thirteen learned coefficients control the cart and telescopes; the existing physical joint servo holds its neutral bend. No planner draws the actual pen trace.

Geometry knows the curve: fixed arm lengths come from the complete curve’s Fourier coefficients. The controller sees the arm angles, current tip-to-target error and a loop-phase clock. It learns variable angular rates on this repeated curve; it does not receive the complete curve at each step. This is curve-specific feedback, not a claim of unseen-curve tracking. Observation encoding experimental

Top-K (default): agent state + 4 nearest bullets (20 inputs). Fast and usually enough for sparse patterns.
Grid 16×16 flat DEPRECATED: agent state + flat 256-cell grid (260 inputs). Was a stepping stone toward CNN; in practice the high input count hurts NEAT's local-mutation search (no spatial prior) and the single resolution misses both close-quarters and global structure. Kept for education / regression comparison only — don't expect it to actually solve dodge.
Multi-scale grid: two grids per tick — a 16×16 LOCAL view (±80px around the agent) for fine maneuvering, plus a 16×16 GLOBAL view of the whole playfield for big-picture awareness. Designed to feed a CNN policy (use with policy = "CNN — multi- scale" on the left). 516 flat inputs is way too many for NEAT or MLP — pick Top-K if you're not on a CNN. Toggling rebuilds the trainer (different input count).
⚠ Grid encodings are compute-heavy (~10× more inputs than Top-K). For the CMA-ES + CNN path that's fine because conv weights are shared across cells -- the multi-scale CNN preset uses pop=30, rollouts=1, ~2s per gen. For NEAT-on-multi-scale (no spatial prior, 516 flat inputs feeding directly into the network), it's impractical -- stick to Top-K for NEAT.
