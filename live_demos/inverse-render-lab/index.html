Batch 19 — Four showpieces, a shape family, and a stretch that had been there all along

  • Full Asset was rendering everything 1.5× too wide. Both screen axes mapped to the same world range whatever the buffer's shape, so the horizontal scale was width/height times the vertical one. Measured on one silhouette: 188×204 pixels in a square buffer against 283×204 in the shipped 360×240 one. This is a different fault from the canvas stretching fixed last batch — that was CSS displaying a canvas at the wrong ratio, this is the renderer drawing at the wrong ratio inside a correctly shaped canvas, which is why the earlier measurement passed it. Now aspect-independent: the same bbox at every buffer shape.
  • Shapes beyond the one solid. The object is an implicit superquadric, so a shape family is one multiplier on its radius as a function of polar angle: sea star, spiral, clover, bloom. The lobes carve inward so an arm cannot grow past the frame and be clipped by it, and the plain solid scales by exactly 1, so it is bit-for-bit what it was and every locked expectation still holds.
  • Four showpieces, and everything else in a sidebar. Full Asset joins 3D reconstruction, hidden-object capture and surface extraction in the main area; the ten method labs are now a compact list beside them rather than a second row of equal-looking cards. Surface extraction keeps an in progress badge, since only one of its three routes is executable. The browser is 375px tall now against 798 when it was four tiers of cards.
  • The control dock stopped moving. Pinning it to the bottom edge did keep it reachable, but a panel of buttons sliding over the content while you read is more distracting than useful. It is static now and sits directly above the visuals in every panel — still collapsible, so an unwanted control block costs one bar of height rather than a screenful.

Batch 18 — Two experimental optics lanes, both of which refused to say what they were supposed to

  • Marked experimental on purpose. Both share this repo's theme — what an optical element passes decides what a measurement can resolve — and neither shares a forward model with the NLOS work or with each other. They are siblings by theme, not by reuse, and each is a small slice of a much larger paper.
  • Coded aperture for depth: a plain round hole wins. Over an eight-rung blur ladder, this coded mask does not beat a fully open aperture even on the flattering axis where both get the same signal level. On the physical axis, where the mask's opacity costs it photons against identical sensor noise, it collapses from 0.891 to 0.500 while the round aperture — nothing to lose, already open — does not move at all. The mask is a fixed pseudorandom pattern, not Levin's optimized one, so this is a claim about this mask; what it does establish is that the round baseline is much stronger than the usual framing admits.
  • Two structural blindnesses, one of them a surprise. A round aperture is its own 180-degree rotation, so near and far produce bit-identical blurs — asserted at exactly zero. The unexpected one: rotating a real PSF conjugates its transform, leaving the magnitude untouched at 3.6e-16. A Gaussian scene model yields a likelihood that sees only that magnitude, so the exact likelihood used everywhere else in the lane is blind to near-versus-far for every aperture. The cue is entirely in the phase.
  • Three necessary conditions, none sufficient. Sign recovery needs an asymmetric aperture, a non-Gaussian scorer, and a non-Gaussian scene — a Gaussian field has Gaussian gradients however its spectrum is shaped, so a sparsity score has nothing to grip. Only the one cell with all three works, at 0.927. Remove any one and it returns to chance.
  • Waterdrop stereo: the compression is the bill. One drop maps 68 degrees of world onto the 0.57 degrees of sensor it covers, a 118× angular compression, with 77 of 96 samples surviving total internal reflection. With the drop's shape known exactly and correspondence given — two gifts no real system gets — depth is still 57% wrong at 96 samples across the drop, and needs about 1536 before it reaches single-digit percent.
  • And the shape dependence that is the actual paper. A 1% error in assumed drop radius costs about 6% in depth, worsening to 8.3× amplification at larger errors — so it is not a fixed gain that could be calibrated away once. That is why recovering drop geometry from surface tension and gravity is the method rather than a preliminary to it. The baseline sweep is also measurably non-monotonic, because sample quantization dominates the trend it was supposed to modulate.

Batch 17 — The coded-aperture result flips when the unknown gets a second axis

  • The last batch's aperture measurement was asking the wrong question. Batch 13 measured that a finite occluder gives 23 usable directions against a bare knife edge's 25 — the anti-pinhole lost. That number stands, and it was never evidence against the technique. An occluder encodes extra dimensions in the shape of its shadow, and a 1D unknown has no extra dimensions for the shape to carry. Lift the unknown to azimuth by elevation, change nothing else, and the ordering reverses: edge 16, occluder 70. A 4.4× flip from re-asking the same question of a bigger unknown.
  • The single edge's blindness is provable, not just measured. Its visibility test compares where the ray crosses the aperture plane in x, and that crossing depends on azimuth alone — so columns sharing an azimuth are exactly proportional and every elevation difference is exactly unobservable. The test asserts it to 3e-17 and pins the rank at 16 of 256.
  • A second cap nobody asks about. At a coarser sensor the edge's rank falls to 9 rather than 12, because azimuth bins whose cuts land between two adjacent sensor columns produce identical rows. Rank is min(unknowns, cuts the sensor can resolve). Sampling the hidden scene more finely than the sensor can distinguish buys nothing.
  • Full rank is not a well posed problem. Two crossed edges, the finite occluder and a random mask all reach rank 256 and are still at 74, 70 and 129 usable directions. Reporting rank alone would call them equivalent. The undesigned random mask beating both structured apertures by nearly 2× is a lower bound on what aperture design buys — it is not even a MURA.
  • What the regularizer was quietly contributing. The edge's recovered elevation profile shifts by 4.9e-5 between two different hidden scenes; turn the smoothness prior off and that collapses to 1.1e-13. Every visible trace of elevation in the regularized picture came from the prior, not the data. A sweep across five thousandfold in the prior weight never lights the axis up.
  • Two scenes that rank the apertures differently, so the lane is not rigged. On vertical stripes — the edge's own subspace — it scores 0.959 against the best aperture's 0.956. It is blind along one axis, not bad at the job. And a narrow horizontal stripe is its worst scene, not its best, which is the intuitive wrong guess: being constant along the blind axis is not the same as being concentrated on it.

Batch 16 — Gaussian splatting, with the density control that is the actual idea

  • A real differentiable splat renderer, adjoint verified. Anisotropic Gaussians composited front to back with explicit transmittance, and a hand-derived backward pass checked against central differences to a worst relative error of 4.2e-9 across all nine parameter types. A wrong adjoint does not crash; it converges slowly and looks like a hard problem, so this check is the whole credibility of the lane.
  • Adaptive density control. Splats under strong positional gradient are cloned if small and split into two narrower children if large; transparent ones are pruned. Adam state follows surviving splats rather than being reset, which was worth 2.4× on its own.
  • Measured, and it does not say what the pitch says. Against a fixed population of the same final size, densification reaches a higher loss at equal iteration count. It only wins on wall clock — 2.8× better at an 8-second budget, a tie by 20 seconds — because early iterations are cheap while the population is small. Equal-iteration was the wrong axis, and both directions are now asserted.
  • The relight failure, shown rather than described. Fit the shaded target, then move the light. Truth changes; the recovery does not, because a splat carries a colour and no notion of a light. This is the limitation SuGaR, 2DGS and GS-IR exist to address, and the lane exhibits it instead of claiming to solve it.

Batch 15 — Equal-work benchmark and a restart ensemble

  • The benchmark immediately qualified the previous commit. Gauss-Newton was reported as three orders better than the first-order lane, on two seeds. With a third, its loss spread on cubeSphere is 2062× — two seeds at 3.0e-7 and one at 6.2e-4. On the other two scenarios the spread is exactly 1.0×. Both halves of that are needed, and only a multi-seed sweep can say the second.
  • npm run bench sweeps scenarios × estimators × seeds under a fixed step budget and writes a CSV. The committed baseline is what a later run diffs against. Deliberately outside check — a full sweep takes tens of minutes.
  • Ensemble audit: the displayed answer is one draw. Restarts the solve from independent seeds and reports per-parameter spread. Measured on capsuleTorus/SPSA, only 2 of 12 coordinates agree, and roughness spreads 27% with a mean well away from truth. Across all three lanes tried, roughness and light elevation are consistently the least-determined — independent confirmation of the earlier finding that SPSA passes the geometry gate and fails the appearance one.
  • It is a spread over restarts, not a posterior. No likelihood, no prior, no guarantee the restarts sample modes in proportion to anything. It answers whether a different run would have said something else, which a point estimate silently assumes away. Fisher information is the usual alternative and is provably blind to the anisotropy branch this lab already demonstrates.

Batch 14 — A second-order lane, and what a broken one looks like

  • Three orders of magnitude, for the same derivative budget. At 100 steps: pattern 1.9e-2, SPSA 6.4e-2, coordinate FD 4.1e-4, Gauss-Newton 3.0e-7. Once the objective's residual vector is exposed, J¹J is the Gauss-Newton Hessian and comes free from the same 2P central differences the coordinate lane already spends.
  • The "free" claim was wrong on wall clock, and is corrected. A GN step costs 3.5× a coordinate step, because each evaluation is a full residual vector over the whole lattice including the surface term, where the coordinate lane evaluates a routed scalar objective that skips most of it. It still wins decisively on equal wall clock.
  • A broken second-order method looks like a bad first-order one. The first version emitted residual entries conditionally, so moving the silhouette changed the vector's length and its index meaning — differencing two of them compared unrelated quantities. There was no error and no crash: the lane ran, converged, looked plausible, and landed a thousand times worse than plain coordinate descent. The vector now has fixed structure with weights frozen at the base point, and both properties are asserted.

Batch 13 — Coded aperture: occlusion adds rank, not measurements

  • Without an occluder the operator is rank one. Every floor sample sees the same hidden range up to a smooth falloff, so the rows are nearly parallel — the second singular value is 1.9×10−8 of the first. No number of photographs fixes that. A knife edge takes it to 25 usable directions. This is the claim the occluder-based NLOS literature rests on, and here it is a factor of 25.
  • But the finite occluder does not beat the knife edge. Measured 23 against 25. The occluder advantage reported in the literature comes from a 2D hidden scene, where the shadow's shape moves as well as its position. With only an angular coordinate to resolve, a knife edge already supplies the discontinuity and the banded operator is marginally worse. Asserted in the measured direction, with a note that a future flip means the README needs rewriting rather than the assertion relaxing.
  • All three apertures are computed over identical geometry and reported with every passive result, so the comparison isolates visibility rather than confounding it with falloff or sampling.

Batch 12 — Naming the ambiguity, and closing three physics gaps

  • The Fisher audit now says what is unconstrained. The Jacobi rotations were already being computed and then discarded, so the panel could count unidentifiable directions without ever naming one. The eigenvector for the smallest eigenvalue, rounded to a small-integer basis, prints as a monomial invariant — on a synthetic albedo/light degeneracy it reads rho / L is unconstrained. Entropy effective rank is reported beside the thresholded count, because it slides rather than steps.
  • Displacement now tilts the shading normal. It previously entered the implicit field only, so it moved the silhouette and never the shading, while the bump waves moved the shading and never the silhouette — two "geometry detail" parameters observed through disjoint channels, which made the detail ablation measure an artifact. The test that used to lock that gap in place is inverted rather than deleted.
  • Smith visibility replaces a mismatched masking term. The lobe paired a GGX distribution with a Cook-Torrance V-cavity term, which come from different microsurface models. Smith is the one GGX is built on.
  • Energy compensation, with an honest furnace test. Single-scatter GGX retains 0.996 of its energy at roughness 0.05 and only 0.541 at roughness 1. Asserted is the deficit and its growth with roughness — not that the compensated integral equals 1, which at F0 = 1 holds by construction and would prove nothing about the tabulated albedo.

Batch 11 — Layered materials: a coat is not two lobes

  • Three operators over one stack. Naive lobe addition (labelled wrong on purpose), statistical adding-doubling, and a position-free stochastic walk that is unbiased under the plane-parallel assumption. Both exist because either alone would be unaccountable: the statistical operator without a reference is asserting an accuracy it cannot measure, and the stochastic one is 60–100× too slow to drive a solver.
  • The trap, in its sharpest form. The naive fit recovers an albedo 3.73× wrong while sitting on a training residual identical to the correct model's to within 0.1%. Not a model that looks bad — one that is indistinguishable until the illumination changes.
  • Non-separability, measured twice. An albedo ratio of 4.00 between channels arrives as a response ratio of 6.74, because the internal series 1/(1 − rho·F_dr) depends on the albedo — it is not a tint. And sweeping the true coat roughness inflates the recovered base roughness 1.50× while the true base never moves, because variances add.
  • A null result that was the fixture's fault. A first run against a Lambertian base gave a held-out gap of only 1.55× and was written up as "weaker than the literature suggests". Against a microfacet base the same experiment gives 36×. That correction is recorded rather than quietly overwritten.

Batch 10 — Transient movie on the relay wall

  • The measured tensor, played back. One time bin per frame, mapped onto the relay wall. The scan index is the grid index, so a bin slice is directly a wall image with no resampling, and nothing new is simulated — it is made entirely of data the worker already computes and already transfers.
  • The expanding ring is the light-cone constraint. A sphere of constant path length meeting the wall, whose slope across the wall is the same cone the LCT deconvolves. Measured: 14 populated bin slices, and the wall centroid moves 2.18 rows across time — asserted, so a flat panel cannot pass as physics.
  • What it is not. Time zero is rectified at the wall: the forward model measures only the wall-object-wall leg, so the sensor-to-wall legs never appear. The expanding front is the hidden object's response, not the outgoing laser pulse, and the panel says so.

Batch 9 — The BSDF base is now a real fit

  • The old base could not fail. Was: an "analytic fit" whose coefficients were rescalings of the preset's own generative parameters, described in the notes as a fit. Now: N GGX lobes solved by Levenberg–Marquardt with multi-start, verified to recover a known two-lobe target to 0.26% on roughness and 0.22% on weight.
  • Lobes are GGX, not Gaussians, on purpose. The synthetic target is a sum of Gaussians in the mirror offset, so fitting Gaussians would be a model-matches-generator cheat and every information criterion would be meaningless.
  • AIC and held-out both fail to reject an unsupported lobe. On synthetic two-lobe data all three criteria prefer three lobes, because the third shaves the residual from 6.53e-4 to 6.41e-4. Only the Gram condition number catches it, jumping 2.7e1 to 1.2e4 as the third basis function goes collinear. The default verdict now rejects lobe counts whose weights are not individually identifiable.
  • On the real presets the answer is one lobe, and that is honest. The residual is flat across lobe counts. A grid search over widely separated widths confirms two distinct lobes do no better than one: a mirror-centred GGX cannot reach a secondary lobe offset from the mirror direction. The large remaining residual is what the learned kernel is for — and the old hand-placed base could never have shown it.

Batch 8 — Anisotropy, and a schedule that did not work

  • The lab's only discrete identifiability failure. Rotating the tangent frame by 180° leaves the render byte-identical, and (αx, αy, φ) equals (αy, αx, φ+90°). No local diagnostic can see either — every other exhibit here is a continuous null direction that sensitivity analysis does detect.
  • Asserting exactness caught two errors. The first parameterization looked symmetric and was not, leaving the 90° branch off by 84/255; a log-symmetric stretch exchanges the widths exactly. And the isotropic reduction failed at precisely the specular peak, because the existing branch floors its denominator at 0.001 and the new one did not — the new code was more correct, which is what broke the identity.
  • The coarse-to-fine schedule was reverted. Named twice in the notes as the obvious missing piece, and measured over 4 scenarios × 3 seeds × 2 estimators: no gain for pattern search, whose cost does not depend on resolution, and a 13% speedup for SPSA bought at the price of far worse loss on topology cases (sphereTorus 7.99e-3 → 2.08e-1). The measurement and what a working version would need are recorded in RESEARCH_NOTES.md.

Batch 7 — Fur ball, a fiber-groom family

  • Third representation family beside surface and medium. SGGX directional extinction, sigma(w) = sqrt(1 − (1 − eps²)(w·f)²), with a far-field fiber BCSDF carrying R, TT and TRT lobes over a Bessel-based longitudinal term and a logistic azimuthal term.
  • It reduces exactly to what already shipped. At groom anisotropy 1 the SGGX cross-section is identically 1, so the fiber integrator collapses onto the isotropic medium — worst deviation below 1e-12 across 200+ samples and three views, asserted. Away from 1 the silhouette must visibly move, also asserted, so the identity cannot be met by the term simply being inert.
  • Density blindness with two different causes in one image. At the centre the ray runs along the fibers and optical depth is far below one; at the rim it crosses them and saturates. Density is identifiable only in the band between. Measured on one render: 89 thin, 91 intermediate, 37 thick masked samples.
  • Two corrections against the design. Cuticle tilt is a longitudinal shift inside Mp, not an azimuthal offset — as originally specified the slider would have rotated the wrong lobe. And Disney's absorption remap is dropped: it is fitted to multiple-scattering albedo, which this single-scatter integrator does not have.
  • Additive. The vector grew 18→21 with defaults chosen so every existing scenario is bit-unchanged — the convergence test still reports iter 267, fit 5.52e-5.

Batch 6 — Specular NLOS branch, and a negative result

  • Hidden-surface reflectance is now selectable. Under an ideal mirror BRDF the only confocal path is emission along the surface normal — a measure-zero event for an ordinary path tracer, which is why it needs its own sampler.
  • Coverage collapse is the lesson. On a curved shell only 14% of surface samples have a normal that reaches the scanned raster, against 100% for a flat plate. The rest of the object is unobserved, not absent, and the panel reports the fraction so a sparse reconstruction cannot read as a complete one.
  • The expected win did not reproduce, and that is recorded rather than hidden. The design predicted f–k migration beating the LCT on a specular target, citing the paper's 3.5× improvement. Measured here, f–k trails the LCT by 0.08–0.11 on both a flat plate and a curved shell. A pure mirror deposits into a single wall cell per surface point, so the measurement is sparse and impulsive — a regime where the LCT's Wiener step denoises and f–k, having no regularizer at all, aliases. The test asserts the measured direction, not the predicted one.

Batch 5 — Edge camera rebuilt

  • The transfer operator had no geometry in it. Was: the forward model integrated the hidden angular profile straight along the floor index, so angle bin i landed on floor sample i with unit weight. That makes the operator an exact lower-triangular matrix of ones, whose inverse is a one-line finite difference. Measured consequences: a single frame recovered 99.5% of the profile, 255 more changed nothing, and even at maximum noise it scored 0.982. Now: the penumbra boundary moves as an arctangent of floor position and floor irradiance falls off as cos/r², so grazing angles are compressed and the informative far samples are the dimmest.
  • Conditioning is now reported, and it is bad on purpose. Condition number 8.0×1011; 25 of 64 singular directions above 1% of the largest; entropy effective rank 12.8. Roughly a dozen angular directions are measurable, and structure beyond them comes from the smoothness prior rather than from the photograph. The panel prints this beside the reconstruction.
  • Rectify-then-average made noise into signal. Was: each frame's derivative was half-wave rectified before averaging, which left about 4.5% phantom angular structure on a featureless scene that more frames could not remove. Now: frames are averaged first and nonnegativity is enforced once, inside a Tikhonov solve.
  • Frames finally mean something. Averaging 1→16 frames gains 0.0088 ZNCC at noise 0.48 against 0.0011 at noise 0.08, and the measurable rank is identical either way. Repeated photographs buy signal-to-noise with diminishing returns and never buy rank — both the inequality and the rank invariance are asserted in the test suite.
  • Latent indexing alias fixed. The inverse addressed measurement rows by angularBins while the forward wrote them by floorSamples. Both are 64, so it worked by coincidence.

Batch 4 — NLOS reconstructor axis

  • Reconstructor is now an axis independent of acquisition. Was: one hardcoded inverse (Wiener LCT) per acquisition mode. Now: three inverses over the identical measurement tensor, so a difference between them is a property of the estimator and not of the data. The test suite asserts the tensors are elementwise identical across all three.
  • Filtered backprojection added. The adjoint A^T y over confocal spheres plus a heuristic depth Laplacian. Additive over wall points, so progressive acquisition is an incremental update rather than a full recomputation.
  • f–k migration added. Stolt migration of the transient as an exploding-reflector wavefield. Zero tunable parameters — that is its entire contrast with the LCT's hand-set Wiener constant, and a structural test asserts it never acquires one.
  • Measured, and both locked as regressions: f–k ties the LCT (gap −0.019) rather than beating it, and the adjoint beats the regularized inverse on depth (1 cm against 7 cm). The second is a measurement of what the LCT's hardcoded regularizer costs.
  • Resolution ledger added. Was: nothing on screen said what the physics allows. Now: every active result prints the FWHM-derived axial and transverse limits beside the reconstruction, and states that the voxel grid is 1.7× finer axially and 3.3× finer laterally than those limits — so detail below them is interpolation, not measurement.

Batch 3 — Reproducibility

  • Seed control. Was: a hardcoded constant, so every convergence number the lab reported was one draw with no variance estimate. Now: settable, echoed in worker metadata, and asserted three ways — same seed reproduces bit-identically, a different seed diverges, an out-of-range seed normalizes.
  • Run manifest. Was: no way to export, cite, or hand off a run. Now: Copy run manifest emits the ten fields the Research protocol section demands, plus the live gate status and a URL that restores the configuration. Fields the app cannot supply say so rather than being omitted.
  • What re-rolling revealed. SPSA plateaus rather than converging on every seed tested — and always fails the same three gates while geometryLoss passes, so its weakness is appearance and novel-view error, not shape. Separately, whether it escapes the capsuleTorus topology basin is seed-dependent (77% on the old default seed, 87–99% on four others).

Batch 2 — Test enforcement (no UI surface)

  • render3d-convergence-test.js was red and orphaned. It ran in neither check nor test:3d, and its appearance-leak probe perturbed indices that legitimately move the silhouette. Fixed and enrolled.
  • check omitted the NLOS tests. The LCT, dual-photography, and passive-edge regressions were not in the default gate. Now they are.

Batch 1 — Panels that displayed numbers they never computed

  • Mesh lab quality metrics. Was: IoU, Chamfer, normal consistency and triangle count read from a three-row lookup table keyed on which route button was clicked, with the winner hardcoded. Now: computed from the real marching-tetrahedra extraction against the target field, with the two unimplemented routes removed rather than faked.
  • Measured-BSDF “analytic fit”. Was: labelled a fit while rescaling the preset's own generative parameters, so it could never fail. Now: labelled a fixed analytic prior, with the residual chart correctly described as measuring only what the learned kernel adds on top.
  • Full-Asset ablation and identifiability panels. Was: two literal constant tables under measurement-sounding headings. Now: per-group parameter RMSE against the truth vector, and a real finite-difference sensitivity matrix.
  • 2D NLOS panels 3 and 4. Was: captioned “Inverse intermediate” and “Hidden reconstruction” while rendering the ground truth through a blur, with a confidence figure that was a closed form in two sliders. Now: relabelled as an illustrative expectation model that explicitly does not invert the measurement, and pointed at the 3D NLOS showpiece where the executable operators live.

Why more lobes need not identify a better material

On several of these presets the residual is flat across lobe counts, and the Gram condition number explodes above one lobe. That is the honest answer rather than a failed solve: a grid search over widely separated widths confirms two distinct lobes do no better than one. A mirror-centred GGX cannot reach a secondary lobe that sits at an offset from the mirror direction, so no arrangement of widths recovers it, and the large remaining residual is what the learned kernel is then for. The old hand-placed base could not show this because it was constructed to look right. Lobes are GGX projected onto the in-plane slice, not Gaussians in the mirror offset. That matters: the synthetic target is a sum of Gaussians in that offset, so fitting Gaussians would be a model-matches-generator cheat and every information criterion computed from it would be meaningless. The held-out band is contiguous rather than a random subset, because random holdout leaks through neighbouring samples under a smooth kernel. The fitted lobe count is a model-selection verdict under an assumed Gaussian noise model, not a claim that N physical lobes exist, and parameters are identifiable only up to lobe permutation. Where the Gram condition number exceeds 1e4 the reported weights are not meaningful individually even though the total residual is lower.

2D Gaussian splatting, and why splats do not relight

Anisotropic Gaussians composited front to back, fitted by a hand-derived analytic adjoint. The population is not fixed: splats whose centre the loss keeps pulling are cloned if small and split if large, and anything that goes transparent is pruned. That adaptive density control is the part that is actually the contribution of Gaussian splatting — rendering ellipses was never the hard bit.

Press "Relight the recovery" after fitting. A splat carries a colour and no notion of a light, so whatever shading was present when it was fitted is baked into per-splat colour and the recovery simply does not change.

Scope. This is the two-dimensional case: no 3D covariance, no view-dependent spherical harmonics, no camera. Depth is an ordering key for compositing, not a coordinate, and the adjoint carries no term for it. Gaussians are cut at three sigma, which is a real approximation. The adjoint itself is checked against central differences to a worst relative error of 4e-9 across every parameter type, so the gradient is not the uncertain part.

On whether densification is worth it, measured rather than assumed: against a fixed population of the same final size, adaptive density control reaches a higher loss at equal iteration count. What it buys is cheap early iterations, so it only wins on wall clock — about 2.8x better at an eight-second budget, and a tie by twenty seconds. At this scale, with a simple target, knowing the right splat count in advance is worth more than placing them adaptively. That is not the regime the technique was designed for, and the honest reading is that this lane demonstrates the mechanism rather than vindicating it.

solving

Why the single edge collapses, provably rather than empirically. Its visibility test is a comparison against where the ray crosses the aperture plane in x, and that crossing depends on azimuth alone. So two columns of the operator that share an azimuth are exactly proportional, every elevation difference lands exactly in the null space, and the rank is capped at the azimuth count — 16 of 256 here. The test asserts this to 3e-17, and separately measures a second cap nobody asks about: at a coarser sensor the rank falls to 9 rather than 12, because azimuth bins whose cuts land between two adjacent sensor columns produce identical rows. Sampling the hidden scene more finely than the sensor can distinguish buys nothing.

Full rank is not the same as a well posed problem. Two crossed edges, the finite occluder and the random mask all reach rank 256 — they can represent any hidden scene — and they are still far apart on how many directions survive a 1% noise floor: 74, 70 and 129. A panel that reported only rank would call these three equivalent. The undesigned random mask beating both structured apertures by nearly 2× is a lower bound on what aperture design buys, since it is not even a MURA.

The regularizer cannot buy an axis, and it is worth seeing that separately. Sweeping the smoothness weight across five thousandfold never lights up the edge's blind direction; the best setting reaches ZNCC 0.66 and over-regularizing gets worse rather than plateauing. More pointedly: the edge's recovered elevation profile does change a little between two different hidden scenes — by 4.9e-5 — and turning the prior off collapses that to 1.1e-13. Every visible trace of elevation in the regularized picture was put there by the prior, not by the data.

Scope. The hidden scene is directional: it lives at infinity and is parameterized only by direction, so there is no range axis and none is claimed. Occlusion is binary, the aperture is a single plane at a known position, and there is no time of flight, no transport and no wavelength — this is a visibility experiment. The anchor for the higher-dimensional version is Baradad et al. 2018, Inferring Light Fields from Shadows, which recovers a 4D light field from the shadow a known occluder casts on a diffuse wall by inverting exactly this kind of linear system.

And here is where coding finally earns its place. The question is only near or far at a blur magnitude the estimator is told, so chance is 0.5. Three conditions are each necessary and none is sufficient, which is why the table has four rows and only one of them works.

An asymmetric aperture. Rays from a near point cross before the sensor and from a far point after it, so the aperture is imaged rotated by 180 degrees between the two cases. A round aperture and an annulus are their own rotation, so their two point spread functions are bit-identical — the test asserts a difference of exactly zero — and they sit at exactly 0.500 in every row no matter what else changes.

A non-Gaussian scorer. This was the surprise. Rotating a real point spread function by 180 degrees conjugates its Fourier transform, which leaves the magnitude untouched — measured at 3.6e-16 for the coded mask, whose two blurs plainly differ to the eye. A zero-mean Gaussian scene model gives a likelihood that depends on the aperture only through that magnitude, so the exact likelihood used everywhere else in this lane is structurally blind to the sign for every aperture. The cue lives entirely in the phase.

Read the near-chance cells as exactly chance rather than as measurements. The two hypotheses there are not hard to separate, they are mathematically identical, so their scores are usually bit-identical in double precision and where they are not the ordering falls to summation rounding. Two independent symptoms of that: the same cell reads differently under Node and in a browser from identical code, and it moved again when the trial seeding was changed without touching any physics. Neither reading carries information. The rounding-independent claim is the 3.6e-16 magnitude equality above.

A non-Gaussian scene. Swapping in a sparse-gradient scorer is still not enough, because a Gaussian field has Gaussian gradients however its spectrum is shaped — there is no sparsity to grip. Only with piecewise-constant scenes and a hyper-Laplacian score does the sign come back, at 0.927. Remove any one of the three and it returns to chance.

Scope. Monochrome, geometric optics, no diffraction and no chromatic aberration. Scenes for the scale experiment are drawn from exactly the 1/f Gaussian model the estimator assumes, which makes the likelihood exact and isolates the aperture as the only difference; real photographs are not that.

solving

And the shape dependence, which is why the paper's actual contribution is recovering drop geometry by minimising surface tension plus gravitational potential energy rather than assuming a sphere. Every recovered ray direction comes from Snell's law at a surface whose curvature was assumed rather than observed, so a shape error becomes a ray error becomes a depth error — amplified about sixfold at a 1% radius error, and worse as the error grows, so it is not a fixed gain that could be calibrated away once.

Scope, and it is large. A two-dimensional cross-section: the window is a line, drops are circular arcs, the sensor is one-dimensional. The drop is modelled as a spherical cap, the zero-gravity equilibrium shape — real drops are gravity-flattened and teardrop-like, and the sensitivity above is exactly what says how much that matters. Correspondence is given rather than solved, which in practice is most of the difficulty. The glass slab is ignored, since two parallel refractions offset a ray without turning it. No Fresnel weighting, no dispersion, no absorption — geometry only.

layered appearance

A coat over a base is not two lobes

One physical stack — a rough dielectric coat of thickness d and extinction sigma over a base of albedo rho — evaluated three ways. Naive lobe addition is what an inverse solver reaches for by default. Statistical adding-doubling transports energy and directional variance in closed form. The position-free stochastic walk is unbiased under the plane-parallel assumption, so it is the reference the other two are scored against rather than a third opinion.

What naive addition omits: internal reflection between the two interfaces, which is a per-channel geometric series rather than a tint; the 1/eta² solid-angle compression on the way in and back out; the roughness convolution that makes apparent base roughness a property of the whole stack; and absorption that depends on the refracted path length. Measured here, an albedo ratio of 4.00 between channels arrives as a response ratio of 6.74, and sweeping the true coat roughness inflates the recovered base roughness by about 1.5x while the true base never moves.

Boundaries. The statistical operator is an approximation: it degrades above coat roughness around 0.3 and over-blurs at grazing angles, which is why the stochastic reference is shown beside it rather than trusted implicitly — its visible scatter is a property of the estimator, not a rendering artifact. Both are plane-parallel: opposing-anisotropy stacks, non-plane-parallel geometry, spatially varying stacks, spectral rendering and polarization are not modelled. The naive baseline here is reciprocal, unlike the Weidlich-Wilkie construction it resembles; its failures are the ones measured above, not a reciprocity violation. And the held-out gap is real but modest at these settings — the literature reports far larger jumps on richer stacks, and the number shown is the one this lab measures.

research map

What the demo is actually comparing

The scene is deliberately small: a photo-sized target is matched by three differentiable ellipse primitives with color, opacity, position, radius, and rotation. That keeps the math visible while still showing the core inverse-rendering loop: render, compare, differentiate or search, then update the scene.

Autodiff

The tape records scalar operations and backpropagates through a stochastic pixel batch. It is flexible and close to modern differentiable renderers, but tape memory and visibility discontinuities become the hard parts.

Analytic

The same renderer has a hand-written adjoint for every primitive. It is usually faster and cleaner for this parameterization, but each new BRDF, visibility event, or geometry class needs careful derivation.

SPSA

Simultaneous perturbation estimates a useful gradient with two renders, no matter how many parameters exist. It is noisy, but it becomes attractive for black-box renderers and large parameter vectors.

Gradient-free

The evolution-strategy lane samples candidate scenes and keeps elites. It parallelizes beautifully and survives nonsmooth objectives, but spends far more render evaluations as dimensions grow.

Pixel L2 is the baseline. Research systems add mask loss, gradient/edge loss, perceptual loss, multi-scale pyramids, normal consistency, eikonal terms, BRDF priors, and relighting losses.

Data regime

Single image is most ambiguous. Multi-view reduces geometry uncertainty. Multi-light or OLAT capture is the real unlock for separating albedo, roughness, specular response, and illumination.

Estimator choice

Soft rasterization is biased but stable. Edge sampling and reparameterization handle visibility. Path replay and differential tracking make physically based gradients usable for path and volume transport.

Regularization

The inverse problem needs taste: smoothness where physics wants smoothness, sparsity where assets should be simple, priors where the image evidence is underdetermined, and uncertainty where answers are non-unique.

Benchmarking

Good evaluation asks whether the result survives new cameras and new lights. Stanford-ORB, OpenIllumination, controlled lighting datasets, and urban multi-illumination sets are more informative than training-view PSNR alone.

Browser plan

Keep the visible app interactive: small live demos in WebGPU, heavier benchmark presets as queued WASM/GPU jobs, and exported configs that can be run by Mitsuba, Dr.Jit, redner, or native research code.

Textures are practical now

Old demos often optimized color per vertex or per primitive. Newer pipelines optimize UV-space or surface-attached texture maps for albedo and SVBRDF parameters, usually with regularization and priors so the result relights instead of just matching pixels.

Shape, material, lighting

The hard part is disentanglement: many combinations produce the same image. Modern systems combine multi-view capture, lighting changes, physical constraints, learned priors, and differentiable visibility to separate geometry, BRDF, and illumination.

Gaussian assets

3D and 2D Gaussian approaches make reconstruction fast and detailed, but relighting needs more than baked color. The frontier adds spatially varying material attributes, ray tracing, and indirect illumination.

Volumetric path

Volumes are differentiable-friendly in topology but expensive in transport. Useful targets include density, emission, absorption, scattering, and phase parameters; serious gradients need careful free-flight and path sampling estimators.

WebGPU

Per-pixel rendering, residuals, reductions, candidate batches, raster primitives, SDF fields, and analytic gradient kernels are natural GPU work. Dynamic tape construction is less GPU-friendly unless the graph is compiled ahead of time.

WebAssembly option

WASM is the pragmatic lane for CPU SIMD, image preprocessing, small autodiff tapes, custom solvers, and browsers without WebGPU. It is also easier to share code with native renderers.

Hybrid path

A serious version would keep render and loss kernels on WebGPU, compile analytic or generated adjoints where possible, and use WASM for optimizer orchestration, image IO, and fallback kernels.
