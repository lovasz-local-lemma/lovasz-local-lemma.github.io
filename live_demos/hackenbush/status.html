What this engine does — and what it doesn’t

An honest account of the combinatorial game theory behind Hackenbush Lab: the parts that are exact and tested, the parts that are heuristic or approximate, and the parts that are genuinely out of reach. The book to read alongside it is Winning Ways for Your Mathematical Plays (Berlekamp, Conway & Guy).

← back to the lab

Read this first: it is not a general solver

This is a practical tool for small and medium Red-Blue-Green Hackenbush positions, not a complete combinatorial-game engine. It computes exact answers within a budget by recursively unfolding game trees. That recursion is exponential in the worst case: green edges (playable by either side) and stable “no-fall” edges multiply the branching, and around a dozen live edges a single tangled component can already become slow — or, on adversarial inputs, take effectively forever. The heaviest curated positions (the Boss tier) solve off the main thread in a Web Worker, behind a Solve button with a live state counter and a Cancel button — but every other analysis, including each edit in Create and each move in Play, still runs synchronously on the main thread, so a hard position you build yourself can still freeze the page. Finite values are reduced to canonical form (domination plus reversible-option bypass), so what is displayed is the game’s simplest name; some theoretical refinements (deep transfinite arithmetic, a full thermal solver, misère quotients) remain deliberately not implemented. The sections below say exactly where the lines are.

The game

Hackenbush is played on a graph of colored edges standing on the ground (a baseline). On your turn you cut one edge of a color you control; any edges left disconnected from the ground then fall away.

  • Blue edges are cut only by Blue (Conway’s Left).
  • Red edges are cut only by Red (Right).
  • Green edges are cut by either player (impartial).
  • An edge survives only while it has a path to a grounded node. After a cut, ordinary edges with no remaining support fall.
  • app extension Stable edges are a Hackenbush-Lab addition: anchor edges that never fall, so they can hold otherwise-unsupported structure in place. They are not part of classical Hackenbush.

The player who cannot move loses (normal play). The whole point of the theory is to assign each position a value that says who wins, by how much, and how the position behaves when added to others.

The theory we use

Every position is read as a partizan game G = { Blue options | Red options } and analyzed recursively. The concepts below are the ones the engine actually computes; each is annotated with where it sits in the literature.

Game values, outcomes & order

Positions are compared with Conway’s order relations (G ≥ H defined recursively), which yields the four outcome classes (Left wins / Right wins / first player wins / second player wins). Winning Ways, Ch. 1–2; On Numbers and Games, Conway.

Surreal numbers (cold positions)

When a position is a genuine number — every Blue option strictly below every Red option, and all options themselves numbers — the engine extracts the exact dyadic/surreal value via the simplicity (“simplest number”) rule, and can show the day-by-day construction and birthday. Crucially, it does not pretend every position is a number: hot and fuzzy positions are shown as their raw short-game forms instead. Winning Ways, Ch. 2 (“Whose Game?”).

Disjunctive sums & components

Independent grounded subgraphs are solved separately and combined as a disjunctive sum, which keeps many real positions tractable. This decomposition is transfinite-aware (it carries infinite components through the sum). Winning Ways, Ch. 2.

Nimbers & Sprague–Grundy

Green “bamboo” stalks are impartial: the engine computes their nim-values with the mex rule and recognizes *n symbolically. Winning Ways, Ch. 3 (“Nim”); Sprague–Grundy.

Infinitesimals

The named infinitesimals that show up next to numbers are recognized for display: star *, up/down ↑ ↓, ↑*, double-up/down ⇑ ⇓, and tiny(n)/miny(n). Winning Ways, Ch. 3–4 (“Uppitiness”).

Switches, temperature & thermography

Hot positions of the form {a | b} with a > b are detected as switches; for the cases it recognizes directly the engine reports the mean value and temperature and draws a thermograph (a frozen vertical wall for cold numbers, a V for switches). This is the engine’s weakest area — see the partial list. Winning Ways, Ch. 6 (“Heating, Cooling…”).

Transfinite values (infinite edges)

Edges flagged infinite stand for unbounded stalks. From the edge topology the engine pattern-matches ordinal values ω, -ω, ω±n, ω·n, ω², and combines parallel infinite green “grass” into ω*[n] by a stalk-count rule. This is heuristic pattern-matching, not general ordinal arithmetic — it is deliberately guarded by negative regression tests so it does not over-fire (e.g. two bridged ω’s must not collapse to plain ω). ONAG (ordinals as games); transfinite Hackenbush.

Loopy games

Edges flagged loop invoke loopy values: on, off, over, under, dud, with real arithmetic for sums, comparison, outcome and display. This covers the five named values and their shifts, not the whole of Conway’s loopy theory. Winning Ways, Ch. 11 (“Survival in the Lost World”).

Canonical position hashing

To avoid re-solving isomorphic subpositions, the memo keys positions by a Weisfeiler–Lehman color-refinement hash (~17× state reduction on symmetric positions). Note: this de-duplicates graph-isomorphic positions; it is not the same as reducing a game to canonical form by domination and reversibility (see below).

  • ✓

Partial & approximate use with care

These work in common cases but have documented gaps or fall back to weaker answers:

  • ⚠
    Multi-infinite mixed with finite edges in one component — bails the shallow heuristic and falls back to general recursion; may return a general game form rather than a clean transfinite number.
  • ⚠
    Mixed-color infinite “flowers” — tagged approximate; move-by-move scoring is suppressed.
  • ⚠
    ω² detection — only a strict trunk + branches topology; ω²·n, ω²+ω are not derived.
  • ⚠
    Hot positions with infinitesimal options — reported as {X|X}-style switches with temperature 0 — no clean V-shape thermograph. This is the engine’s thinnest area.
  • ⚠
    Loopy game arithmetic — five named values + shifts; doesn’t capture every subtle case (e.g. {on|over} falls back to dominance).
  • ✓
    Canonical form (domination + reversibility) — applied to every finite short game. Dominated options are removed and reversible options are bypassed, so the displayed value is the game’s unique simplest name — the worst label in the curated library fell from 4,024 characters to 17, and forms like { * | * } now print as the 0 they always were. Verified by an invariant run (no outcome and no search size changed anywhere), by fuzzing G + (−G) = 0, and by over-simplification guards ({1|1} stays {1|1}). Loopy and transfinite games are left untouched — the theory does not cover them — and are badged red Heuristic, since they were pattern-matched rather than derived in the first place. Measured across all 98 loadable positions the badges now come out 9 Proved, 78 Exact, 11 Heuristic and zero Not simplified: the amber level survives only as a fail-open signal, for the case where the simplifier hits its iteration guard and returns an unreduced form rather than a wrong one.
  • ⚠
    Performance on dense post-infinite-cut positions — off-thread for Boss-tier positions; everywhere else still synchronous, so large chains show an in-board shimmer and can still feel slow.

Why it’s not a general engine

Three honest constraints, in order of how often they bite:

  1. Exponential search. Exact analysis unfolds the game tree. Green edges double the moves available at each node, and stable edges keep more structure alive to branch on. The README puts it plainly: pure cold blue/red positions stay practical longest; green and stable edges enlarge the space sharply; and “around a dozen live edges, exact recursive search can already become expensive unless the position splits into independent grounded components.” Some inputs simply do not finish in reasonable time.
  2. It can still block the page. Boss-tier positions were moved into a Web Worker, so those solve without freezing and can be cancelled. Everything else — each edit you make in Create, each move in Play, Auto mode, the self-test — is still synchronous JavaScript on the UI thread. A hard position you build yourself shows an in-board shimmer, but the tab can stutter or freeze until it resolves, and there is no timeout that guarantees a fast bail.
  3. Some theory is heuristic or skipped. Transfinite values are pattern-matched from topology, not derived by general ordinal arithmetic (and are fenced in by negative tests precisely because a naive matcher over-fires). Canonical reduction by domination and reversibility is applied to every finite short game, but loopy and transfinite values are pattern-matched and left unreduced. The thermal machinery has never actually been exercised: no position anywhere in this app reaches a positive temperature (see the testing section), so “handles simple switches” is untested rather than proven. For anything outside the recognized patterns, the engine returns an honest general game form (or, for a few mixed-infinite shapes, an explicitly approximate result) rather than a false clean value.

The design choice throughout is honesty over false precision: when the engine cannot prove a clean value it says so — showing a raw short-game form, an approximate tag, or a fuzzy outcome — instead of guessing a tidy number. That is also why it is best described as a workbench for small and medium exact studies, not a general-purpose Hackenbush solver.

How we check it (and where coverage is thin)

An in-app regression harness (the Run Self-Test button) currently runs 88 cases across 14 categories, asserting on the analyzer’s computed value fields. Coverage is uneven — deliberately heaviest where bugs have bitten, lightest exactly where the theory is weakest:

Two caveats worth stating. These in-browser checks are separate from the npm test suite, which adds 137 Node tests (165 with HB_SLOW=1, which re-solves every curated position instead of the cheap subset) covering the engine primitives, canonical form, the Fusion Principle, misère play, and locking every curated position's winner against a golden file. Most importantly, Hot and Composites have only two cases each, and one Hot check passes on a loose condition — so the thermal/switch behavior is the part you should trust least.

And it did not survive checking. Measuring every position in the app found that not one has a positive temperature: all seven “Hot Games”, and 3,398 randomly generated mixed boards, come out at temperature 0. Several positions had been labelled “±1”, “switch” or “hot” purely because their value printed with braces — but { X | X } is tepid, not a switch, and the partisan options here keep resolving to infinitesimals. Those labels have been corrected to state the winner instead, and a test now refuses any entry that advertises heat the solver cannot find. Whether Blue-Red-Green Hackenbush can be hot at these sizes is left open; what is certain is that nothing in this library is.
