Rendering Measures: Direction, Surface, Volume, and Path Space

The one rule

A Monte Carlo estimator is only dimensionally meaningful when the integrand and the PDF are expressed with respect to the same measure.

\[ I = \int_\Omega f(x)\,d\mu(x), \qquad X \sim p_\mu(x)\,d\mu(x), \qquad \widehat I = {f(X) \over p_\mu(X)} . \]

If a sample is generated in a different coordinate system or with respect to a different measure \(d\nu\), the PDF must be converted. The conversion is the Jacobian:

\[ p_\mu(x) = p_\nu(\phi(x)) \left|{d\nu \over d\mu}\right|. \]

Core idea

There is no objectively "extra" term. There is only a term that has not yet been moved into the same measure as the estimator.

The term may live in the integrand, the PDF conversion, or a geometry factor, depending on which form of the integral you choose.

Measure space to Monte Carlo estimator

The missing piece in the compact formula is that a PDF is not a free-floating object. It is a density of one measure with respect to another measure. Once that is explicit, importance sampling is just a change of measure.

Measure space first, probability second

A measure space is the minimal structure needed before the phrase "size of a set" means anything:

\[ (\Omega,\mathcal F,\mu), \qquad \mu:\mathcal F\to[0,\infty]. \]

\(\Omega\) is the set of possible outcomes or integration points. \(\mathcal F\) is the collection of subsets that are allowed to be measured; these subsets are the measurable events. \(\mu\) assigns size to those events. The two core rules are nonnegativity and countable additivity:

\[ \mu(\emptyset)=0, \qquad A_i\cap A_j=\emptyset \Rightarrow \mu\!\left(\bigcup_i A_i\right) = \sum_i \mu(A_i). \]

A probability measure is just a measure whose total mass is one:

\[ P(\Omega)=1, \qquad \Pr(A)=P(A),\quad A\in\mathcal F . \]

That is the direct answer to "how does a probability measure give you a probability?" It is a function that takes an event \(A\) and returns a number in \([0,1]\), with the normalization and additivity rules that probabilities must obey. A PDF is only a convenient way to describe \(P\) when \(P\) has a density against some base measure.

Probability from a measure

\[ P(A)=\int_A dP. \]

This form works for discrete, continuous, mixed, and singular distributions. It asks for the mass of the event \(A\).

Probability from a density

\[ dP(x)=p_\mu(x)\,d\mu(x), \qquad P(A)=\int_A p_\mu(x)\,d\mu(x). \]

This form works only after the base measure \(\mu\) is named. The density \(p_\mu\) is not itself the probability of \(x\); probabilities belong to sets.

Random variables push probabilities forward

A random variable is a measurable function out of the sample space. It does not have to be the sample itself:

\[ X:\Omega\to S, \qquad P_X(B)=P(X^{-1}(B)). \]

\(P_X\) is the distribution, or law, of \(X\). In rendering, a path sampler might generate a large transcript \(\omega\in\Omega\), while the random variable is the contribution value \(Y(\omega)\), the sampled direction, the path length, the hit light id, or a visibility event. Expectations are integrals over the probability measure:

\[ \mathbb E[X] = \int_\Omega X(\omega)\,dP(\omega) = \int_S s\,dP_X(s). \]

Why do we need \(\mathcal F\)?

This is one of the places where books often feel more formal than helpful. The point of \(\mathcal F\) is not to make probability look complicated. It is the contract that says which set-questions the measure is willing to answer. A measure does not have to assign a size to every imaginable subset of \(\Omega\); it assigns size to the events in \(\mathcal F\).

In a finite space, the contract can usually be "all subsets." For a die, every subset of \(\{1,\ldots,6\}\) is measurable. Continuous spaces are different: if we demand all the properties we want from length, area, or probability (nonnegative size, countable additivity, translation-like invariance, and sane interval sizes), then arbitrary subsets are too wild. The usual fix is to use a sigma-algebra such as the Borel sets, often completed by adding subsets of null sets. That includes essentially every set a renderer intentionally asks about: intervals, pixels, cones, surface patches, voxels, time bins, path length ranges, and finite or countable combinations of those.

The closure rules are practical. If \(A\) is a valid event, then "not \(A\)" should also be valid. If \(A_1,A_2,\ldots\) are valid events, then "one of these events happened" should be valid too. Countable additivity is the rule that makes disjoint bins, strata, light selections, path classes, and temporal buckets add up to the same total measure.

Common confusion What is actually happening Rendering translation
"Why not measure every subset?" In finite spaces you can. In continuous spaces, arbitrary subsets cannot all be given a sane length or probability while preserving the usual rules. \(\mathcal F\) names the subsets that are allowed. Real renderers almost always live inside Borel/completed events: patches, rays, cones, distances, pixels, and path families.
"Is \(\mathcal F\) the support?" No. \(\mathcal F\) is the family of measurable sets. Support is where a particular measure or density can place mass. A BSDF PDF may have support on glossy directions, but the measurable events are still direction sets such as caps, bins, or their combinations.
"Why must a random variable be measurable?" To ask \(P(X\in B)\), the preimage \(X^{-1}(B)\) must itself be an event in \(\mathcal F\). Otherwise the distribution of \(X\) is not defined. A contribution value, path length, hit-light id, or visibility flag must be an event you can score under the sampler's probability space.
"Why can point probability be zero?" Probabilities belong to sets. Under a continuous measure, single points are measurable but often have zero mass. A point matters only if the measure has a singular component there. Delta lights, specular chains, and exact constraints are not ordinary area/solid-angle/volume densities. They need singular or mixed measures.
"Why does a PDF need a base measure?" A PDF is a conversion rate: \(dP=p_\mu\,d\mu\). Without \(d\mu\), the units and the event sizes are missing. The same light sample has different numerical PDFs per unit area, per unit solid angle, per unit distance, or in path space.
"Do null sets never matter?" They do not change an integral against the same smooth measure, but they can matter after changing to a singular measure, differentiating a discontinuity, or conditioning on a lower-dimensional event. Visibility edges, specular manifolds, photon beams/surfaces, and deltas are often invisible to one measure but visible to another.

Rendering intuition: \(\mathcal F\) is the legal vocabulary of path questions. "Does the path hit this pixel?", "does the light sample land on this patch?", "is the free-flight distance in this interval?", and "does this transcript contain a null collision?" are events. A PDF, MIS weight, or expected contribution only makes sense after those events and their base measure are named.

The full setup

Start with a measurable space \((\Omega,\mathcal F)\), a target measure \(\mu\), and an integrable function \(f\). The exact quantity is

\[ I = \int_\Omega f(x)\,d\mu(x). \]

To sample, choose a probability measure \(P\) on the same measurable space. If \(P\) has density \(p_\mu\) with respect to \(\mu\), then

\[ dP(x)=p_\mu(x)\,d\mu(x), \qquad \int_\Omega p_\mu(x)\,d\mu(x)=1. \]

Now the algebra becomes a probability statement:

\[ I = \int_\Omega {f(x)\over p_\mu(x)}\,dP(x) = \mathbb E_{X\sim P} \left[{f(X)\over p_\mu(X)}\right], \]

and the \(N\)-sample estimator is

\[ \widehat I_N = {1\over N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} {f(X_i)\over p_\mu(X_i)}, \qquad X_i\sim P. \]

What was missing?

The expression \(p(x)\,d\mu(x)\) is the probability measure being sampled. The density \(p\) has units of \(1/\mu\), so \(f/p\) has the same units as the original integral.

The identity is valid only where the sampler has support: \(p_\mu(x)>0\) for every contributing region, up to measure-zero exceptions. For useful Monte Carlo, the second moment also has to be finite.

General change of measure

\[ I=\int_\Omega f\,d\mu = \int_\Omega f\,{d\mu\over dP}\,dP. \]

The factor \(d\mu/dP\) is the Radon-Nikodym derivative. In the usual PDF case, \(d\mu/dP=1/p_\mu\). This is the clean measure-theory version of dividing by the PDF.

Mixtures and delta events

\[ P = w_s P_{\mathrm{surface}} +w_d P_{\mathrm{delta}} +w_v P_{\mathrm{volume}}. \]

A sampler can mix densities over surfaces, directions, distances, and discrete events. Then the "PDF" is really a density with respect to a mixed measure, not always a single ordinary function over Euclidean volume.

A familiar version: \(f(x)\,d(g(x))\)

A good way to make the measure idea feel less exotic is the notation \(\int f(x)\,d(g(x))\). This is a Riemann-Stieltjes or Lebesgue-Stieltjes style integral. The function \(g\) defines the measuring stick. If \(g\) is differentiable and increasing, then

\[ d(g(x)) = g'(x)\,dx, \qquad \int_a^b f(x)\,d(g(x)) = \int_a^b f(x)\,g'(x)\,dx. \]

So \(d(2x)\) is not a mysterious new variable. It is simply a measure that assigns twice as much size to every interval:

\[ \int_0^1 f(x)\,d(2x) = 2\int_0^1 f(x)\,dx. \]

Why does the matching differential feel automatic? Because \(\int f(x)\,dx\) is shorthand for "integrate the function written in the \(x\)-coordinate against the standard length measure in the \(x\)-coordinate." A non-matching differential is meaningful only after it names a measure on the same domain, such as \(d(g(x))\), or after you give a map between coordinates. Without that map, \(\int f(x)\,dy\) is incomplete notation.

Same target, renamed variable, or new measure?

The slippery part is that \(x\) and \(y\) can mean three different things in informal calculus notation:

Operation Example Same target? What must happen?
Dummy-variable rename \(\int_0^1 x^2\,dx=\int_0^1 y^2\,dy\) Yes. Only the bound variable name changed. Domain and measure are identical.
Coordinate substitution \(y=2x\), so \(x=y/2\) Yes, if transformed correctly. Transform the bounds, compose the function, and include the Jacobian.

Integral against an unspecified differential \(\int_{x\in[0,1]} f(x)\,dy\) Not well-formed yet. Define \(y=g(x)\), or define a measure \(dy\) on the \(x\)-domain.

For a real coordinate change, the safe version is:

\[ I = \int_{A_x} f_x(x)\,dx, \qquad y=g(x),\quad x=h(y), \] \[ I = \int_{g(A_x)} f_x(h(y))\,|h'(y)|\,dy. \]

So if \(A_x=[0,1]\) and \(y=2x\), the same target is

\[ \int_0^1 f_x(x)\,dx = \int_0^2 f_x\!\left({y\over2}\right) {1\over2}\,dy . \]

This is the precise version of your sentence "the range in \(x\) expressed in \(y\)." The range changed from \([0,1]\) to \([0,2]\), and the density of \(x\)-length inside \(y\)-length is \(dx/dy=1/2\). If someone writes \(\int_0^2 f(y)\,dy\) and means the same target, then their \(f(y)\) is not the old formula with \(x\) replaced by \(y\). It must already include the transformed density:

\[ f_y(y) = f_x\!\left({y\over2}\right){1\over2}, \qquad f_x(x)\,dx = f_y(y)\,dy . \]

That last equality is the grown-up invariant object. The bare function \(f\) is usually coordinate-dependent; the density \(f\,d\mu\) is what carries the integral.

Indefinite integrals are different

A definite integral asks for a number attached to a target measure and domain. A change of variables can preserve that number. An indefinite integral asks for an antiderivative with respect to a chosen variable:

\[ \int f(x)\,dx = F(x)+C, \qquad {dF\over dx}=f(x). \]

If \(y=2x\), then \(F(y)\) and \(F(x)\) are not the same function of the same underlying point. For \(f(t)=t^2\):

Indefinite expression Antiderivative As a function of \(x\), with \(y=2x\) Derivative with respect to \(x\)
\(\int x^2\,dx\) \(x^3/3+C\) \(x^3/3+C\) \(x^2\)
\(\int y^2\,dy\) \(y^3/3+C\) \(8x^3/3+C\) \(8x^2\)
\(\int (y/2)^2\,{dy\over2}\) \(y^3/24+C\) \(x^3/3+C\) \(x^2\)
\(\int f(2x)\,d(2x)\) \((2x)^3/3+C\) \(8x^3/3+C\) \(8x^2\)

This is why indefinite integrals look "different" after substitution. There is no fixed pair of endpoints forcing the transformed expression to represent the same accumulated quantity. To preserve the same antiderivative as a function of the underlying point, you must transform the whole differential expression:

\[ x^2\,dx = \left({y\over2}\right)^2 {dy\over2} = {y^2\over8}\,dy . \]

If you instead write \(y^2\,dy\), you are integrating a different density. It is computable, but the target changed.

Common confusion distilled: a "non-matching" differential is not wrong once it has a meaning, but assigning it a meaning changes the measure unless you also transform the integrand and bounds to preserve the target. In rendering, this is why an area-sampled light has to be converted before being scored in a solid-angle integral: the sample is fine, but its density is written against a different measuring stick.

Rendering does the same thing, just in geometry instead of one-dimensional calculus:

\[ \int_{\Omega_x} F(\omega)\,d\omega = \int_{A_L} F(\omega(y))\, {|\cos\theta_y|\over \|y-x\|^2}\,dA(y). \]

The solid-angle differential \(d\omega\) and the area differential \(dA\) are both valid, but they measure different sets. The Jacobian is the conversion rate, exactly like \(d(2x)=2dx\), except now the rate varies with distance and angle.

Curve, surface, and volume integrals

Geometry adds one more layer of notation ambiguity. Sometimes \(ds,dA,dV\) mean positive size measures: curve length, surface area, volume. Sometimes the differential is an oriented object, such as \(d\mathbf r\), \(\mathbf n\,dA\), or \(dx\wedge dy\wedge dz\). The first kind counts "how much set." The second kind also remembers a direction or orientation, so it can project a field onto the local tangent, normal, or orientation.

This is the differential-form version. A 1-form integrates over a curve, a 2-form over a surface, and a 3-form over a volume. The wedge products encode the oriented coordinate parallelograms or parallelepipeds. In ordinary vector calculus notation, the 2-form above is the same information as the flux integrand \(F\cdot\mathbf n\,dA\).

So what is the volume analogue? There is no extra normal projection for a full-dimensional volume in its ambient space. A tiny volume element already spans all three local directions, so the oriented version is a volume form with a determinant sign. Normals appear when a volume talks to its boundary: \(\int_V \nabla\cdot F\,dV=\int_{\partial V}F\cdot\mathbf n\,dA\). In rendering terms, volume events use \(dV\) or distance times solid angle; cosine/normal projection terms appear when you cross a surface or convert a surface patch into a directional measure.

What if the PDF is forgotten?

Suppose the true target is still \(\int_0^1 x^2\,dx=1/3\), but samples are drawn as \(X=\sqrt U\). The density with respect to \(dx\) is \(p(x)=2x\). The correct estimator is \(X^2/(2X)=X/2\). If you instead average \(X^2\), you estimate \(\mathbb E_P[X^2]\), not the original integral:

\[ \mathbb E[X^2] = \int_0^1 x^2\,2x\,dx = {1\over2}. \]

That is exactly the same kind of error as sampling a light by surface area and treating it as though it had been sampled by solid angle. The sample is not wrong; the measure used to score the sample is wrong.

Connection to the Lebesgue integral

The Lebesgue integral is value-centric in exactly the useful way. For a simple function

\[ f(x)=\sum_k a_k\,\mathbf 1_{A_k}(x), \qquad \int f\,d\mu = \sum_k a_k\,\mu(A_k). \]

It says: take each value \(a_k\), multiply by the measure of the set on which that value occurs, and add. For nonnegative functions this becomes the limit of simple functions, or equivalently the layer-cake view:

\[ \int_\Omega f\,d\mu = \int_0^\infty \mu\bigl(\{x:f(x)>t\}\bigr)\,dt . \]

Monte Carlo does not change the target integral. It chooses a probability measure \(P\), turns the integral into the expectation of the weighted random variable \(Y=f(X)/p_\mu(X)\), and estimates the mean of that value distribution:

\[ I = \mathbb E_P[Y], \qquad Y={f(X)\over p_\mu(X)}. \]

This is why the measure viewpoint is so powerful for rendering. Direction, projected solid angle, surface area, volume, path vertices, discrete event labels, and delta constraints all say what "size of a set" means. A sampler is valid only after its probability measure is expressed against the same notion of size as the target integral.

How is a Lebesgue integral actually computed?

The definition is a limiting construction; practical computation is another matter. For nonnegative \(f\), the Lebesgue integral can be defined as the best lower approximation by simple functions:

\[ \int_\Omega f\,d\mu = \sup_{0\le s\le f,\ s\ \mathrm{simple}} \int_\Omega s\,d\mu, \qquad s=\sum_k a_k\mathbf 1_{A_k}. \]

For a signed function, split it into positive and negative parts:

\[ f=f^+-f^-, \qquad \int f\,d\mu = \int f^+\,d\mu-\int f^-\,d\mu, \]

provided the expression is not the undefined form \(\infty-\infty\). So the conceptual calculation is "approximate by value bins, measure the sets in each bin, then refine." Numerical methods differ mostly in how they approximate those measured sets or the equivalent expectation.

How close is Monte Carlo to Lebesgue integration?

Very close. Expectation is a Lebesgue integral on a probability space:

\[ \mathbb E[X] = \int_\Omega X(\omega)\,dP(\omega). \]

Monte Carlo is the numerical method that estimates this Lebesgue integral by samples. If \(Y\) is integrable and \(Y_i\) are independent samples with the same distribution, the law of large numbers says

\[ {1\over N}\sum_{i=1}^N Y_i \longrightarrow \mathbb E[Y] \quad\text{almost surely}. \]

To estimate a general Lebesgue integral \(\int f\,d\mu\), first rewrite it as an expectation. If \(\mu(\Omega)<\infty\), normalize \(\mu\):

\[ P(A)={\mu(A)\over \mu(\Omega)}, \qquad \int_\Omega f\,d\mu = \mu(\Omega)\,\mathbb E_{X\sim P}[f(X)]. \]

If \(\mu\) is not finite, or if uniform sampling is inefficient, choose any probability measure \(P\) that covers the contributing support. When \(dP=q\,d\mu\),

\[ \int_\Omega f(x)\,d\mu(x) = \int_\Omega {f(x)\over q(x)}\,dP(x) = \mathbb E_{X\sim P}\!\left[{f(X)\over q(X)}\right]. \]

That is not merely analogous to Lebesgue integration. It is the Radon-Nikodym change-of-measure identity, estimated by random samples.

How close are they? Expectation is a Lebesgue integral. Monte Carlo is a stochastic way to evaluate that integral after choosing a probability measure. The theory of Lebesgue integration tells you what the target means; probability tells you how samples are distributed; Monte Carlo tells you how the sample average converges to the target. They are not separate worlds.

One more trap: integrable does not mean low variance. The law of large numbers needs \(\mathbb E|Y|<\infty\), but useful error bars usually need finite variance \(\mathbb E[Y^2]<\infty\). A valid Lebesgue integral can still produce a brutal Monte Carlo estimator.

More Lebesgue machinery that is useful in rendering

Lebesgue theory becomes practical once you stop thinking of it as "a fancier Riemann integral" and start thinking of it as a language for comparing measures. The important objects are not just functions; they are functions together with the measure they are integrated against.

Almost-everywhere equality

\[ f=g\quad \mu\text{-a.e.} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \int f\,d\mu=\int g\,d\mu . \]

Point values do not matter under length, area, solid-angle, or volume measure. They do matter under a delta measure placed on that point.

Absolute continuity and singularity

\[ \nu\ll\mu \Rightarrow d\nu=\rho\,d\mu, \qquad \nu\perp\mu \Rightarrow \text{no ordinary }\rho . \]

If a measure is absolutely continuous with respect to another, it has a density. Delta lights, specular constraints, and surface measures inside volume space are usually singular with respect to the smooth measure you first wrote.

Direction sampling without myths

Direction sampling is not about making \(\theta\) look uniform. It is about choosing a probability measure on the sphere or hemisphere, then sampling coordinates whose Jacobian matches that measure.

With polar angle \(\theta\) measured from the normal or \(z\)-axis and azimuth \(\phi\), solid angle on the unit sphere is

\[ d\omega = \sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi. \]

If \(z=\cos\theta\), then \(dz=-\sin\theta\,d\theta\), so the same measure is simply

\[ d\omega = d\phi\,dz \quad\text{up to orientation.} \]

This is the whole trick. Uniform directions are uniform in solid angle, not uniform in \(\theta\). Sampling \(z=\cos\theta\) uniformly makes equal-height bands on the sphere equally likely.

The hat-box theorem view

The circumference of a latitude ring shrinks near the pole, but the surface becomes more slanted. Those two effects cancel. The area of a thin band between heights \(z\) and \(z+dz\) on the unit sphere is

\[ dA_{\mathrm{band}} = 2\pi\,dz, \qquad d\omega=dA_{\mathrm{unit\ sphere}}. \]

So the better story is not "sample \(\theta\) according to cosine." For a uniform sphere or hemisphere, the story is "sample height \(z=\cos\theta\) uniformly, sample azimuth \(\phi\) uniformly, and convert to a direction."

What if we sample \(\theta\) uniformly anyway?

It is allowed. It is just not uniform solid angle. Suppose we sample a hemisphere by choosing

\[ \theta\sim U(0,\pi/2), \qquad \phi\sim U(0,2\pi). \]

The coordinate density is constant: \(q_{\theta,\phi}=1/\pi^2\). But the rendering integral is usually against \(d\omega\), and

\[ d\omega = \sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi, \qquad p_\omega(\omega) = {q_{\theta,\phi}(\theta,\phi)\over \sin\theta} = {1\over \pi^2\sin\theta}. \]

So uniform-\(\theta\) sampling oversamples the pole and undersamples the horizon relative to equal solid-angle area. The pole density looks singular, but that does not mean a single direction has finite probability. It means a tiny polar cap gets too much probability compared with its area:

\[ P_{\theta\text{-uniform}}(0\le\theta\le\epsilon) = {2\epsilon\over\pi}, \qquad P_{\omega\text{-uniform}}(0\le\theta\le\epsilon) = 1-\cos\epsilon \approx {\epsilon^2\over2}. \]

This is the whole practical problem: uniform \(\theta\) assigns \(O(\epsilon)\) probability to an \(O(\epsilon^2)\) solid-angle cap. Near the normal it spends too many samples per unit spherical area; near the horizon it spends too few.

theta-uniform \(p_\omega\) solid-angle uniform

If we weight by the correct \(p_\omega\), the estimator is still unbiased:

\[ I = \int_{\mathcal H^2} f(\omega)\,d\omega, \qquad \widehat I_{\theta\text{-uniform}} = {f(\omega)\over p_\omega(\omega)} = \pi^2 f(\omega)\sin\theta. \]

The failure mode is usually not bias; it is bad allocation. For example, integrating \(f=1\) over the hemisphere should give \(2\pi\). Uniform solid angle gives the constant estimator \(2\pi\), hence zero variance. Uniform \(\theta\), correctly weighted, gives \(\pi^2\sin\theta\): still unbiased, but no longer constant. Uniform in which measure?

The phrase "uniform direction" is incomplete until the measure is named. Uniform solid angle means every equal-area patch on the unit sphere has equal probability. Uniform projected solid angle means every equal-area patch in the disk projection has equal probability. They are both useful, but they are not the same distribution:

\[ d\omega^\perp = \cos\theta\,d\omega, \qquad p_{\omega,\mathrm{cos}}(\omega) = {1\over \pi}{d\omega^\perp\over d\omega} = {\cos\theta\over\pi}. \]

That is why cosine-weighted sampling is so natural for diffuse reflection. The rendering equation has a \(\cos\theta\,d\omega\) factor. If the rest of the integrand is roughly constant, sampling uniformly in projected solid angle makes the weight nearly constant.

The local surface rendering equation is directional

At a fixed surface point \(x\), the classical surface rendering equation integrates incoming radiance over directions on the hemisphere:

\[ L_o(x,\omega_o) = L_e(x,\omega_o) + \int_{\mathcal H^2(x)} f_s(x,\omega_i,\omega_o)\, L_i(x,\omega_i)\, |\cos\theta_i|\, d\omega_i . \]

This equation is written in solid angle measure \(d\omega_i\) at \(x\). A light sample chosen as a position on an emitter is not naturally a \(d\omega_i\) sample. It is naturally a surface area sample \(dA(y)\). That is where the familiar conversion factor comes from.

BSDF, phase functions, and diffuse light

The cosine belongs to surfaces because a surface has an orientation. A volume scattering point has no projected surface area, so the phase-function integral has no matching cosine term.

Surface scattering: BSDF

A BSDF converts incident irradiance into outgoing radiance. The irradiance from one incoming direction is radiance times projected area:

\[ dE_i(x) = L_i(x,\omega_i)\,|n_x\cdot\omega_i|\,d\omega_i . \]

\[ dL_o = f_s(x,\omega_i,\omega_o)\,dE_i . \]

So the cosine is not secretly inside the BSDF. It appears because incoming radiance must be projected onto the receiving surface.

Volume scattering: phase function

A phase function is a directional redistribution density at a point in a medium. The scattering source term is usually written:

\[ L_s(x,\omega_o) = \sigma_s(x) \int_{\mathcal S^2} p(x,\omega_i,\omega_o)\, L_i(x,\omega_i)\, d\omega_i . \]

There is no surface normal and no projected area. The chance of interaction per unit distance is carried by \(\sigma_s\); the angular choice is carried by \(p\), with \(\int_{\mathcal S^2}p\,d\omega=1\).

Why does a diffuse light have a cosine?

An ideal diffuse area light is usually modeled as a Lambertian emitter: its emitted radiance \(L_e\) is constant over the outgoing hemisphere. Radiance is measured per unit projected area, so the power leaving a real patch \(dA\) into solid angle \(d\omega\) is

\[ L_e(x,\omega)=L_0, \qquad n_x\cdot\omega > 0. \]

If the light is also spatially uniform, this same \(L_0\) is used at every point on the emitting surface. The emitted flux is still not uniform over directions, because the real surface area must be projected:

\[ d\Phi = L_e(x,\omega)\,|n_x\cdot\omega|\,dA\,d\omega . \]

If \(L_e\) is constant, the emitted flux per direction is cosine-weighted:

\[ M = \int_{\mathcal H^2} L_e\,|n_x\cdot\omega|\,d\omega = \pi L_e, \qquad p(\omega)={|n_x\cdot\omega|\over \pi}. \]

Constant radiance and cosine-distributed photons are the same Lambertian model. If you sample emitted particles from a diffuse area light, cosine sampling matches constant emitted radiance. Uniform outgoing directions would instead represent a different flux distribution, or require compensating weights.

Your physical intuitions are all possible microscopic stories: a hot material can emit after absorption and thermalization, a rough glowing surface can have many tiny scattering events, and a cavity with small openings can produce nearly direction-independent radiance. Rendering usually abstracts those stories into a boundary condition: this surface emits \(L_e(x,\omega)\). For a diffuse light, \(L_e\) is chosen independent of \(\omega\), and the cosine appears only when converting that radiance into flux through the oriented surface.

What changes for a volume light?

A volume emitter adds radiance along distance, not through a surface aperture. A simple emission coefficient \(j(x,\omega)\) contributes

\[ dL(x,\omega)=j(x,\omega)\,ds. \]

If the volume emits isotropically, the direction distribution is uniform over the sphere:

\[ p(\omega)={1\over 4\pi}. \]

There is no emitter-side cosine because there is no emitter-side surface normal. But if that volume light illuminates a surface point \(x\), the receiver still has a projected-area term:

\[ dE(x) = T_r(x,y)\,j(y\!\to\!x)\, {|n_x\cdot\omega_{xy}| \over \|y-x\|^2}\,dV(y). \]

Volume light summary: no cosine at the emitting volume vertex; yes cosine at any receiving surface vertex. In path-space notation, this is the same rule as before: surface endpoint \(D=|n\cdot\omega|\), medium endpoint \(D=1\).

Surface change of measure

Let \(y\) be the surface point seen from \(x\) along direction \(\omega_{xy}\), with distance \(r=\|y-x\|\). A small patch \(dA(y)\) subtends a small solid angle at \(x\):

\[ d\omega_x = {|\cos\theta_y| \over r^2}\,dA(y), \qquad p_\omega(\omega_{xy}) = p_A(y)\,{r^2 \over |\cos\theta_y|}. \]

The inverse conversion is just as important:

\[ p_A(y) = p_\omega(\omega_{xy})\,{|\cos\theta_y| \over r^2}. \]

Same direct lighting, area form

\[ L_d = \int_{A_L} L_e(y\!\to\!x)\, f_s(x,\omega_{xy},\omega_o)\, V(x,y)\, {|\cos\theta_x|\,|\cos\theta_y| \over r^2} \,dA(y). \]

Sampling the light by area uses \(p_A(y)\) directly. The cosine-over-distance term is part of the integrand.

Same direct lighting, solid-angle form

\[ L_d = \int_{\Omega_L} L_e(y(\omega)\!\to\!x)\, f_s(x,\omega,\omega_o)\, V(x,y)\, |\cos\theta_x| \,d\omega . \]

Sampling the light by area now requires \(p_\omega=p_A r^2/|\cos\theta_y|\). The same geometry is paid for through the PDF conversion.

Volume: angle plus distance

In a medium, a direction alone does not determine the next interaction. You also need a distance, or equivalently a volume point. This is the local angle-distance measure:

\[ y = x + r\omega, \qquad dV(y) = r^2\,dr\,d\omega, \qquad dr\,d\omega = {1 \over r^2}\,dV(y). \]

So if a medium sample is generated by first sampling a direction with density \(p_\omega(\omega)\) and then a distance with density \(p_r(r)\), its density with respect to volume is

\[ p_V(y) = {p_\omega(\omega)\,p_r(r) \over r^2}. \]

If the segment ends on a surface instead of in a medium, the endpoint conversion uses the surface formula \(d\omega = |\cos\theta_y|\,dA/r^2\). In mixed surface-volume path integrals, each edge carries the same idea: convert from local ray variables to vertex measure.

Generalized transmittance

Classical transmittance is exponential because the medium is memoryless: extinction events along the ray behave like a local Poisson process. Generalized or non-exponential transmittance replaces that assumption with a more general survival law.

Classical Beer-Lambert survival

\[ T(s) = \exp\!\left(-\int_0^s \sigma_t(x+u\omega)\,du\right). \]

This composes cleanly: \(T(0,s_1+s_2)=T(0,s_1)\,T(s_1,s_1+s_2)\). The medium forgets how the ray arrived at the current point.

General survival function

\[ T(s)=P(S>s), \qquad f(s)=-{dT(s)\over ds}, \qquad h(s)={f(s)\over T(s)}. \]

\(f\) is the free-flight density and \(h\) is the hazard rate. In generalized transport, \(h\) can depend on flight age, origin type, local correlations, or unresolved geometry rather than only on a local \(\sigma_t(x)\).

Beer-Lambert linear decay long-tailed correlated medium

Why this is not "just another sigma"

For any smooth positive survival curve you can define a hazard \(h(s)=-d\log T(s)/ds\). That does not automatically make it the classical extinction coefficient. In classical transport, \(\sigma_t(x)\) is a local material property and the ray has no memory. In generalized transport, the effective rate may depend on distance since the last collision, whether the ray started at a boundary or at a medium event, or the statistics of unresolved blockers.

\[ T(s) = \max(1-s/d_0,0) \quad\Rightarrow\quad h(s)={1\over d_0-s}. \]

This linear example is surface-like: the survival probability can decay linearly over a beam footprint. Its hazard is not a constant material density; it changes with the remaining distance to the blocking structure.

Unbiased ray marching and debiasing

Ordinary ray marching is a deterministic quadrature of optical depth. It is useful and often beautiful, but biased. Modern unbiased ray-marching methods keep the marching flavor while adding stochastic correction terms.

Why ordinary marching is biased

\[ \tau=\int_0^d\sigma_t(x+s\omega)\,ds, \qquad T=e^{-\tau}. \]

A step estimate \(\tau_h\) gives \(T_h=e^{-\tau_h}\). If \(\tau_h\ne\tau\), this is quadrature bias. If \(\widehat\tau\) is random and unbiased, \(e^{-\widehat\tau}\) is still generally biased because the exponential is nonlinear.

Power-series correction

\[ e^{-\tau} = e^{-c} \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} {(-1)^n\over n!}(\tau-c)^n . \]

Choose \(c\) from a cheap march or control variate. Most samples evaluate the low-order term; rare higher-order terms correct the bias. U-statistics and stratified or comb-like samples reduce variance in the product estimates.

\[ \widehat T_{\mathrm{debiased}} = B_0 + \sum_{k=1}^{N} {B_k-B_{k-1}\over P(N\ge k)} . \]

The debiasing view is broader than transmittance: it says a biased estimator can be used as the first approximation, provided you can also sample a correction series whose expectation equals the missing bias. In transmittance, the biased estimator is often a ray march at some resolution.

Where this fits with null tracking: null tracking samples a survival process. Unbiased ray marching samples a correction to a quadrature-like estimate. Integral formulations show these are different Monte Carlo views of the same transmittance problem, and sometimes null scattering is best understood as a variance-reduction control variate rather than a required physical story.

Fast biased methods for participating media

Biased methods are not "wrong" methods. They spend error deliberately: fewer samples, lower-dimensional caches, screen-space assumptions, temporal reuse, or approximate scattering models. In real-time graphics and production lookdev, that trade is often exactly what you want.

The main bias knobs: step size, grid resolution, interpolation, filtering, shadow-map resolution, temporal reprojection, limited bounce count, approximate phase functions, and cached or learned lighting. Most fast methods combine several of these.

Deterministic ray marching

The basic fast estimator is just quadrature along the camera ray. For single scattering, the continuous integral is approximated by a fixed or adaptive set of samples:

\[ L_v \approx \sum_i T_{\mathrm{view}}(0,t_i)\, \sigma_s(x_i)\, p(\omega_l,\omega_o)\, L_l(x_i)\, T_{\mathrm{light}}(x_i)\, \Delta s_i . \]

A common implementation accumulates transmittance front-to-back:

\[ T_{i+1}=T_i\,e^{-\sigma_t(x_i)\Delta s_i}, \qquad C_{i+1}=C_i+T_i\,S(x_i)\,\Delta s_i . \]

Jitter, blue noise, interleaved sampling, temporal accumulation, adaptive step sizes, empty-space skipping, mipmapped density bounds, and early termination all improve speed or hide banding. They do not remove the quadrature bias; they make it acceptable.

When biased is the right choice

Use biased fast methods when

The medium is mostly a visual effect, frame time is tight, temporal stability is more important than strict expectation correctness, or the desired look is authored rather than measured.

Use unbiased or consistent methods when

You need predictive accuracy, reliable convergence, difficult multiple scattering, spectral effects, or a reference image for validating the fast path.

The trap: temporal accumulation and denoising can make a biased volume look converged. Always check step-size, grid-resolution, and history-length sweeps against a reference before trusting the energy.

Bias roadmap: correctness, blur, reuse, and correlation

The useful split is not simply biased versus unbiased. Ask four questions: what is the estimator's expectation, does the bias vanish under refinement, what visual shape does the remaining error have, and are the samples independent or correlated?

\[ \mathrm{bias}_N = E[\widehat I_N]-I, \qquad \mathrm{MSE}(\widehat I_N) = \mathrm{Var}(\widehat I_N)+\mathrm{bias}_N^2 . \]

Unbiased methods put all expected error into variance. Biased methods spend some error as a systematic displacement of the answer. Consistent methods may be biased at any finite setting, but the bias disappears as sample count and refinement parameters go to their limits. An inconsistent method converges cleanly to the wrong image.

Correlation is not the same as bias

Correlated samples are allowed. Stratification, QMC, MLT, path guiding, reservoir resampling, photon reuse, and temporal reuse all introduce dependence between samples or between pixels. The mean is still correct if the estimator samples the right distribution and uses the right weights. The variance story changes:

\[ \mathrm{Var}(\bar X_N) \approx {\sigma^2\over N} \left(1+2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\rho_k\right) = {\sigma^2\over N_{\mathrm{eff}}}. \]

Positive autocorrelation lowers the effective sample count and makes naive variance estimates too optimistic. Negative or low-discrepancy correlation can reduce visible error. The diagnostic question is therefore: does correlation only change variance, or did the algorithm also approximate the measure, PDF, visibility, support, or reconstruction?

Cross-field toolkit: transport, shape derivatives, and moving measures

Rendering is one member of a larger transport family. Neutron transport, kinetic theory, continuum mechanics, shape calculus, and statistics all study the same recurring problem: integrate a quantity over a measure, then change the measure, the domain, the sampling law, or the geometry.

Rendering / radiative transfer

\[ \omega\cdot\nabla L + \sigma_t L = \sigma_s\int_{\mathbb S^2}p(\omega'\!\to\!\omega)L(\omega')\,d\omega' + Q . \]

Radiance flows through space and direction. Extinction removes it, scattering redistributes it, and emission or source terms add it.

Neutron / particle transport

\[ {1\over v}{\partial \psi\over\partial t} + \hat\Omega\cdot\nabla\psi + \Sigma_t\psi = \int \Sigma_s(E',\hat\Omega'\!\to\!E,\hat\Omega)\psi'\,dE'\,d\hat\Omega' + q . \]

Angular flux \(\psi(r,E,\hat\Omega,t)\) is radiance with extra coordinates: energy, sometimes time, and reactions such as scattering, absorption, and fission.

The 1D Leibniz rule, with proof

The moving-boundary story starts in one dimension. Let \(I(t)=\int_{a(t)}^{b(t)} f(x,t)\,dx\). The complete derivative is

\[ {dI\over dt} = \int_{a(t)}^{b(t)}{\partial f\over\partial t}(x,t)\,dx +f(b(t),t)b'(t)-f(a(t),t)a'(t). \]

The proof is just "freeze the interval, then pay for the endpoints." Pick a fixed reference coordinate \(s\in[0,1]\) and write \(x(s,t)=a(t)+s(b(t)-a(t))\). Then

\[ I(t) = \int_0^1 f(x(s,t),t)\, (b(t)-a(t))\,ds . \]

Differentiate under the fixed integral:

\[ {dI\over dt} = \int_0^1 \left[ f_t(x,t)(b-a) +f_x(x,t)x_t(b-a) +f(x,t)(b'-a') \right]ds. \]

Now use \(dx=(b-a)ds\), \(x_t=a'+s(b'-a')\), and notice that

\[ f_x x_t+f\,{\partial x_t\over\partial x} = {\partial\over\partial x}(f x_t), \qquad {\partial x_t\over\partial x}={b'-a'\over b-a}. \]

So the moving part integrates to endpoint flux:

\[ \int_{a(t)}^{b(t)}{\partial\over\partial x}(f x_t)\,dx = f(b,t)b'-f(a,t)a'. \]

This proof is the miniature version of the high-dimensional rule. The fixed reference map creates a Jacobian stretch term, and the divergence theorem turns that stretch-plus-carry package into boundary flux.

The high-dimensional Leibniz rule

The ordinary one-dimensional Leibniz rule has endpoint terms. Reynolds transport theorem is the same idea for a moving high-dimensional domain. If a domain \(\Omega(\theta)\) moves with boundary normal velocity \(v_\theta\cdot n\), then

\[ {d\over d\theta}\int_{\Omega(\theta)} f(x,\theta)\,dx = \int_{\Omega(\theta)}{\partial f\over\partial\theta}\,dx + \int_{\partial\Omega(\theta)} f(x,\theta)\, (v_\theta(x)\cdot n(x))\,dS . \]

In differentiable rendering, \(\Omega(\theta)\) is often not a literal volume in world space. It can be a region of image space, path space, primary-sample space, or ray-parameter space where a visibility decision stays constant. When geometry moves, that region changes, and the derivative gets a boundary term.

Reynolds transport theorem

Reynolds transport theorem is the continuum-mechanics name for the same moving control-volume identity. If the boundary of \(\Omega(t)\) moves with velocity \(w(x,t)\), then

\[ {d\over dt}\int_{\Omega(t)} q(x,t)\,dV = \int_{\Omega(t)}{\partial q\over\partial t}\,dV + \int_{\partial\Omega(t)} q\,w\cdot n\,dS = \int_{\Omega(t)} \left({\partial q\over\partial t}+\nabla\cdot(qw)\right)dV. \]

If the control volume moves with the material velocity \(u\), the theorem becomes the material-volume form

\[ {d\over dt}\int_{\Omega(t)}q\,dV = \int_{\Omega(t)} \left({Dq\over Dt}+q\,\nabla\cdot u\right)dV, \qquad {Dq\over Dt}={\partial q\over\partial t}+u\cdot\nabla q. \]

View Formula shape Simulation meaning
Fixed Eulerian control volume \(\frac{d}{dt}\int_\Omega q\,dV=\int_\Omega q_t\,dV\) The box stays still; flux through the boundary appears in the PDE for \(q_t\).
Material volume \(\int_{\Omega(t)}(Dq/Dt+q\nabla\cdot u)\,dV\) The region follows the material; density changes plus local expansion change total amount.
ALE / arbitrary moving mesh \(\int q_t\,dV+\int_{\partial\Omega}q\,w\cdot n\,dS\) The mesh can move differently from the material; relative flux must be tracked.
Conservation law \(q_t+\nabla\cdot F=s\) Local PDE form: cell amount changes by flux through faces plus sources.

This is why finite-volume solvers feel so natural for fluids: they are Reynolds' theorem turned into a bookkeeping rule for cell averages. It is also why differentiable rendering cannot ignore moving visibility regions: the "cell" in sample space changes, and boundary flux is part of the derivative.

How to remember the high-dimensional terms

The cleanest mnemonic is not to memorize a 3D expanded formula. Memorize the moving control-volume story:

\[ {d\over d\theta}\int_{\Omega(\theta)} f\,dx = \int_{\Omega(\theta)} \left({\partial f\over\partial\theta} +\nabla\cdot(fv)\right)dx = \int_{\Omega(\theta)} \left({\partial f\over\partial\theta} +v\cdot\nabla f +f\,\nabla\cdot v\right)dx . \]

The three expanded terms are easy to interpret:

Term Memory hook Meaning
\(\partial_\theta f\) Paint changes. The value at a fixed spatial point changes because the parameter changed.
\(v\cdot\nabla f\) The paint is carried. The domain moves through a spatially varying field, so it samples a different value.
\(f\,\nabla\cdot v\) The volume stretches. The moving map expands or compresses the local measure element.
\(\nabla\cdot(fv)\) Flux packaging. The carry and stretch terms combine into one divergence, which becomes a boundary term.

If the field \(f\) is only defined inside the moving domain and you do not want to choose a particular extension outside it, use the boundary-flux form instead: interior value change plus whatever crosses the boundary. This is why the Reynolds version feels cleaner for visibility derivatives: the boundary is the silhouette, shadow edge, image-space discontinuity, or path-space event surface.

Three pictures for one moving integral

The same derivative can be drawn three useful ways. In one dimension, new mass enters through moving endpoints. In a fixed reference chart, the changing domain is hidden in a deformation map and shows up as a Jacobian. In rendering, the boundary is often not a visible surface patch but a surface in sample space where a hit, visibility, or path class changes.

Green, Stokes, divergence, and Cauchy are one family

The unifying statement is the generalized Stokes theorem:

\[ \int_M d\alpha = \int_{\partial M}\alpha . \]

Different familiar theorems are just different choices of dimension and differential form. The philosophical slogan is: an interior derivative can be measured by a boundary integral. The even shorter slogan is \(\partial\partial M=0\), the boundary of a boundary cancels.

Theorem What is integrated inside What appears on the boundary Mental picture
Fundamental theorem of calculus Derivative \(f'(x)\) on an interval. Endpoint difference \(f(b)-f(a)\). Boundary of a 1D interval is two oriented endpoints.
Green's theorem 2D scalar curl \(\partial_x Q-\partial_y P\). Line integral \(P\,dx+Q\,dy\) around the curve. Tiny circulations inside cancel, leaving only circulation on the outer curve.
Stokes' theorem Curl of a vector field through a surface. Line integral around the surface boundary. Green's theorem lifted to curved surfaces in 3D.
Divergence theorem Divergence over a volume. Flux through the closed surface. Sources and sinks inside equal net outward flow.
Cauchy's integral theorem \(\partial_{\bar z}f\) over a region. \(\oint f(z)\,dz\). If \(f\) is holomorphic, the interior obstruction is zero, so closed contours vanish.

This is the internal connection between Green's theorem and the Cauchy-Riemann condition. Write \(f=u+iv\) and \(dz=dx+i\,dy\). Then

\[ f(z)\,dz = (u\,dx-v\,dy) + i(v\,dx+u\,dy). \]

Apply Green's theorem to the real and imaginary parts of the contour integral:

\[ \oint_{\partial\Omega} f(z)\,dz = \int_\Omega \left[ (-v_x-u_y) + i(u_x-v_y) \right]dA . \]

The Cauchy-Riemann equations \(u_x=v_y\) and \(u_y=-v_x\) make the bracket zero. So a holomorphic function is not merely "complex differentiable" in isolation; it makes the 1-form \(f(z)\,dz\) locally closed. Green's theorem then says the contour integral depends only on boundary obstructions such as holes or singularities. With no singularity inside, the closed contour integral is zero.

Cauchy theorem from Green, slowly

For a \(C^1\) complex function on a simply connected region, the proof really is just Green's theorem applied twice. Split the contour integral into real and imaginary parts:

\[ \oint_{\partial\Omega} f(z)\,dz = \oint_{\partial\Omega}(u+iv)(dx+i\,dy) = \oint_{\partial\Omega}(u\,dx-v\,dy) + i\oint_{\partial\Omega}(v\,dx+u\,dy). \]

Now use Green's theorem in the form \(\oint(P\,dx+Q\,dy)=\int_\Omega(Q_x-P_y)dA\). For the real part, \(P=u,\ Q=-v\). For the imaginary part, \(P=v,\ Q=u\):

\[ \oint_{\partial\Omega}(u\,dx-v\,dy) = \int_\Omega(-v_x-u_y)\,dA, \qquad \oint_{\partial\Omega}(v\,dx+u\,dy) = \int_\Omega(u_x-v_y)\,dA. \]

If \(f\) is holomorphic, the Cauchy-Riemann equations make both integrands zero. Therefore \(\oint_{\partial\Omega} f(z)\,dz=0\). More compactly, Wirtinger notation packages the same statement as

\[ d(f\,dz) = -2i\,{\partial f\over\partial\bar z}\,dx\wedge dy, \qquad \partial_{\bar z}f=0 \Rightarrow d(f\,dz)=0. \]

This is the exterior-calculus version: holomorphic means the 1-form \(f\,dz\) is closed, so Stokes/Green says its integral over a boundary is zero. The full Cauchy-Goursat theorem is slightly stronger because it avoids assuming \(f\) is \(C^1\) in advance, but the intuition is the same: no interior obstruction, no boundary circulation.

Exterior-calculus version of Leibniz

For a compact way to remember every moving-boundary formula, use Cartan's formula for the Lie derivative:

\[ {d\over d\theta}\int_{M(\theta)}\alpha_\theta = \int_{M(\theta)} \left(\partial_\theta\alpha_\theta+\mathcal L_v\alpha_\theta\right), \qquad \mathcal L_v\alpha = i_v d\alpha+d(i_v\alpha). \]

Then Stokes turns the last term into a boundary integral:

\[ {d\over d\theta}\int_{M(\theta)}\alpha_\theta = \int_{M(\theta)} \left(\partial_\theta\alpha_\theta+i_vd\alpha_\theta\right) + \int_{\partial M(\theta)}i_v\alpha_\theta . \]

That is the high-dimensional Leibniz rule in a compact costume. If \(\alpha=f\,dV\) is a top-dimensional volume form, the \(i_v d\alpha\) term vanishes in ordinary \(n\)-space and the motion appears as boundary flux. If you pull the domain back to a fixed reference domain, the same effect becomes the Jacobian/stretch term \(f\,\nabla\cdot v\). Same derivative, different bookkeeping.

Rendering translation: differentiating only the shader or BSDF is the interior term. Differentiating visibility, silhouettes, occlusion changes, or topology changes creates boundary terms. Edge sampling estimates those boundary measures directly; reparameterization and warped-area methods try to move or rewrite them into friendlier domains.

Tensor analysis: coordinates are bookkeeping

A tensor is best understood by what it does, not by the array of numbers used to write it. Coordinates change the components; the geometric object, its contractions, and the scalar answers it produces do not.

Same vector, two bases

\[ v = v^i e_i = v'^a e'_a, \qquad e'_a={\partial x^i\over\partial x'^a}e_i . \]

The arrow is invariant. Its numbers change because the basis changed. Contravariant vector components transform opposite to the basis.

Same differential, two coordinates

\[ df = {\partial f\over\partial x^i}dx^i = {\partial f\over\partial x'^a}dx'^a, \qquad {\partial f\over\partial x'^a} = {\partial x^i\over\partial x'^a} {\partial f\over\partial x^i}. \]

\(df\) is a covector: it eats a vector and returns a scalar directional derivative \(df(v)=v[f]\).

Coordinate change rules

Let \(J^a{}_i=\partial x'^a/\partial x^i\) and \(K^i{}_a=\partial x^i/\partial x'^a\), with \(K=J^{-1}\). Then

\[ v'^a=J^a{}_i v^i, \qquad \alpha'_a=K^i{}_a\alpha_i, \qquad \alpha_i v^i=\alpha'_a v'^a . \]

This last scalar contraction is the first invariant to memorize. Vectors and covectors transform oppositely so their pairing stays the same. In rendering terms, this is the same structural idea as "PDF times differential is invariant": components are coordinate-dependent, paired quantities are not.

Level 2 tensors: bilinear machines and linear maps

A rank-2 tensor can be stored as a matrix in a basis, but the invariant object is a map. There are several common types:

Type What it eats / returns Component rule Examples
\((0,2)\) Two vectors, returns a scalar: \(T(u,v)=T_{ij}u^iv^j\). \(T'_{ab}=K^i{}_aK^j{}_bT_{ij}\). Metric, first fundamental form, Hessian as a bilinear form.
\((1,1)\) One vector to one vector, or one covector plus one vector to a scalar. \(A'^a{}_b=J^a{}_i A^i{}_j K^j{}_b\). Jacobian of a vector field, deformation gradient, linear operators.
\((2,0)\) Two covectors, returns a scalar: \(B(\alpha,\beta)=B^{ij}\alpha_i\beta_j\). \(B'^{ab}=J^a{}_iJ^b{}_jB^{ij}\). Inverse metric, covariance-like objects in contravariant form.

Contractions are the invariant sanity checks:

\[ g(v,w)=g_{ij}v^iw^j, \qquad \operatorname{tr}A=A^i{}_i, \qquad \alpha(A v)=\alpha_i A^i{}_j v^j . \]

The component arrays change under coordinates, but these scalar results do not. When graphics code "gets tensor transforms wrong," it usually transforms every index the same way, or forgets that covariant and contravariant indices transform in opposite directions.

Higher levels: objects defined by what they eat

The clean ladder is:

Object Coordinate-free view Rendering / simulation intuition
Scalar field \(f\) A function that returns a number at each point. Density, temperature, optical depth, signed distance.
Vector \(v\) A direction-and-magnitude object; in geometry, a derivation \(v[f]\). Velocity, ray direction, displacement, infinitesimal parameter motion.

This is close to what you called "level 3: a function on functions." Strictly, finite-rank tensors are multilinear maps on vectors and covectors. Functionals and operators are higher-level objects on function spaces. They are not usually called ordinary tensors, but the same invariant attitude applies: define the object by its action, then ask how its coordinate or basis representation changes.

\[ \ell[f]=\int_\Omega k(x)f(x)\,d\mu(x), \qquad (Kf)(x)=\int_\Omega k(x,y)f(y)\,d\mu(y). \]

A pixel sensor is a functional on radiance. A transport step is an operator on radiance. A finite matrix is what an operator becomes after choosing basis functions and a measure-weighted inner product.

Tensor memory: a scalar is invariant by itself. A vector is invariant only after pairing components with a basis. A covector is invariant after pairing with a vector. A rank-2 tensor is invariant through contractions or through the map it defines. A functional or operator is invariant through what it does to functions; matrices are just chosen-basis shadows.

Matrix and operator view

Matrices are what continuous transport and simulation become after choosing basis functions, grid cells, particles, samples, or control volumes. The same question keeps coming back: is the operator dense or sparse, symmetric or not, positive definite or not, well-conditioned or not, and do we solve it exactly, iterate it, sample it, or differentiate through it?

Rendering as a linear operator

\[ L = L_e + K L, \qquad (I-K)L=L_e, \qquad L=\sum_{b=0}^{\infty}K^b L_e . \]

\(K\) transports radiance through one scattering or reflection event. The Neumann series is the infinite-bounce path expansion. Path tracing is a matrix-free Monte Carlo way to estimate entries and powers of this operator.

Simulation as a sparse solve

\[ M\ddot q + C\dot q + f_{\mathrm{int}}(q)=f_{\mathrm{ext}}, \qquad A\,\Delta q=b . \]

Time stepping, constraints, fluids, cloth, FEM solids, and diffusion usually reduce each frame or Newton iteration to a sparse linear system. The matrix is the local coupling structure of the physical model.

Rendering matrices and transport operators

Classic radiosity makes the matrix view explicit by discretizing diffuse surfaces into patches:

\[ B_i = E_i + \rho_i\sum_j F_{ij}B_j, \qquad (I-RF)B=E . \]

\(F_{ij}\) is a form-factor matrix entry: how much flux leaves patch \(j\) and reaches patch \(i\). For general path tracing, the matrix is too large, continuous, glossy, volumetric, and visibility-dependent to assemble. But the conceptual operator is still there.

Simulation matrices

Physical simulation often starts from an energy, weak form, constraint, or conservation law. Discretization turns local interactions into assembled matrices:

\[ K = \sum_e P_e^T K_e P_e, \qquad M = \sum_e P_e^T M_e P_e, \qquad A\,x=b . \]

\(K_e\) and \(M_e\) are element matrices; \(P_e\) scatters local element degrees of freedom into the global vector. This assemble/scatter pattern is the simulation cousin of accumulating path contributions into pixels.

Field theory to matrices in physical simulation

A continuum simulation is often a field theory in the practical numerical sense: unknown fields minimize an energy, satisfy a weak form, or conserve a quantity. The infinite-dimensional field is then expanded in basis functions,

\[ u(x)\approx \sum_i q_i\phi_i(x), \qquad \delta u(x)\approx \sum_i \delta q_i\phi_i(x). \]

After this choice, a field becomes a vector \(q\), a functional becomes a scalar function \(E(q)\), and a differential operator becomes a matrix or a matrix-free kernel. Variational calculus becomes ordinary calculus on the coefficients:

\[ E[u]=\int_\Omega W(u,\nabla u,x)\,dV \quad\longrightarrow\quad E(q), \qquad f_{\mathrm{int}}(q)={\partial E\over\partial q}, \qquad K(q)={\partial^2E\over\partial q^2}. \]

Solvers are part of the model

In graphics, you rarely assemble a dense inverse. You exploit structure: sparsity, locality, symmetry, positive definiteness, low rank, hierarchy, or the ability to multiply by the operator without storing it.

Roadmap of matrix instincts

Ask about structure

Is the operator sparse or dense? Local or global? Symmetric? Positive definite? Conservative? Does it have a null space from constants, rigid modes, gauge freedom, or disconnected components?

Ask about spectrum

Eigenvalues tell you stability, diffusion, convergence rate, and conditioning. A spectral radius below one makes a Neumann series converge; a large condition number makes iterative solves crawl.

Ask about discretization

Which measure became each vector entry: area, volume, solid angle, time, wavelength, path length, element mass, or pixel area? Missing weights often show up as a nonsensical matrix.

Ask about adjoints

Forward operators push state to observations. Transposes pull sensitivity back. This is the common language of sensor importance, inverse problems, differentiable rendering, and simulation control.

Measure view: a matrix is an operator plus a choice of basis and inner product. Change the measure or basis and the entries, transpose, symmetry, and adjoint can change. This is why mass matrices matter in FEM and why rendering PDFs must be compared with respect to the same measure.

Photon mapping and density-estimator families

There is no single canonical count, because many photon methods are hybrids. A useful working taxonomy has about twenty families: classic surface photon maps, progressive variants, unbiased or debiased variants, bidirectional merging methods, volumetric point/beam/plane/surface estimators, transient variants, and learned or hardware-oriented reconstructions.

Short answer: the old boundary was "path tracing samples complete paths; photon mapping stores light subpaths and blurs them." The modern boundary is softer. Photon mapping, VCM, UPBP, unbiased photon gathering, and photon surfaces can all be described as path-space estimators with different sampling supports, correlations, and reconstruction kernels.

\[ L_o(x,\omega_o) \approx {1\over N\,\pi r^2} \sum_{j:\|x-x_j\|

Classical photon mapping replaces a point query for incident radiance with a finite kernel query over nearby light particles. That kernel is the source of its practical strength and its finite-radius bias. Shrink the radius carefully and it can be consistent; replace or debias the density estimate and it can become unbiased in expectation, usually with more variance.

Why photon mapping and path tracing blur together

A camera tracer, a light tracer, a bidirectional tracer, and a photon mapper can all generate the same physical path. The difference is the path-space chart and the estimator support. Path tracing usually samples a complete path and evaluates a point contribution. Photon mapping samples light subpaths, stores them, and lets many camera subpaths query them through a finite support. VCM makes this explicit: vertex connection is BDPT-style exact connection; vertex merging is photon density estimation; MIS combines them in one path-space framework.

From the "photon surface" perspective, an estimator is less about whether you call it PM or PT and more about which geometric support is intersected by which other support. NEE can be read as an analytic, zero-radius connection to a light-sampling manifold. A photon gather is a finite-radius ball. A beam query intersects a camera ray with a light segment. Photon planes and surfaces keep increasing the support dimension so the estimator hits more difficult path families.

Photon primitives with multiple camera intersections

Suppose a light-side sample creates a photon primitive by sweeping angular parameters, for example a photon sphere. A camera ray can miss it, touch it once, or pass through it twice. Those outcomes are not a problem for Monte Carlo; they just mean that one sampled primitive induces a random finite set of measurement roots.

The clean modeling choice is:

\[ U\sim p_U(u), \qquad H(U)=\{h_1,\ldots,h_{K(U)}\}, \qquad \widehat L(U)= \sum_{h\in H(U)} {C(h,U)\over \lambda(h)} . \]

Here \(U\) is the sampled photon primitive, \(H(U)\) is the set of intersections with the camera ray, \(C\) is the throughput or contribution at that root, and \(\lambda\) is the induced density of roots with respect to the measurement/path measure. In photon-surface derivations, \(\lambda\) is where the surface measure, root multiplicity, and coarea/Jacobian factors live. It is not usually an equal-probability choice among roots.

Question All-hit interpretation Selected-hit interpretation
The primitive misses the camera ray. \(K=0\); the sampled primitive contributes zero. No selectable root; contribution is zero unless another fallback is sampled.
The primitive has one tangent root. Score the root if it appears numerically; exact tangency is usually a measure-zero boundary. Selection probability is one, but numerical robustness matters.
The primitive has two roots. Score both roots. They are two path/measurement samples induced by one primitive. Choose one root with known probability, often \(1/2\), and multiply by the inverse probability.
Which MIS model is this? A compound sample, or a random-count multi-sample after unfolding \((U,\mathrm{root})\). A one-sample model on the augmented space \((U,J)\).
Which is usually better? Lower variance when all roots are cheap and few. Useful when enumerating all roots is expensive or unbounded.

For the photon sphere example: model the sphere as one sampled primitive that induces \(K\in\{0,1,2\}\) camera-ray roots. If you evaluate all roots, it is an all-hit estimator and there is no "pick one with equal probability" step. If you intentionally pick one root, then yes, it becomes a one-sample selected-root estimator and the \(1/K\) selection probability must be included in the density or compensated by a factor \(K\).

Photon primitive zoo and estimator supports

A photon primitive is not just a geometric doodle. It is a sampled support with a measure, a query rule, and an induced path density. The same visual shape can be used as a biased kernel, a consistent shrinking-radius estimator, or an unbiased path-space estimator if the support measure and MIS densities are derived exactly.

\[ \text{sample primitive }P(U), \qquad H(U)=Q\cap P(U), \qquad \widehat I(U)= \sum_{h\in H(U)} {C(h,U)\over p_H(h)} . \]

\(Q\) is the camera query object: a point, surface vertex, ray segment, beam, time bin, or sensor manifold. Photon surfaces are powerful because they choose \(P(U)\) so that difficult path dimensions are integrated analytically rather than found by rare random coincidences.

What is still useful about the name "photon mapping"?

The practical boundary

Photon methods usually trace from lights, store or reuse subpaths, and answer many camera queries with one light sample. This creates correlation between pixel estimates and tends to introduce kernel, cache, or reconstruction choices.

The theoretical boundary

The hard boundary mostly dissolves in path space. PM, PT, BDPT, VCM, and UPBP differ by how they sample, connect, merge, and weight path manifolds under a common measure.

When PM-like support wins

Caustics, SDS paths, small light paths viewed through specular chains, dense multiple scattering, and many-query reuse all benefit from storing light transport and gathering it later.

When PT-like support wins

Directly visible illumination, high-frequency visibility at the camera, easy BSDF sampling, and low-storage streaming renderers often prefer independent or lightly correlated path samples.

Measure view: photon density estimation is a change from a delta measurement to a finite-support measurement. VCM and UPBP are important because they compare exact connections, finite-radius merges, beams, and paths under compatible measures. Once you can write those PDFs, PM and PT are no longer separate religions; they are sampling strategies.

Physical simulation method map

Simulation methods are mostly choices about representation: grid, particle, mesh, rigid body, constraint graph, modal basis, or hybrid transfers between them. Each representation makes some conservation laws, topology changes, collisions, and solver structures easy, and others painful.

Eulerian grid

\[ {\partial u\over \partial t} +(u\cdot\nabla)u = -{1\over\rho}\nabla p + \nu\nabla^2u + f, \qquad \nabla\cdot u=0 . \]

Fields live on grid cells. Excellent for pressure projection, smoke, fire, diffusion, and stable large-scale flow.

Lagrangian mesh or particles

\[ M\ddot q + C\dot q + f_{\mathrm{int}}(q)=f_{\mathrm{ext}}, \qquad K\phi_i=\omega_i^2 M\phi_i . \]

State moves with material points. Excellent for solids, cloth, contact-rich objects, and modal vibration or sound.

Audio and modal analysis

Your instinct is right: if the goal is physically meaningful, high-definition impact sound, FEM-style modal analysis is usually the serious tool. The core solve is a generalized eigenproblem:

\[ K\phi_i=\omega_i^2 M\phi_i, \qquad u(x,t)\approx \sum_i a_i(t)\phi_i(x). \]

The eigenvectors \(\phi_i\) are vibration modes and the eigenvalues determine resonance frequencies. Mass-spring and PBD can produce plausible motion, but their spectra are usually not reliable enough for measured-looking audio. For sound, the geometry, material parameters, boundary conditions, damping, contact impulse, and radiation model all matter.

Simulation map: use grids when fields and projections dominate, meshes when material coordinates and constitutive laws matter, particles when topology change and free surfaces matter, rigid bodies when deformation is irrelevant, and reduced/modal bases when many repeated solves or audio-rate response make full resolution too expensive.

Wave optics, quantum amplitudes, and coherence

Ray optics is the high-frequency limit where phase is usually averaged away. Holography, diffraction, speckle, polarization, quantum erasers, and coherent sensors live one level lower: amplitudes add first, then intensities or probabilities are formed.

Classical wave optics

\[ U(x,t)=A(x)e^{i\phi(x)}e^{-i\omega t}, \qquad I(x)\propto |U(x)|^2 . \]

\(U\) is a complex field amplitude. The detector usually responds to time averaged intensity, so phase has to be converted into an intensity modulation by interference.

Quantum amplitude

\[ \psi(x)=\psi_1(x)+\psi_2(x), \qquad P(x)=|\psi(x)|^2 . \]

\(\psi\) is a probability amplitude. The Born rule squares the total amplitude, so alternatives interfere unless some other degree of freedom marks them as distinguishable.

The tiny wave-optics kit

The simplest scalar wave picture is already enough to explain most holography algebra. A monochromatic field satisfies a Helmholtz-style equation,

\[ (\nabla^2+k^2)U=0, \qquad k={2\pi\over\lambda}, \]

and free-space propagation can be viewed as summing phase-delayed secondary contributions. In paraxial optics this becomes Fresnel propagation; in the far field it becomes Fourier optics. The important rendering-theory translation is: a coherent sensor integrates complex amplitudes, while an ordinary radiance sensor integrates intensities after phase has effectively been marginalized.

Holography and quantum eraser: same coherence slot

In holography, the unknown object wave \(O\) is mixed with a known reference wave \(R\). If their polarization states are \({\bf e}_O\) and \({\bf e}_R\), an ideal intensity measurement contains

\[ I = |O{\bf e}_O+R{\bf e}_R|^2 = |O|^2+|R|^2 +2\operatorname{Re}\!\left[ O R^*({\bf e}_R^*\!\cdot{\bf e}_O) \right]. \]

The last term is the useful term. It stores relative phase as visible fringes. If the fields are mutually incoherent, the time average kills it. If the polarizations are orthogonal, the dot product kills it. Off-axis holography does not change this algebra; it gives the cross term a spatial carrier so it can be separated from the DC terms in Fourier space.

In a two-path quantum eraser, the marker state plays the same mathematical role as the polarization or coherence overlap. A simple entangled state is

\[ |\Psi\rangle = \psi_1(x)|m_1\rangle + \psi_2(x)|m_2\rangle . \]

If the marker is ignored, the position probability is

\[ P(x) = |\psi_1(x)|^2+|\psi_2(x)|^2 +2\operatorname{Re}\!\left[ \psi_1(x)\psi_2^*(x)\langle m_2|m_1\rangle \right]. \]

So yes, your intuition is the right one: the non-orthogonal overlap is the slot where fringes live. Orthogonal marker states mean perfect which-path information is available, so the unconditioned interference term disappears. Non-orthogonal marker states leave partial coherence and partial visibility.

\[ V \le {2|a_1a_2|\over |a_1|^2+|a_2|^2} |\langle m_2|m_1\rangle|. \]

The eraser step is a conditional measurement. If we project the marker onto an eraser state \(|e\rangle\), the conditional pattern becomes

\[ P_e(x) \propto \left| \psi_1(x)\langle e|m_1\rangle + \psi_2(x)\langle e|m_2\rangle \right|^2 . \]

A complementary eraser outcome often gives anti-fringes. Summing the fringe and anti-fringe subensembles removes the pattern again, which is why the quantum eraser cannot be used for signaling. The similarity to holography is the coherence algebra; the difference is that holography records a classical field interference pattern, while quantum erasure sorts conditional detection probabilities in an entangled state.

Quantum toolkit for rendering intuition

The useful mental split is not "quantum is random." Classical Monte Carlo is random too. The quantum difference is that alternatives are represented by complex amplitudes, and probabilities appear only after a measurement rule squares or traces them. Indistinguishable alternatives add as amplitudes; distinguishable alternatives add as probabilities.

\[ |\psi\rangle=\sum_i \alpha_i |i\rangle, \qquad P(i)=|\alpha_i|^2, \qquad \sum_i|\alpha_i|^2=1. \]

The rule that removes many quantum myths

A good operational rule is: add amplitudes for alternatives that the final measurement cannot distinguish; add probabilities for alternatives that are marked by the detector, environment, polarization, time bin, path label, or another stored degree of freedom. The boundary is not philosophical. It is encoded by inner products between the states that carry the label.

\[ P = \begin{cases} \left|\sum_i A_i\right|^2, & \text{indistinguishable alternatives},\\[4pt] \sum_i |A_i|^2, & \text{perfectly distinguishable alternatives}. \end{cases} \]

Partial distinguishability lives between those two cases. In a two-path density matrix,

\[ \rho= \begin{pmatrix} p_1 & c \\ c^* & p_2 \end{pmatrix}. \]

The diagonal entries \(p_1,p_2\) behave like ordinary probabilities. The off-diagonal entry \(c\) is the coherence slot. If \(c=0\), the alternatives act like a classical mixture. If \(c\neq0\), interference can appear in a measurement basis that recombines the alternatives. Entangling a path with a marker or environment reduces the visible coherence by an overlap factor:

\[ c_{\mathrm{visible}} \propto \psi_1\psi_2^*\langle m_2|m_1\rangle . \]

This is the bridge to the quantum eraser: orthogonal marker states make the unconditioned fringes disappear; measuring the marker in another basis can sort the data into conditional subensembles where fringes return. The total unsorted data still has no signaling pattern because the complementary subensembles cancel.

Hilbert-space bookkeeping and measurement

The compact quantum bookkeeping object is not a point in ordinary phase space. It is a vector in a complex Hilbert space, or more generally a density matrix. Choosing a basis is like choosing coordinates: useful, but not the physical object itself.

\[ |\psi\rangle=\sum_i \alpha_i |i\rangle, \qquad \alpha_i=\langle i|\psi\rangle, \qquad \langle\phi|\psi\rangle=\sum_i \phi_i^*\psi_i. \]

A unitary change of basis rotates the coordinate description while preserving inner products. That is why interference is basis-dependent in appearance but invariant in prediction: the detector decides which basis is being queried.

\[ \tilde \alpha = U^\dagger \alpha, \qquad U^\dagger U=I, \qquad |\langle\phi|\psi\rangle|^2 \text{ is unchanged.} \]

That last inequality is the non-classical part in one line. It is not just measurement noise from a bad instrument. This is one reason Wigner-style phase-space pictures are useful but also dangerous: they look like distributions, yet they are not always probability measures.

Quantum path integrals versus rendering path integrals

Rendering path integrals usually add energy-like contributions over path space:

\[ I=\int_{\mathcal P} f(\bar x)\,d\mu(\bar x). \]

Quantum path integrals add complex phase amplitudes first, then square at measurement:

\[ A(b,a)=\int_{\mathcal P_{a\to b}} e^{iS[\bar x]/\hbar}\,\mathcal D\bar x, \qquad P(b|a)=|A(b,a)|^2. \]

Phase cancellation is the major difference from ordinary radiance transport. In the high-frequency limit, stationary-phase paths reinforce; nearby paths cancel less, distant phase-winding paths cancel more, and geometric rays emerge as the stable limit. That is why the notation can look like rendering path space while the estimator intuition is very different: the integrand is not a nonnegative contribution but a rotating complex amplitude.

Quantum phase space versus radiance

It is tempting to say that quantum optics just replaces radiance with a sharper light field. The more careful statement is: radiance is a nonnegative energy density over ray phase space in the geometric-optics limit; quantum and coherent wave optics often need correlation functions or quasi-probabilities.

\[ J(x_1,x_2)=\langle U(x_1)U^*(x_2)\rangle, \qquad W(x,k)=\int J(x+s/2,x-s/2)e^{-ik s}\,ds. \]

\(J\) is the mutual intensity: it says how coherent two points of the field are. \(W\) is the Wigner distribution, a phase-space representation. In smooth, high-frequency, mostly incoherent limits it behaves radiance-like. In genuinely coherent or quantum regimes it can oscillate, encode phase, and even go negative.

Partial coherence is where wave optics meets Monte Carlo

A fully coherent model propagates one complex field \(U\). A fully incoherent ray model adds intensities. Partial coherence sits between them, but it is not an awkward interpolation: under the usual finite-power and compactness assumptions, the mutual intensity \(J\) is a positive integral operator and therefore has a coherent-mode decomposition. With the inner product defined against the optical-domain measure \(d\mu\),

\[ \int J(x_1,x_2)u_n(x_2)\,d\mu(x_2) =\lambda_n u_n(x_1), \qquad J(x_1,x_2) =\sum_n \lambda_n u_n(x_1)u_n^*(x_2), \quad \lambda_n\ge 0. \]

Let \(H\) be a linear wave-propagation operator: Fresnel propagation, an angular spectrum step, a lens, or any composition of linear optical elements. Then

\[ J_{\mathrm{out}}=H J_{\mathrm{in}}H^\dagger, \qquad I_{\mathrm{out}}(y) =J_{\mathrm{out}}(y,y) =\sum_n\lambda_n\left|(Hu_n)(y)\right|^2. \]

For finite total power this exposes an exact Monte Carlo estimator. Put \(\Lambda=\sum_n\lambda_n\) and sample a mode index \(N\sim q_n=\lambda_n/\Lambda\). Then

\[ I_{\mathrm{out}}(y) =\Lambda\,\mathbb E_{N\sim q} \left[\left|(Hu_N)(y)\right|^2\right]. \]

Each sample is one coherent wave propagation: amplitudes within that mode still interfere before squaring. Monte Carlo is only selecting members of the incoherent mixture. Randomly selecting rays inside a coherent mode and adding their intensities would discard the phase cross terms and solve a different physical problem.

Measure-theory connection: this is the spectral decomposition of a positive operator on \(L^2(X,\mu)\). The mode orthogonality, eigenproblem, and kernel density all depend on the named measure \(\mu\). A coordinate change must transform them together, just as a PDF and its base measure must transform together.

Etendue: projected measure becomes a mode budget

The same projected surface-direction element used by radiometry is optical etendue:

\[ dG =n_r^2\,|\mathbf n\!\cdot\!\omega|\,dA\,d\omega, \qquad N_{\mathrm{spatial}} \sim {G\over \lambda_0^2} \quad\text{per polarization}. \]

Here \(n_r\) is refractive index and \(\lambda_0\) is vacuum wavelength. Passive lossless optics preserve etendue in the geometric-optics limit, and diffraction says that one independent spatial mode occupies roughly a vacuum-wavelength-squared cell of this refractive-index-weighted phase space. Exact factors depend on aperture, polarization, boundary conventions, and what counts as an independent channel; the scaling is the important fact.

This gives the brightness theorem a wave interpretation. A passive optical system can rotate, shear, clip, or discard phase-space cells, but it cannot squeeze many mutually incoherent input modes into one output mode without loss. A nearly diffraction-limited laser is already close to a single spatial mode, which is why its focusing behavior differs from an extended incoherent emitter without contradicting radiance conservation.

\[ L_\nu={2h\nu^3\over c^2}\,\bar n_\nu \qquad \text{(vacuum, two polarizations)}. \]

Spectral radiance can therefore be read as photon energy times a density of optical modes times the mean occupation \(\bar n_\nu\) of each mode. This is the cleanest bridge from classical radiometry to quantum optics: etendue counts available modes; the state says how those modes are occupied and correlated.

A research paper as four maps

Most modern imaging papers become much easier to read when separated into source, propagation, measurement, and inference. In quantum notation the first three fit in one line:

\[ \rho \xrightarrow{\;\mathcal E_\theta\;} \rho_\theta \xrightarrow{\;\{E_y\}\;} p(y\mid\theta) =\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[E_y\mathcal E_\theta(\rho)\right], \qquad \widehat\theta=R_\phi(y). \]

The classical versions replace \(\rho\) by radiance \(L\), a field \(U\), or mutual intensity \(J\); replace the quantum channel \(\mathcal E_\theta\) by a transport or wave operator; and replace the POVM by the camera response. The reconstruction \(R_\phi\) is a fourth, computational layer. A learned inverse can be novel without defining a new radiometric quantity, and a new detector encoding can be novel without changing propagation physics.

Photon statistics: physical noise is not Monte Carlo noise

Mean radiance or intensity is only a first-order statistic. Photon-counting papers often care about the normalized second-order statistic

\[ g^{(2)}(0) ={\langle n(n-1)\rangle\over\langle n\rangle^2}, \qquad \begin{array}{c|c} \text{ideal coherent state} & g^{(2)}(0)=1\\ \text{single-mode thermal light} & g^{(2)}(0)=2\\ \text{ideal one-photon state} & g^{(2)}(0)=0 \end{array} \]

\(g^{(2)}(0)>1\) is bunching; \(g^{(2)}(0)<1\) is antibunching. In the standard photodetection model, antibunching is a nonclassical signature. Notice what this statistic asks: not only how many detections occur on average, but whether detections arrive together more or less often than independent Poisson events.

Holography and coded imaging roadmap

Strictly, holography means recording enough interference information to recover a complex wavefront. In the broader computational-imaging sense, it sits next to cameras that encode depth, arrival time, epipolar consistency, velocity, coherence, polarization, or angular samples. At this granularity there are about 23 holography families, and about 34 families if time-of-flight, gated, Doppler, epipolar, structured-light, and transient cameras are included as adjacent coded-imaging methods.

Hologram measurement

\[ I(x)=|O(x)+R(x)|^2 =|O|^2+|R|^2+O R^*+O^*R . \]

\(O\) is the unknown object wave and \(R\) is a known reference wave. The cross terms carry phase. Inline holography overlaps the orders; off-axis holography tilts \(R\) so those terms move away from the DC term in Fourier space.

CW-ToF phase measurement

\[ z={c\,\phi\over 4\pi f_m} \pmod {c\over 2f_m}, \qquad f_D \approx {2v_r\over \lambda}. \]

Continuous-wave time-of-flight measures the phase delay of a modulated light signal, not the optical phase of the wave itself. Doppler methods measure a frequency shift, which turns radial velocity into another sensor coordinate.

Epipolar methods: rendering and sensing

Epipolar methods exploit a geometric constraint: a camera ray and a light or projector ray lie in a shared plane. That plane becomes a one-dimensional structure in the image, so work can be concentrated along lines instead of spread across all pixels and all depths.

\[ \Pi = \mathrm{span}(C, L, x), \qquad \Pi\cap\mathrm{image} = \ell_{\mathrm{epi}} . \]

For single scattering from a point or small light, all camera samples whose rays lie in the same epipolar plane share similar visibility structure. The scattering signal changes sharply at shadow/depth discontinuities, but is often smooth along the shaft between those discontinuities.

Measure view: epipolar methods are a reparameterization of path families. Instead of treating every pixel-depth sample independently, they group samples by the light-camera plane. In rendering this reduces integration work; in sensing it rejects paths that do not satisfy the expected geometric constraint.

Transient and time-resolved media

Steady-state rendering integrates over arrival time. Transient rendering keeps time as part of the measurement. The sensor records not just how much light arrived, but when it arrived.

Steady-state measurement

\[ I_j=\int_{\mathcal P} f_j(\bar x)\,d\mu(\bar x). \]

All photons that contribute to pixel \(j\) are integrated together, regardless of travel time.

Transient measurement

\[ I_j(t)= \int_{\mathcal P} f_j(\bar x)\, W(t-\tau(\bar x))\,d\mu(\bar x). \]

\(W\) is the sensor time gate or impulse response. \(\tau(\bar x)\) is the path's time of flight.

\[ \tau(\bar x) = \sum_i { \eta_i\,\|x_{i+1}-x_i\| \over c} + \sum_k \tau_{\mathrm{delay},k}. \]

The first term is geometric travel time, slowed by index of refraction \(\eta_i\). Extra delay terms can model fluorescence, phosphorescence, sensor response, or other time-dependent material effects.

Why media are hard in transient state

Participating media already add random distances and scattering directions. In transient rendering, those choices also determine the time bin. The estimator is now sparse in a larger space: image position, path vertices, wavelength, and arrival time.

Measure view: transient rendering adds \(dt\) to the measurement space. A path no longer only has a throughput; it also has a travel time. The camera response decides whether that path contributes to an early bin, a late bin, or the ordinary steady-state image after integrating over all \(t\).

Null-scattering path space

Yes: the null-space view treats tentative distance samples as vertices of an augmented path. They are like virtual bounces, except null vertices do not represent physical scattering. They preserve direction, position the estimator in a common measure, and disappear when marginalized.

\[ \sigma_{\mathrm{maj}}(x) = \sigma_a(x)+\sigma_s(x)+\sigma_n(x), \qquad \sigma_n(x)=\sigma_{\mathrm{maj}}(x)-\sigma_t(x). \]

The majorant medium is easier to sample than the original heterogeneous medium. Every tentative event sampled from \(\sigma_{\mathrm{maj}}\) is then classified:

\[ P(\mathrm{absorb}\mid x)={\sigma_a(x)\over\sigma_{\mathrm{maj}}(x)}, \qquad P(\mathrm{scatter}\mid x)={\sigma_s(x)\over\sigma_{\mathrm{maj}}(x)}, \qquad P(\mathrm{null}\mid x)={\sigma_n(x)\over\sigma_{\mathrm{maj}}(x)}. \]

A null event has a degenerate phase function:

\[ p_n(\omega_o\mid\omega_i)=\delta(\omega_o-\omega_i). \]

That is the formal version of "nothing happened": the path got an extra vertex, but the ray did not turn, emit, absorb, or physically scatter.

How this relates to the familiar trackers

Delta / Woodcock tracking

Sample tentative vertices from \(\sigma_{\mathrm{maj}}\). At each one, either classify it as null and continue, or classify it as real and stop the shadow ray with a zero transmittance sample. If the ray reaches the endpoint first, return one.

\[ \widehat T_{\Delta} = \begin{cases} 1, & \text{no real event before } b,\\ 0, & \text{real event before } b. \end{cases} \]

Ratio tracking

Use the same tentative vertices, but do not flip a real/null coin. Multiply the expected survival probability at each tentative event:

\[ \widehat T_{\mathrm{ratio}} = \prod_i {\sigma_n(x_i)\over\sigma_{\mathrm{maj}}(x_i)} = \prod_i \left(1-{\sigma_t(x_i)\over\sigma_{\mathrm{maj}}(x_i)}\right). \]

In path-space language, both are sampling the same augmented object. Delta tracking samples event labels stochastically; ratio tracking analytically averages over the label choice at each tentative event. Residual ratio tracking, decomposition tracking, spectral tracking, and progressive null tracking are different ways of choosing the artificial medium, control terms, wavelengths, or majorants so that the augmented paths have lower variance.

The practical payoff: once null vertices are part of the path, a renderer can write down a PDF for a path that includes them. That turns rejection-style medium sampling into an ordinary path sampling technique, which can be compared with other techniques and combined using MIS.

Related modern terminology

Most modern papers are not inventing new radiometric units. They are naming new path measures, estimator states, learned fields, or derivative quantities.

Null collision / null scattering A fictitious event that changes neither direction nor energy. A sampling device that turns a heterogeneous medium into an easier majorant process.
Virtual bounce An intuitive name for a null vertex inserted along a ray. Useful mental model, as long as you remember it is not physical scattering.
Pure-forward scattering A collision with a delta phase function \(p(\omega_o|\omega_i)=\delta(\omega_o-\omega_i)\). Another way to describe a null event: the ray continues unchanged.

Homogenized medium The original medium plus null particles so the total event rate is easier to sample. Physical medium is unchanged after null events are marginalized.

Flight age The distance since the previous real collision or boundary origin. A hidden state variable needed by many non-exponential models.

Sigma-algebra The measurable subsets \(\mathcal F\), closed under complement and countable union. It says which events are legal to assign probability or measure to.
Power set The collection of all subsets of \(\Omega\). Fine for finite/discrete sample spaces; too ambitious for continuous geometry with sane length or probability.
Borel sets The sigma-algebra generated by open sets. Contains the ordinary intervals, boxes, cones, patches, pixels, and limits used in rendering formulas.

Nonmeasurable set A subset of \(\Omega\) that is not in the chosen \(\mathcal F\). It is not assigned size by this measure; it explains why \(\mathcal F\) exists in continuous spaces.

Event A measurable subset \(A\in\mathcal F\). A probability is the measure of an event: \(\Pr(A)=P(A)\).
Base measure The measure \(d\mu\) that defines the target integral. Examples: length, area, solid angle, volume, counting measure, or path measure.
Probability measure A normalized measure \(P\) with \(P(\Omega)=1\). The actual law that generates random samples.
Random variable A measurable function \(X:\Omega\to S\). Turns outcomes or path transcripts into values such as directions, distances, or contributions.
Measurable function A function whose preimage of every measurable value-set is measurable. Required before the sampled value, score, or contribution has a well-defined distribution.
Distribution / law The pushforward \(P_X(B)=P(X^{-1}(B))\). The probability measure induced on the values of a random variable.
Expectation An integral over probability: \(\mathbb E[X]=\int_\Omega X\,dP\). Monte Carlo estimates expectations by sample averages.
Lebesgue integral The integral defined by simple-function approximation and measure of level sets. The general target that includes sums, ordinary integrals, expectations, and mixed measures.
Finite measure normalization If \(\mu(\Omega)<\infty\), use \(P=\mu/\mu(\Omega)\). Turns \(\int f\,d\mu\) into \(\mu(\Omega)\mathbb E_P[f]\).
Monte Carlo estimator A sample average of a random variable whose expectation equals the target. It estimates a Lebesgue expectation; it does not redefine the integral.
Law of large numbers For integrable iid samples, sample averages converge to expectation. The convergence theorem behind unbiased Monte Carlo estimates.
Finite variance \(\mathbb E[(Y-\mathbb E Y)^2]<\infty\). Needed for ordinary \(1/\sqrt N\) error behavior and reliable variance estimates.
Density with respect to \(\mu\) A function \(p_\mu=dP/d\mu\) such that \(dP=p_\mu\,d\mu\). The PDF only has meaning after the reference measure is named.
Solid angle measure The area measure on the unit sphere: \(d\omega=\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi=dz\,d\phi\). Direction PDFs such as \(p_\omega\) are densities against this measure.
Projected solid angle \(d\omega^\perp=|n\cdot\omega|\,d\omega\). Cosine-weighted hemisphere sampling is uniform in this projected measure.
Hat-box theorem Equal-height bands on a sphere have equal surface area. The reason uniform \(z=\cos\theta\) plus uniform \(\phi\) gives uniform solid angle.
Uniform hemisphere sampling \(p_\omega=1/(2\pi)\), sampled by uniform \(z\in[0,1]\) and uniform \(\phi\). Uniform in solid angle, not uniform in \(\theta\).
Uniform \(\theta\) sampling \(\theta\sim U(0,\pi/2),\ \phi\sim U(0,2\pi)\), giving \(p_\omega=1/(\pi^2\sin\theta)\). A valid but nonuniform solid-angle sampler: poles are oversampled and the horizon is undersampled.
Cosine-weighted sampling \(p_\omega=\cos\theta/\pi\), often sampled by uniform disk projection. Moves the diffuse cosine factor into the PDF.
Coordinate PDF A density for sampled parameters such as \((\theta,\phi)\) or \((z,\phi)\). Convert it to a measure PDF before dividing the rendering contribution by it.
Coordinate chart A local naming system \(x^i\) for points and components. The chart is bookkeeping; invariant objects should not depend on the chosen names.
Vector A geometric direction, with components \(v^i\) only after choosing a basis. In differential geometry, a vector acts as a derivation \(v[f]\) on scalar functions.
Covector / one-form A linear functional on vectors, such as \(df\). Pairs with a vector to make the invariant scalar \(df(v)\).
Rank-2 tensor A bilinear map or a linear map, depending on index type. Metrics, deformation gradients, stress, and Hessians are component arrays only after a basis choice.
Tensor contraction Pairing one covariant and one contravariant index. Creates invariant scalars or lower-rank tensors, like \(\alpha_i v^i\) or \(\operatorname{tr}A\).
Functional An object that eats a function and returns a scalar. A pixel measurement, loss, weak form, or distribution is often better viewed this way.
Operator An object that eats a function and returns another function. Transport, blur, Laplacian, and rendering-equation maps become matrices after discretization.
Radon-Nikodym derivative The derivative \(d\mu/dP\) or \(dP/d\mu\) between compatible measures. The measure-theory version of PDF conversion and importance weights.
Stieltjes integral An integral like \(\int f\,dg\), where \(g\) defines the measure. If \(g\) is smooth, \(dg=g'(x)dx\); if \(g\) jumps, the measure has point masses.
Lebesgue-Stieltjes measure The measure induced by an increasing function \(g\). It makes notation like \(d(2x)\), weighted lengths, and delta-like jumps precise.
Pullback measure A measure transported back through a map, such as \(u=g(x)\). The source of Jacobians: measure in one coordinate becomes a scaled measure in another.
Dummy variable A bound integration variable whose name has no meaning outside the integral. Renaming \(x\) to \(y\) changes nothing if the domain, measure, and formula are otherwise identical.
Coordinate substitution A map \(y=g(x)\) used to rewrite the same target integral in another coordinate. Requires transformed bounds, composed function, and the Jacobian.
Density A function that only has meaning relative to a measure, such as \(f_x(x)\,dx\). Under coordinate changes the invariant object is the density, not the bare formula.
Antiderivative A function \(F\) satisfying \(dF/dx=f\) for a chosen variable. Indefinite integrals are coordinate-dependent; definite integrals can be target-invariant.
Almost everywhere A statement holds except on a set of measure zero. Changing point values does not change an ordinary integral, but can matter under a singular measure.
Null set A measurable set \(A\) with \(\mu(A)=0\). Invisible to \(\mu\)-integrals but possibly visible to another, singular, or derivative measure.
Absolute continuity \(\nu\ll\mu\): every \(\mu\)-zero set is also \(\nu\)-zero. Exactly the condition that allows a density \(d\nu/d\mu\) to exist.
Singular measure \(\nu\perp\mu\): the measures live on disjoint measurable support up to null sets. Delta lights, specular constraints, and surface measure inside volume measure are common examples.
Lebesgue decomposition A measure split into absolutely continuous and singular parts. Useful mental model for continuous PDFs plus delta or lower-dimensional components.
Product measure The measure on pairs or tuples, such as \(d\mu(x)d\nu(y)\). Path-space measures are products of vertex, direction, distance, and discrete-event measures.
Tonelli / Fubini Theorems that justify swapping integration order. Explains why camera-first, light-first, distance-first, and direction-first forms can be equivalent.
Monotone convergence If \(0\le f_n\uparrow f\), then \(\int f_n\to\int f\). Useful for increasing approximations to nonnegative transport.
Dominated convergence If \(f_n\to f\) and \(|f_n|\le h\) with \(h\in L^1\), then integrals converge. The classic condition for moving limits through rendering integrals safely.
Importance sampling Choosing \(P\) so samples land where the weighted contribution matters. Unbiased when scored by \(f/p_\mu\) and support is not lost.
Multiple importance sampling Combining samples from several techniques using weights that sum to one. Lets light sampling, BSDF sampling, BDPT strategies, and photon strategies estimate one target.
Technique PDF The density \(p_s\) for one sampling strategy, expressed against a named base measure. Must be comparable with every other technique PDF before an MIS weight is meaningful.
Partition of unity Weights satisfying \(\sum_s w_s(x)=1\) almost everywhere on the target support. The short reason MIS remains unbiased after splitting responsibility among techniques.
Balance heuristic \(w_s=n_sp_s/\sum_i n_ip_i\). Default MIS weighting; sample counts and comparable PDFs both matter.
Power heuristic \(w_s=(n_sp_s)^\beta/\sum_i(n_ip_i)^\beta\), often with \(\beta=2\). Sharper weighting that often suppresses bad techniques more aggressively.
One-sample MIS Randomly choose technique \(s\) with probability \(\alpha_s\), then sample from \(p_s\). The actual proposal is the mixture \(q=\sum_s\alpha_sp_s\); forgetting \(\alpha_s\) misweights the estimator.
Multiple-sample MIS Take \(n_s\) samples from each technique and weight each technique's partial estimator. Common in direct lighting and BDPT; changing sample counts changes the heuristic weights.
Compound sample One random object whose evaluation returns a finite set or sum of contributions. A photon primitive can intersect a camera ray several times from one sampled primitive.
All-hit estimator Enumerate every hit induced by a sampled primitive and sum the scored roots. Natural for photon spheres with 0, 1, or 2 camera-ray intersections.
Selected-hit estimator Choose one hit from a primitive's hit set using a known conditional probability. Requires inverse selection-probability weighting; often noisier than all-hit enumeration.
Induced hit measure \(\Lambda(B)=E[\sum_{h\in H(U)}\mathbf 1_B(h)]\), the expected count of generated hits in \(B\). The density of this measure is the PDF used to score all-hit primitive estimators.
Photon primitive A sampled support object such as a point, disk, ball, beam, plane, sphere, cone, or cylinder. The primitive shape, query rule, and induced hit density together define the estimator.
Support layer The geometric support an estimator integrates or intersects. Separate it from the strategy layer (PT, BDPT, VCM, UPBP) and the correlation/proposal layer (MLT, guiding).
Mixture PDF The proposal density \(q=\sum_s\alpha_sp_s\) after randomly selecting a technique. One-sample MIS can be read as ordinary importance sampling with this mixture density.
Reverse PDF The density of generating an already-built path or edge using another technique direction/order. BDPT, VCM, and path-space MIS need PDFs for unchosen strategies, not only the chosen one.
Zero-variance proposal For nonnegative \(f\), the ideal density \(p=f/I\). It makes every weight equal to \(I\), but requires knowing the answer.
Simple function A finite sum \(f=\sum_k a_k\mathbf 1_{A_k}\). The Lebesgue integral starts from value times measure of each value set.
Pushforward distribution The distribution of values \(Y=g(X)\) induced by sampling \(X\sim P\). Monte Carlo estimates the mean of the weighted value distribution.
Complex field A wave quantity \(U(x)=A(x)e^{i\phi(x)}\) with amplitude and phase. Holography is valuable because it recovers this field from intensity measurements.
Born rule Measurement probabilities come from squared amplitudes, e.g. \(P(x)=|\psi(x)|^2\). The quantum cousin of intensity-from-amplitude in wave optics.
Density matrix \(\rho\) stores diagonal probabilities and off-diagonal coherence. The right object for mixed states, partial coherence, and decoherence.
Hilbert space The complex vector space where quantum states live before choosing a basis. Changing basis changes coordinates, not the physical predictions.
Observable A Hermitian operator; its eigenvectors define sharp measurement outcomes. More structured than an ordinary sensor channel because not all observables commute.
Projector A yes/no measurement operator such as \(|a\rangle\langle a|\). A perfect subspace filter, useful for thinking about polarization and path labels.
POVM Positive measurement operators \(E_m\) with \(P(m)=\operatorname{Tr}(E_m\rho)\). A quantum detector-response model, analogous in spirit to a sensor functional.
Coherent state A laser-like state with a well-defined complex field amplitude. One reason classical holography can be described accurately by wave optics.
Wigner function A phase-space quasi-probability for a quantum state. It resembles a distribution over position and direction, but can be negative.
Mutual intensity \(J(x_1,x_2)=\langle U(x_1)U^*(x_2)\rangle\). Stores spatial coherence; its diagonal is intensity and its off-diagonal part enables fringes.
Coherent-mode decomposition \(J=\sum_n\lambda_n u_nu_n^*\), with orthogonal coherent fields \(u_n\). Turns a partially coherent field into an incoherent mixture of coherent wave propagations.
Etendue Projected optical phase-space measure \(dG=n_r^2|\mathbf n\cdot\omega|dA\,d\omega\). Its conservation is the geometric brightness theorem; \(G/\lambda_0^2\) estimates spatial mode count.
Mode occupation The mean photon number \(\bar n\) carried by one optical mode. Connects spectral radiance to quantum state statistics instead of treating a photon as a classical ray.
Quantum channel A physical map \(\mathcal E\) from input density matrices to output density matrices. Packages propagation, loss, noise, and decoherence before the detector POVM is applied.
Second-order coherence \(g^{(2)}(0)=\langle n(n-1)\rangle/\langle n\rangle^2\). Distinguishes bunched, Poisson-like, and antibunched photon arrivals with the same mean intensity.
Antibunching Suppressed zero-delay coincidences, \(g^{(2)}(0)<1\). A nonclassical photon-statistics witness, but not automatically an imaging advantage.
Commutator \([A,B]=AB-BA\). If it is nonzero, the two measurement questions need not share an ordinary joint distribution.
Quantum path integral Sum complex phases \(e^{iS/\hbar}\) over paths, then square the amplitude. Looks like rendering path-space notation, but interference and phase cancellation dominate.
Ghost imaging Image formation from correlations between detector arms, often with one bucket detector. The image is in the joint statistics, not in either marginal signal alone.
Hanbury Brown-Twiss Second-order intensity-correlation measurement, usually written with \(G^{(2)}\). Separates photon-statistics/coherence questions from ordinary field-intensity imaging.
Hologram An interference record \(I=|O+R|^2\) between object and reference waves. The cross terms carry the object wave's phase information.
Reference wave A known coherent wave mixed with the unknown object wave. Acts like the carrier that turns optical phase into measurable intensity fringes.
Coherence cross term The off-diagonal term such as \(O R^*\) or \(\psi_1\psi_2^*\langle m_2|m_1\rangle\). Fringes live here; incoherence or orthogonal marker states erase it in the unconditioned signal.
Marker-state overlap The inner product \(\langle m_2|m_1\rangle\) between which-path marker states. It controls how much two-path quantum interference survives after ignoring the marker.
Quantum eraser conditioning Projecting marker states onto a basis where path alternatives are no longer distinguished. Restores conditional fringes and anti-fringes; their unconditioned sum still has no signal pattern.
Off-axis carrier A spatial frequency introduced by tilting the reference beam. Separates DC, +1, and -1 diffraction orders in Fourier space.
Phase shifting Capturing several interferograms with known reference phase offsets. Trades multiple exposures for cleaner complex-field reconstruction.
Heterodyne holography Mixing with a frequency-shifted reference wave. Moves the signal to a beat frequency for sensitive phase or Doppler measurement.
Numerical propagation Propagating a reconstructed complex field by Fresnel or angular-spectrum kernels. Lets a digital hologram be refocused after capture.
Phase unwrapping Recovering continuous phase from values known modulo \(2\pi\). A core ambiguity in holography, QPI, ToF, and any phase-coded sensor.
Coherence gate A depth filter caused by finite source coherence length. Only path lengths close enough to the reference path interfere strongly.
CW-ToF / indirect ToF Depth from phase delay of actively modulated illumination. It measures modulation phase, not optical wave phase; multipath causes phase mixing.

1. Microfacets: three different direction densities hide in one BRDF

Material measure

A microfacet normal distribution \(D(m)\) is not, by itself, the PDF of an ordinary direction. It is conventionally normalized by projected microfacet area:

\[ \int_{\mathcal H^2} D(m)\,|n\cdot m|\,d\omega_m=1. \]

Therefore an NDF normal sampled with respect to solid angle has \(p_{\mathrm{NDF}}(m)=D(m)|n\cdot m|\). The familiar cosine is not an extra lighting factor; it converts the projected-area definition of \(D\) into an ordinary solid-angle probability.

The reflection BRDF combines that distribution with Fresnel and masking:

\[ f_r(\omega_i,\omega_o)= {F(\omega_i,m)\,D(m)\,G(\omega_i,\omega_o,m) \over 4|n\cdot\omega_i|\,|n\cdot\omega_o|}, \qquad m={\omega_i+\omega_o\over\|\omega_i+\omega_o\|}. \]

Sampling the visible-normal distribution adds another change: it conditions the facet population on what the incident direction can actually see:

\[ p_{\mathrm{vis}}(m\mid\omega_i)= {D(m)\,G_1(\omega_i,m)\,|\omega_i\cdot m| \over |n\cdot\omega_i|}, \qquad \omega_i\cdot m>0. \]

Finally, a sampled half-vector is mapped to the reflected direction. For specular reflection,

\[ d\omega_o=4|\omega_o\cdot m|\,d\omega_m, \qquad p_o(\omega_o)= {p_m(m)\over4|\omega_o\cdot m|}. \]

This is the clean reading of a microfacet sampler: \(D\) describes projected facet area, a VNDF changes the probability by visibility conditioning, and the half-vector map contributes a Jacobian. These factors answer three different questions and should not be merged mentally into one mysterious cosine.

Useful debugging invariant: ask whether the code currently holds a density per projected facet area, per normal solid angle, or per outgoing solid angle. A missing \(n\cdot m\), \(G_1\), or \(4|\omega_o\cdot m|\) usually means two of those measures were silently identified.

2. Adjoint transport is not the same claim as reciprocity

Operator direction

Write forward transport as \(L=L_e+KL\), and a sensor as the functional \(I=\langle W,L\rangle_\mu\). The adjoint \(K^*\) is defined by the chosen pairing and base measure:

\[ \langle a,Kb\rangle_\mu=\langle K^*a,b\rangle_\mu. \]

If the importance field satisfies \(\psi=W+K^*\psi\), then

\[ I= \left\langle W,(I-K)^{-1}L_e\right\rangle_\mu = \left\langle (I-K^*)^{-1}W,L_e\right\rangle_\mu = \langle\psi,L_e\rangle_\mu. \]

Forward transport asks where source energy goes. Adjoint transport asks which source states could matter to the sensor. Camera tracing is therefore an importance construction even when it is implemented with ordinary rays.

Reciprocity is a separate physical symmetry. A reciprocal scalar BSDF satisfies an appropriate interchange relation such as \(f_s(\omega_i,\omega_o)=f_s(\omega_o,\omega_i)\) under consistent direction and measure conventions. The mathematical adjoint still exists when reciprocity fails: fluorescence changes wavelength, magneto-optic media can be nonreciprocal, and shading-normal or polarization frame conventions may make the backward kernel need explicit correction. "Trace it backward" is valid only after writing the actual adjoint under the pairing being used.

3. Polarization enlarges the transported value, not the geometric measure

State ladder

Scalar radiance transports one nonnegative intensity. Polarized transport keeps more state at each \((x,\omega,\lambda,t)\); the area, angle, wavelength, and time measures can stay the same.

\[ E= \begin{bmatrix}E_x\\E_y\end{bmatrix}, \qquad C=\mathbb E[EE^\dagger], \qquad S=(I,Q,U,V)^T. \]

The coherency matrix is Hermitian positive semidefinite. Its four real degrees of freedom can be written as a Stokes vector, with \(I=\operatorname{Tr}C\) and \(I^2\ge Q^2+U^2+V^2\). The degree of polarization is \(\sqrt{Q^2+U^2+V^2}/I\).

\[ S_{\mathrm{out}}=M S_{\mathrm{in}}. \]

A Mueller matrix can describe deterministic polarization changes and depolarization. At each surface or medium event, the Stokes basis must usually be rotated between the incoming meridian frame, the scattering frame, and the outgoing meridian frame. Omitting those rotations is the polarization analogue of comparing PDFs in mismatched measures.

The normalized matrix \(C/\operatorname{Tr}C\) has the same mathematical form as a two-level density matrix. That is a genuine bridge to quantum polarization, but not a proof of nonclassical light: ordinary classical partial polarization already has this matrix structure. Quantum behavior requires claims about the source state and measurement statistics beyond mean classical coherence.

4. Spectral transport may preserve wavelength, redirect it, or change it

Spectral state

The RGB rendering equation hides wavelength inside three broad detector responses. A more general surface operator needs both incident and outgoing wavelengths:

\[ \begin{aligned} L_o(\lambda_o,\omega_o) ={}&L_e(\lambda_o,\omega_o)\\ &+\int_\Lambda\int_{\mathcal H^2} K(\lambda_o,\omega_o\leftarrow\lambda_i,\omega_i)\, L_i(\lambda_i,\omega_i)\, |n\cdot\omega_i|\, d\omega_i\,d\lambda_i. \end{aligned} \]

Ordinary elastic reflection is diagonal in wavelength: \(K\) contains \(\delta(\lambda_o-\lambda_i)\). Dispersion changes the direction as a function of wavelength but still preserves each photon's wavelength. Fluorescence and phosphorescence are different: they redistribute absorbed energy across output wavelength and often across delay time.

A spectral kernel must also declare its units. Photon spectral radiance and radiant-energy spectral radiance differ because one photon carries \(E_\gamma=hc/\lambda\):

\[ L_{\mathrm{photon}}(\lambda) ={L_{\mathrm{energy}}(\lambda)\over hc/\lambda} ={\lambda\over hc}L_{\mathrm{energy}}(\lambda). \]

Consequently, a fluorescence distribution normalized by emitted photon count is not numerically the same as one normalized by emitted radiant energy. At thermal equilibrium, emission and absorption cannot be chosen independently: the mode-resolved balance behind Kirchhoff's law makes the equilibrium radiation field a fixed point. This is the spectral version of checking that an operator preserves the right invariant measure.

5. Deterministic media solvers trade random error for discretization error

Solver spectrum

Monte Carlo is one solver family for the radiative transfer equation, not the definition of transport itself:

\[ \omega\cdot\nabla L(x,\omega) +\sigma_t(x)L(x,\omega) = \sigma_s(x)\int_{\mathbb S^2} p(\omega'\to\omega)L(x,\omega')\,d\omega' +Q(x,\omega). \]

Deterministic methods discretize space, angle, or moments and solve the resulting operator system. They have no Monte Carlo variance, but they are not automatically exact: angular truncation, mesh resolution, numerical diffusion, boundary closure, and iterative solver tolerance replace sampling noise.

Family What is discretized? Strong when Characteristic artifact or cost
Characteristics / deterministic ray marching Streaming along selected rays; spatial integration along each ray. Directions and sources are structured, visibility is important, and the field can be cached. Spatial quadrature bias, interpolation blur, and a large ray set.
Discrete ordinates \(S_N\) A fixed finite set of angular directions. Geometry is grid-like and angular resolution can be afforded. Ray effects and angular aliasing aligned with the selected ordinates.

A useful comparison axis: Monte Carlo error is random and dimension-friendly but converges slowly. Deterministic error is structured and can converge rapidly for smooth low-dimensional fields, but its phase-space cost grows sharply and its artifacts can look like real beams or blur. Hybrid methods are often strongest when the deterministic solve captures the easy bulk and Monte Carlo estimates only the hard residual.

6. Inverse rendering is limited by identifiability before optimization

Information measure

An inverse problem observes \(y=F(\theta)+\varepsilon\). Near a current parameter estimate, the Jacobian \(J=\partial F/\partial\theta\) tells which parameter changes are visible:

\[ F(\theta+\delta\theta) \approx F(\theta)+J\,\delta\theta, \qquad J\,\delta\theta\approx0 \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{locally invisible change}. \]

With Gaussian noise covariance \(\Sigma\), the local Fisher or Gauss-Newton information matrix is

\[ \mathcal I(\theta)=J^T\Sigma^{-1}J. \]

Small eigenvalues identify poorly observed combinations of parameters. A prior or regularizer can choose among those combinations, but it does not create evidence that the measurement never contained.

This gives a practical roadmap for experimental design. New views, wavelengths, times, polarizations, source patterns, or reference fields are valuable when they rotate or fill the current null space. A larger network or a better optimizer may fit the data more convincingly, but only a changed measurement operator can make an unobservable mode observable.

Breadth filter: a new concept earns space when it changes at least one of five things: the transported state, the base measure, the transport operator, the detector statistic, or the inverse problem's null space. That test separates genuinely new radiometry from a renamed implementation detail.

Multiple importance sampling

MIS is a way to combine several unbiased sampling stories for the same integral. The nontrivial part is that the weights are not arbitrary confidence scores. They are a measurable partition of the same target integral, expressed with respect to one common base measure.

Start with the target

\[ I=\int_\Omega f(x)\,d\mu(x). \]

Suppose there are \(M\) sampling techniques. Technique \(s\) draws from a probability density \(p_s\) with respect to the same measure \(d\mu\). MIS assigns a weight function \(w_s(x)\) to each technique. With \(n_s\) samples from technique \(s\), the multiple-sample estimator is

\[ \widehat I_{\mathrm{MIS}} = \sum_{s=1}^M {1\over n_s} \sum_{j=1}^{n_s} w_s(X_{s,j})\,{f(X_{s,j})\over p_s(X_{s,j})}, \qquad X_{s,j}\sim p_s\,d\mu . \]

This is not "sample with many PDFs and average the answers." It is "split the integrand by weights, then estimate each weighted integral with the technique assigned to it."

Why it is unbiased

Take the expectation of the estimator. Each technique estimates the integral of one weighted piece:

\[ \mathbb E\!\left[ {1\over n_s}\sum_{j=1}^{n_s} w_s(X_{s,j}){f(X_{s,j})\over p_s(X_{s,j})} \right] = \int_\Omega w_s(x)f(x)\,d\mu(x). \] \[ \mathbb E[\widehat I_{\mathrm{MIS}}] = \int_\Omega f(x)\left(\sum_{s=1}^M w_s(x)\right)d\mu(x). \]

Therefore the estimator is unbiased when the weights form a partition of unity on the contributing support:

\[ \sum_{s=1}^M w_s(x)=1 \quad \mu\text{-a.e. where } f(x)\ne 0, \qquad w_s(x)=0\text{ wherever }p_s(x)=0. \]

The second condition is the quiet one that prevents division by zero and prevents a technique from being assigned responsibility for a region it cannot sample.

Requirement What it means Common failure mode
Same target measure Every \(p_s\) must be a density with respect to the same \(d\mu\). Comparing area PDF to solid-angle PDF, or volume density to surface density.
Support coverage If \(f\) contributes on a set, at least one technique with nonzero weight must sample it. A guide, light sampler, or delta strategy gives zero probability to a real path family.
Partition of unity The weights satisfy \(\sum_s w_s=1\) almost everywhere on the target support. Weights are normalized over the wrong set of strategies or omit a possible technique.
No weight where no PDF If \(p_s(x)=0\), then \(w_s(x)\) must be zero or the term is undefined. Assigning a BSDF technique weight to a light path that the BSDF cannot generate.
Correct sample counts When \(n_s\) samples are used, the heuristic uses \(n_s p_s\), not just \(p_s\). Doubling one technique's samples without changing weights overcounts its region.
Technique probability In the one-sample model, the probability \(\alpha_s\) of choosing technique \(s\) is part of the PDF. Using \(f/p_s\) after randomly selecting \(s\), forgetting the outer selection probability.
All comparable PDFs The weight at \(x\) needs the densities for all techniques that could have produced \(x\). BDPT/VCM reverse PDFs or null-collision event PDFs are not computed for unchosen strategies.
Integrability Unbiasedness needs the weighted random variables to be integrable. A rare path has finite mean but huge or infinite variance, giving fireflies or unstable estimates.

Balance and power heuristics

The common heuristics are not required for unbiasedness; the partition-of-unity conditions are. The heuristics are variance choices.

Balance heuristic

\[ w_s(x) = {n_s p_s(x)\over \sum_i n_i p_i(x)}. \]

This gives each technique responsibility proportional to how much probability mass it places at the sampled point. It is the cleanest measure-theoretic default.

Power heuristic

\[ w_s(x) = {(n_s p_s(x))^\beta\over \sum_i (n_i p_i(x))^\beta}, \qquad \beta=2\text{ is common}. \]

This sharpens the winner. It often reduces practical variance by suppressing weak techniques more aggressively, while still keeping weights normalized.

The one-sample model

Many renderers do not take one sample from every technique. They randomly choose a technique \(S\) with probability \(\alpha_s\), then sample \(X\sim p_S\,d\mu\). The actual sampling density is the mixture

\[ q(x)=\sum_s \alpha_s p_s(x), \qquad \widehat I={f(X)\over q(X)}. \]

The same estimator can be written in MIS form by using

\[ w_s(x) = {\alpha_s p_s(x)\over q(x)}, \qquad {w_S(X)f(X)\over \alpha_S p_S(X)} = {f(X)\over q(X)}. \]

This is the source of many implementation bugs: the probability of choosing the technique is part of the sampling law. If you choose light sampling half the time and BSDF sampling half the time, that factor belongs in the PDF accounting.

The phrase "one-sample model" can be misleading in rendering discussions. In Veach-style MIS, it means one technique label is randomly selected for one estimator trial. It does not mean that every random object you generate must produce exactly one geometric contribution.

Multi-sample MIS Take fixed or scheduled counts \(n_s\) from each technique. \(\sum_s {1\over n_s}\sum_j w_s f/p_s\). Useful if you deliberately generate several primitive families per shading query.
One-sample MIS Choose one technique \(S\sim\alpha\), then draw one sample from \(p_S\). Ordinary importance sampling with \(q=\sum_s\alpha_sp_s\), or \(w_S f/(\alpha_Sp_S)\). Applies if you choose one primitive family or one intersection root randomly.
Compound / all-hit sample Draw one random object \(U\), then deterministically enumerate all hits \(H(U)\). Score a finite sum over hits; the random variable is the sum. This is the natural model for a photon sphere intersecting a camera ray twice.
Selected-hit sample Draw \(U\), compute \(K=|H(U)|\), then choose one hit \(J\) with known probability. Score the chosen hit with the inverse selection probability folded into the PDF. Valid but usually noisier than all-hit when there are only a few intersections.
Thinned all-hit sample Draw \(U\), then randomly keep each hit with probability \(r(h)\). Each kept hit gets an extra \(1/r(h)\) factor. Useful when a primitive can have many hits and enumeration is expensive.

A compact way to write the all-hit case is to let \(U\) be the sampled primitive parameters and \(H(U)=\{h_1,\ldots,h_{K(U)}\}\) be the finite set of induced hits. The primitive defines an induced hit measure:

\[ \Lambda(B) = \mathbb E_U\!\left[ \sum_{h\in H(U)} \mathbf 1_B(h) \right]. \]

If \(d\Lambda(h)=\lambda(h)\,d\nu(h)\) against the target hit/path measure, then scoring all hits as

\[ \widehat I_{\mathrm{all\ hit}}(U) = \sum_{h\in H(U)} {F(h)\over \lambda(h)} \]

is an ordinary unbiased Monte Carlo estimator: \(\mathbb E[\widehat I_{\mathrm{all\ hit}}]=\int F(h)\,d\nu(h)\). If you instead choose one of \(K(U)\) hits uniformly, the conditional selection probability \(1/K(U)\) must be included. In the simple case where the all-hit density for that root is \(\lambda_{\mathrm{all}}\), the selected-hit score carries the familiar extra factor \(K(U)\):

\[ \widehat I_{\mathrm{select}} = {K(U)\,F(h_J)\over \lambda_{\mathrm{all}}(h_J)}, \qquad J\sim\mathrm{Uniform}\{1,\ldots,K(U)\}. \]

Practical answer: for a photon primitive with 0, 1, or 2 camera ray intersections, evaluate all intersections when that is cheap. Treat the primitive as one random trial whose contribution is a sum over roots. Only use a one-selected-hit model if you intentionally randomize over the roots, and then include the root-selection probability in the PDF.

What is nontrivial?

The short proof can make MIS look like bookkeeping. The implementation reality is less tame: you must know which measure every technique samples, which techniques could have generated the same point or path, and which discrete choices were part of the probability.

Looks trivial Actually nontrivial Rendering example
"Just weight by the PDFs." The PDFs must be densities with respect to the same measure. Light sample \(p_A\) and BSDF sample \(p_\omega\) are incomparable until converted.
"Only the used technique matters." The weight asks how all techniques would have sampled the same \(x\). BDPT must evaluate the alternate camera/light splits of the same full path.
"A zero PDF just means low weight." Zero means impossible under that technique. The weight must be zero there. A delta specular path cannot be compared as an ordinary solid-angle density.
"Power heuristic is more correct." Balance and power can both be unbiased; they differ in variance behavior. \(\beta=2\) often damps bad techniques, but can over-trust one technique in edge cases.
"More samples from one technique just lowers noise." The sample count changes the weighting through \(n_s p_s\). Taking four light samples and one BSDF sample should not use 50/50 technique weights.
"A learned guide is just another PDF." Only if the actual guide PDF is known, nonzero on needed support, and the adaptation is accounted for. Path guiding can remain unbiased, but stale or clipped guides can create support holes.
"Reuse is MIS with old samples." Reuse needs a target weight, proposal history, visibility, shift Jacobian, and correlation story. Reservoir methods are exact only under their resampling assumptions; approximations can be biased.
"If it is unbiased, it is solved." MIS controls variance spikes but cannot guarantee low variance for every scene. Caustics, small portals, rare media paths, or huge dynamic range can still be hard.

The deep interpretation: MIS is a soft partition of the target measure. Ordinary importance sampling chooses one probability measure and divides by its density. MIS says: several probability measures can cover the same target, but the estimator must decide, point by point, how much of the target each technique is responsible for.

Path-space MIS

Direct lighting MIS is the easy case. The full power appears in path space, where many algorithms generate the same complete transport path by different stories: camera-first, light-first, a connection at vertex \(i\), a merge near a vertex, a null-collision transcript, or a reused reservoir candidate. The common object is the path \(\bar x\); each story has a density \(p_s(\bar x)\) against the same path measure.

Use case Techniques being combined Nontrivial PDF accounting
Direct lighting Light sampling and BSDF sampling. Convert light-area density and BSDF solid-angle density to the same measure.
BDPT Different camera/light subpath splits that construct the same full path. Compute alternate path densities, including reverse scattering PDFs and discrete choices.
VCM Vertex connection and vertex merging. Connection is exact path support; merging has kernel support and radius-dependent measure.
UPBP / photon beams Path, point, beam, and higher-dimensional photon estimators. Different support dimensions need compatible density and kernel normalization.
Null-scattering media Physical path samplers and augmented null-collision transcripts. Null labels and tentative distances are part of the augmented path PDF until marginalized.
Path guiding BSDF, light, and learned directional proposals. The learned proposal must expose its actual PDF and keep support for important paths.
Reservoir methods Current candidates, temporal candidates, spatial candidates, and shifted candidates. Target weights, proposal probabilities, shift mappings, and visibility may be approximate.

The most common MIS bug: the rendered image looks plausible even when the weights are wrong. Wrong weights often create energy drift, over-dark highlights, missing caustic paths, or scene-dependent bias rather than an obvious crash. The best debug tool is still units: write every PDF as "per area", "per solid angle", "per volume", "per path", or "per discrete choice" before comparing it.

Path-space setup

A path-space formulation treats a full light transport path as the integration variable:

\[ \bar x = (x_0,x_1,\ldots,x_k). \]

A common base measure is a product of vertex measures: area for surface vertices and volume for medium vertices, plus a discrete sum over path length.

\[ d\mu(\bar x) = dA(x_0) \prod_{i=1}^{k-1} d\eta(x_i) dA(x_k), \qquad d\eta \in \{dA,dV\}. \]

Path-space algorithms usually factor the contribution into scattering terms, emitted importance or radiance, visibility/transmittance, and geometry terms. One useful shorthand is

\[ G(x_i,x_{i+1}) = V(x_i,x_{i+1})\,T_r(x_i,x_{i+1})\, {D(x_i\!\to\!x_{i+1})\,D(x_{i+1}\!\to\!x_i) \over \|x_{i+1}-x_i\|^2}, \] \[ D(x\!\to\!y)= \begin{cases} |n_x\cdot\omega_{xy}|, & x \text{ is a surface vertex},\\ 1, & x \text{ is a medium vertex}. \end{cases} \]

This is not a different physical equation. It is the recursive rendering equation unfolded into a complete path and expressed with respect to a common measure.

The ambiguity you are feeling

Why are algorithms often described in path space? Because path space gives every complete transport path a common coordinate system. Path tracing, light tracing, next-event estimation, bidirectional path tracing, and Metropolis light transport can all generate the same geometric path through different sampling stories. For MIS, their PDFs must be compared against one common measure. Path space is the clean bookkeeping layer.

Then why does sampling a light position seem to add a term? Because the local rendering equation is usually written in solid angle. If you sample an emitter point by area, you have sampled \(dA\), but the local integral expects \(d\omega\). The factor \(r^2/|\cos\theta_y|\) converts the area PDF into a solid-angle PDF.

Why is it not the opposite? It can be the opposite. If you write direct lighting as an area integral over the emitter, then light-area sampling is native and BSDF direction sampling needs the conversion \(p_A=p_\omega|\cos\theta_y|/r^2\). The apparent direction of the "extra term" is chosen by the base measure of the formula you started from.

Is angle-distance the alternate space? Yes, it is a local ray-coordinate chart. Surface rendering often uses angle only, because ray intersection supplies the distance to the next surface. Volume rendering uses angle plus distance, because scattering can happen anywhere along the ray. Path space is the global vertex-coordinate chart for the same transport paths.

The trap: never compare a PDF per unit area with a PDF per unit solid angle in MIS. Convert both to the same measure first, then compare.

Implementation checklist

When an estimator feels ambiguous, annotate every PDF with its measure. That almost always reveals where the missing factor belongs.

Target measure Write the exact integral first: \(I=\int f\,d\mu\). Name whether \(d\mu\) is length, area, solid angle, volume, counting, path, or a mixed measure.
Event first A probability is \(P(A)\) for a measurable event \(A\), not usually \(P(x)\) for one point. Ask what set the probability is assigned to.
Allowed sets \(\mathcal F\) is the family of events the measure can answer. In continuous spaces, use ordinary Borel/completed sets; do not require arbitrary subsets to have a sane area, length, or probability.
Probability measure Check normalization \(P(\Omega)=1\) and additivity over disjoint events. A PDF is only one representation of this measure.
Support vs sigma-algebra Support says where a particular distribution can put mass. \(\mathcal F\) says which sets are legal to measure. They are related, but not the same object.
Random variable Separate the sampled outcome \(\omega\) from the value \(X(\omega)\). The value distribution is the pushforward \(P_X\).
Measurable score To ask for \(P(X\in B)\), \(X^{-1}(B)\) must be a measurable event. For renderers, this means path labels, hit ids, time bins, visibility flags, and contribution branches need a probability-space interpretation.
Lebesgue to MC To compute \(\int f\,d\mu\) by Monte Carlo, first rewrite it as \(\mathbb E_P[f\,d\mu/dP]\).
Finite measure If \(\mu(\Omega)<\infty\), normalize \(P=\mu/\mu(\Omega)\) and estimate \(\mu(\Omega)\mathbb E_P[f]\).
Variance check Unbiased and integrable is not enough for pleasant images. Check whether the weighted random variable has finite or enormous variance.
Sampling measure A PDF means \(dP=p_\mu\,d\mu\). If the sample was generated in another chart, convert the density before dividing by it.
Estimator weight The unbiased score is \(f(X)/p_\mu(X)\), or more generally \(f(X)\,d\mu/dP\). The units should match the original integral.
MIS target All MIS techniques must estimate the same integral. Convert every PDF to the same base measure before computing weights.

MIS sample counts Balance and power heuristics use \(n_s p_s\). If one technique takes more samples, its count belongs in the denominator.
Technique choice In one-sample MIS, include the probability \(\alpha_s\) of choosing the technique. The mixture PDF is \(q=\sum_s\alpha_s p_s\).
Unchosen PDFs The MIS denominator needs every technique that could have produced the sample, not only the technique that actually produced it.
MIS singulars Delta, specular, beam, surface, and volume strategies may live on different measures. Compare only compatible densities or treat them as separate components.
MIS adaptation Learned guides and reused reservoirs need actual proposal PDFs, nonzero support, and a clear conditioning story. Correlation is not bias, but wrong weights are.
All-hit primitive If one sampled primitive induces several intersections, the MC random variable can be the sum over all hits. This is not the same as one-sample MIS.
Selected root If you randomly choose one of \(K\) intersections, include the conditional selection probability. Uniform root selection usually adds a factor \(K\).
Induced hit density For photon spheres, beams, planes, and surfaces, compute the density of generated intersections on the measurement/path space, including root multiplicity and Jacobians.
Stieltjes differential \(d(g(x))\) names a measure. If \(g\) is smooth, replace it by \(g'(x)dx\); if \(g\) jumps, expect point-mass contributions.
Changed argument \(f(2x)d(2x)\) is not the same as \(f(x)d(2x)\). If it is a true substitution \(u=2x\), adjust the domain as well as the differential.
Dummy rename \(\int f(x)dx=\int f(y)dy\) only when \(y\) is just a renamed bound variable, not a different coordinate \(y=g(x)\).
Same target substitution For \(y=g(x)\), rewrite \(f_x(x)dx\) as \(f_x(h(y))|h'(y)|dy\). Bare \(f(y)dy\) is usually a new target.
Indefinite integral An antiderivative is with respect to a chosen variable. To compare after a coordinate change, transform the whole density, not just the symbol.
Support If \(p_\mu=0\) on a region where \(f\) contributes, the estimator cannot recover that missed energy by weighting.
Ideal proposal For nonnegative \(f\), \(p=f/I\) gives zero variance. Real importance sampling tries to approximate this without already knowing \(I\).
Lebesgue view Think "value times measure of the set where that value occurs." Monte Carlo samples a probability measure and averages the resulting weighted values.
Almost everywhere A value on a zero-measure set does not affect a smooth-measure integral. It can matter immediately if the measure has a delta or singular component there.
Singular support If a contribution lives on a lower-dimensional set, do not expect an ordinary PDF against volume or solid angle to see it.
Product measure Before swapping sample order, identify the product measure and whether Tonelli or Fubini conditions are the reason the swap is legal.
Limit exchange Pointwise convergence is not enough. Look for monotone convergence, dominated convergence, or a known bias/consistency argument.
Radiance Defined per projected area: \(L=d^2\Phi/(|n\cdot\omega|\,dA\,d\omega)\).
Projected solid angle \(d\omega^\perp=|n\cdot\omega|\,d\omega\). Using it hides, but does not remove, the cosine.
Direction PDF State whether a direction density is with respect to \(d\omega\) or \(d\omega^\perp\). A coordinate recipe is not enough.
Uniform hemisphere Sample \(z=\cos\theta\) uniformly and \(\phi\) uniformly. Do not sample \(\theta\) uniformly unless that is the intended nonuniform distribution and you use \(p_\omega=1/(\pi^2\sin\theta)\).
Uniform theta trap A constant-integrand smoke test can pass even with the wrong direction PDF. Test a cosine or another angular function to catch the measure mismatch.
Hat-box check Equal \(z\)-height bands on the unit sphere have equal solid angle. This is the geometric reason uniform \(z\) works.
Cosine hemisphere Cosine-weighted sampling is uniform in projected solid angle, or uniform on the disk projection. Its height density is \(p_z=2z\), not flat.
Coordinate Jacobian If your PDF is in \((\theta,\phi)\), convert using \(d\omega=\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi\). If it is in \((z,\phi)\), then \(d\omega=dz\,d\phi\).

Transmittance \(T_r=\exp(-\int\sigma_t ds)\). Analytic if possible; estimate the optical depth or survival process otherwise.
Generalized \(T\) Start from \(T(s)=P(S>s)\), \(f=-T'\), and \(h=f/T\). Do not assume a local memoryless \(\sigma_t\) unless the model gives one.
Ray-marching bias An unbiased \(\widehat\tau\) does not imply unbiased \(e^{-\widehat\tau}\). The nonlinear exponential needs its own estimator.
Unbiased marching Use power-series or debiased-telescoping corrections when you want march-like samples without quadrature bias.
Fast biased march Sweep \(\Delta s\), jitter, and history length. If the image changes materially, the approximation is visible.
Froxel grid Watch for light leaks and slice banding. Tune froxel depth distribution, temporal reprojection, and shadow-map resolution together.
Shadow caches Deep/opacity/volumetric shadow maps move light transmittance into a filtered lookup. Great for speed, but resolution and filtering define the bias.
Bias type Name the bias before fixing it: quadrature, kernel blur, reconstruction, temporal history, visibility filtering, model simplification, truncation, or clamping.
Consistency knob If the method is only consistent, identify the refinement parameter: step size, grid cell size, kernel radius, cache spacing, or guide update schedule.
Blur bias Photon maps, beams, transient bins, caches, and denoisers need radius/filter sweeps. Fixed blur can look converged while staying wrong.
Temporal bias Reset history, cut the camera, move a light, and test disocclusions. Reuse bias often appears only when the scene changes.
Clamping Track how much radiance is capped. Firefly clamping is a biased energy-removal operation unless a compensating estimator adds the missing tail.
Correlated samples Do not call a method biased just because samples are correlated. Check the target distribution, transition density, support, and final weight.
MLT chains Watch burn-in, normalization, acceptance rate, mutation reversibility, and autocorrelation. Sample count is not the same as effective sample count.
Path guiding Evaluate the actual guide PDF used for the sample. Keep a fallback distribution so rare but important path families never get zero probability.
Reservoir reuse For ReSTIR-style methods, decide whether the implementation is exact-resampling or approximate-reuse. Validate target weights, visibility, shifts, and Jacobians.
Moving domains If a parameter moves a boundary in image, ray, or path space, apply Reynolds-style reasoning: interior derivative plus boundary flux.
1D endpoint test Before trusting a high-dimensional moving-domain derivative, reduce it to \({d\over dt}\int_{a(t)}^{b(t)}f\,dx\). The signs should become \(+f(b)b'-f(a)a'\).
Leibniz memory Expanded moving-volume derivatives are local change, carry term, and stretch term: \(\partial_\theta f\), \(v\cdot\nabla f\), and \(f\nabla\cdot v\).
Boundary package The carry and stretch terms combine as \(\nabla\cdot(fv)\). Divergence theorem turns that package into the boundary flux \(\int_{\partial\Omega}f\,v\cdot n\,dS\).
Stokes family FTC, Green, Stokes, divergence theorem, and Cauchy contour integrals are all versions of \(\int_M d\alpha=\int_{\partial M}\alpha\).
Scalar vs oriented \(\int_\gamma\rho\,ds\) and \(\int_Sg\,dA\) use positive size measures. \(\int_\gamma F\cdot d\mathbf r\) and \(\int_SF\cdot n\,dA\) are oriented projections. Volume's oriented version is a 3-form, not a normal projection.
Cauchy link For \(f=u+iv\), Green's theorem turns \(\oint fdz\) into an area integral whose coefficients are killed by the Cauchy-Riemann equations.
Residue link If the form is closed except at isolated singularities, remove tiny disks around them. The outer contour equals the sum of the inner boundary contributions.
Weak-form link Integration by parts, Green identities, FEM weak forms, and adjoint boundary conditions are the same Stokes bookkeeping with derivatives moved between factors.
Visibility gradients Plain AD through a sampled path usually misses silhouette and shadow-boundary terms. Use edge sampling, reparameterization, or a boundary-aware estimator.
Shape derivative Tangential boundary motion is mostly reparameterization; normal motion changes which rays or paths are inside the domain.
Adjoint view For many parameters and one loss, ask whether a backward importance or differential radiance solve is cheaper than differentiating every path transcript.
Particle tallies Collision estimators score at events; track-length estimators score along segments. The same distinction appears in volume rendering.
Tensor invariant Do not trust the component array alone. Ask what scalar pairing, contraction, bilinear map, or operator action stays unchanged under coordinate changes.
Vector vs covector Vector components and covector components transform oppositely. Their pairing \(\alpha_i v^i\) is the invariant object.
Functional view A pixel, loss, weak form, or distribution often eats a function and returns a number. After discretization, that functional becomes a row vector.
Matrix measure Know what each vector entry integrates over: point value, area average, cell volume, basis coefficient, pixel sample, or path contribution.
Field to matrix A derivative, determinant, length, or weak form becomes algebra only after a basis and inner product are chosen. Track \(D\), \(M\), \(K\), \(F\), \(\det F\), and the metric they imply.
SPD check Before using CG or Cholesky, verify symmetry, positive definiteness, boundary conditions, and removed null modes.
Null spaces Pressure Poisson with Neumann boundaries has constant null space; elasticity has rigid modes; transport may have conserved modes. Pin or project them deliberately.
Conditioning If iterations stall, inspect scale, units, mesh quality, time step, penalty stiffness, and preconditioner before blaming the solver.
Matrix-free If the matrix is too dense or high-dimensional, implement \(Ax\) directly: trace paths, scatter element products, or apply local grid stencils.
Adjoint solve Differentiating \(Ax=b\) needs the implicit derivative. Reverse mode pulls gradients through \(A^T\lambda=\bar x\), not through a hand-waved inverse.
Photon kernel Always state the support radius, kernel measure, shrink schedule, and whether the estimator is fixed-bias, consistent, unbiased, or debiased.
Photon support Point, disk, ball, beam, plane, sphere, cone, cylinder, surface, and path estimators have different support dimensions. Convert their PDFs before MIS.
Primitive roots If a sampled primitive intersects the camera query several times, decide whether the estimator is all-hit or selected-root before writing the weight.
VCM / UPBP Treat connection and merging as different strategies for the same path family. MIS only makes sense after all densities are expressed in the same path measure.
Photon reuse Reusing one light subpath for many camera queries creates correlation. That can be efficient without being wrong, but it changes variance diagnostics.
Simulation representation Choose grid, mesh, particle, rigid body, or hybrid transfers based on what must be conserved, what changes topology, and what needs contact.
FEM / modal sound For physically credible impact audio, solve modes from \(K\phi=\omega^2M\phi\). Mass-spring spectra are usually only stylized.
MPM / FLIP transfers Particle-grid methods live or die by transfer quality: P2G, grid solve, G2P, dissipation, noise, and boundary coupling.
SPH kernels Check support radius, neighbor count, density error, boundary particles, and incompressibility correction before trusting free-surface behavior.
Wave optics Ask whether fields add before squaring. If yes, track phase, polarization, coherence, and detector averaging instead of only radiance.
Coherence term Fringes come from off-diagonal terms: \(O R^*\) in holography, or \(\psi_1\psi_2^*\langle m_2|m_1\rangle\) in a two-path quantum setup.
Quantum eraser Erasure restores conditional fringes by measuring the marker in a basis that stops distinguishing paths. Summing all outcomes removes the pattern again.
Density matrix Diagonal entries behave like probabilities; off-diagonal entries carry coherence. Decoherence damps those off-diagonal terms in the measurement basis.
Measurement basis Ask what the detector can distinguish. Basis changes preserve inner products, but the measurement basis determines which alternatives interfere in the recorded data.
Noncommuting questions If two observables do not commute, do not assume there is a hidden ordinary joint density over their exact values. Wigner-style pictures need this warning.
Indistinguishable alternatives Add amplitudes when the final measurement cannot tell alternatives apart. Add probabilities when a detector, marker, environment, or stored label distinguishes them.
Quantum path integral Rendering usually sums path contributions. Quantum mechanics sums complex path amplitudes first, then squares, so phase cancellation is central.
Quantum phase space Radiance is nonnegative in ray optics. Wigner functions and mutual intensity can encode phase and coherence, so they are not automatically sampleable densities.
Quantum imaging Identify whether the paper changes the source state, detector POVM, or correlation estimator. Ghost imaging and HBT live in joint statistics, not single images alone.
Partial coherence If modes are sampled, keep coherent amplitude interference inside each mode and add or sample mode intensities using the coherent-mode eigenvalues.
Etendue and modes Track projected area-angle measure before claiming stronger concentration or resolution. Diffraction gives roughly one spatial mode per \(\lambda_0^2\) of etendue.
Noise source Separate Monte Carlo variance, photon statistics, coherent speckle, and detector artifacts. They live in different layers and need different remedies.
Research contribution Mark what changed: source/state, propagation/channel, measurement, or inference. This prevents a new reconstruction network from being mistaken for new radiometry.
Quantum advantage Compare against the best classical method at equal photons, bandwidth, acquisition time, loss, and detector constraints. Low flux or coincidence counting is not enough.
Holography Ask whether the method reconstructs a complex wavefront or only estimates depth, velocity, or a path class with active modulation.
Off-axis setup Verify that the carrier separates DC, +1, and -1 orders without wasting too much sensor bandwidth.
Phase shifting Multi-shot phase recovery is clean only if the scene, sample, and reference phase stay synchronized across exposures.
Phase unwrap Any phase-coded method needs an ambiguity strategy: unwrapping, multiple wavelengths/frequencies, priors, or a shorter trusted range.
CW-ToF State the modulation frequency, ambiguity distance, demodulation samples, and multipath handling. Phase is a wrapped range code.
Gated camera Gate width is a depth filter. Wider gates increase signal but blur range; narrow gates reject paths but need more light or more exposures.
Doppler imaging Check the velocity-to-frequency mapping, sign convention, temporal sampling, and aliasing before interpreting a velocity image.
Epipolar camera The optical mask or synchronized row pair is a path-space filter. Leaks, calibration error, and finite slit width become bias.
Epipolar sampling Group camera samples by light-camera planes. Spend samples near depth and shadow discontinuities, interpolate along smoother shaft regions.
Epipolar sensing A synchronized light sheet and camera row reject ambient, multipath, and many scattered paths before reconstruction.
Transient bins Add \(W(t-\tau(\bar x))\) to the measurement. Path length controls which time bin receives the contribution.
Transient media Volume paths are sparse in space and time. Use time-aware sampling or spatio-temporal density estimation to avoid empty histograms.
Null tracking Sample from \(\sigma_{\mathrm{maj}}\), then classify tentative events as real or null using the local coefficients.
Null-space view Tentative distance samples are augmented path vertices. Null vertices preserve direction and disappear after marginalization.
Delta vs ratio Delta tracking samples event labels; ratio tracking averages over labels by multiplying \(\sigma_n/\sigma_{\mathrm{maj}}\).
Path-space MIS Convert every technique's path PDF to the same vertex measure before weighting.
Collision vs track length Collision estimators score at events; track-length estimators score along traveled segments.
Geometry factor Surface endpoint contributes a cosine; medium endpoint contributes \(1\).
Phase function No cosine term: the medium has no surface normal. Use \(\sigma_s p(\omega_i,\omega_o)\).
Diffuse emitter Constant emitted radiance implies cosine-weighted emitted flux: \(p(\omega)=|n\cdot\omega|/\pi\).
Volume emitter No emitter cosine. Isotropic emission uses \(p(\omega)=1/(4\pi)\), while receiving surfaces still project by \(|n\cdot\omega|\).
Microfacet PDF Distinguish projected NDF measure, visible-normal conditioning, and the half-vector-to-outgoing-direction Jacobian. Bare \(D(m)\) is not usually the PDF to divide by.
Adjoint vs reciprocity Derive the adjoint from the chosen pairing. Reciprocity may simplify it, but is a separate physical symmetry and can fail for wavelength-changing, nonreciprocal, or frame-dependent transport.
Polarization basis A Stokes vector needs a reference frame. Rotate into and out of each scattering plane before applying a Mueller matrix, and preserve the positivity constraints of physical polarization states.
Spectral units State whether spectra and wavelength-transition kernels carry radiant energy or photon count. They differ by \(hc/\lambda\), so their normalized PDFs differ too.
Deterministic solver bias For \(S_N\), \(P_N\), diffusion, FEM, or fixed ray marching, sweep angular order, mesh size, closure, boundary treatment, and solver tolerance. No random noise does not mean no error.
Identifiability Inspect the null or small-eigenvalue directions of \(J\) or \(J^T\Sigma^{-1}J\). Priors select an answer; additional measurements are what make an invisible parameter mode observable.
Delta events Specular/refraction chains are not ordinary densities against area or solid angle. Handle them as singular measures in the sampling strategy.

Summary: start with \(I=\int f\,d\mu\), choose a probability measure \(dP=p_\mu d\mu\), and estimate \(\mathbb E_P[f(X)/p_\mu(X)]\). The PDF only means something relative to its base measure. MIS combines several such probability measures by weights that sum to one on the target support; it is unbiased because it splits the integrand into weighted pieces, but only after every technique PDF, selection probability, sample count, and singular component is expressed against a compatible measure. A probability measure assigns probabilities to measurable events, and random variables push that measure forward to distributions over values. Expectation is itself a Lebesgue integral; Monte Carlo is a stochastic estimator for that integral after a target measure is rewritten as a probability expectation. \(\mathcal F\) is the family of legal events; finite spaces can often use all subsets, while continuous spaces use Borel or completed families because arbitrary subsets cannot all be given a sane additive length or probability. Familiar notation like \(d(2x)=2dx\) is the one-dimensional version of the same idea: changing the differential changes the measure, while changing the argument changes the function or the domain. A definite integral can be rewritten in another coordinate only by transforming bounds, integrand, and differential together; an indefinite integral is an antiderivative with respect to a chosen variable. Lebesgue theory adds the language for null sets, almost-everywhere equality, product measures, limit exchange, and singular components such as deltas or lower-dimensional path constraints. The original local rendering equation is naturally in solid angle. Uniform direction sampling means uniform solid angle: sample \(z=\cos\theta\), not \(\theta\), uniformly. Uniform \(\theta\) is a valid nonuniform sampler only if weighted by its \(1/\sin\theta\) solid-angle density. Cosine-weighted sampling is uniform projected solid angle. Path space is the global bookkeeping measure. Surface cosines come from projected area; constant diffuse radiance becomes cosine-weighted flux; phase functions and volume lights skip emitter cosines because volume points have no normal. Null-scattering path space adds virtual vertices so medium trackers have explicit PDFs; integrating those vertices away recovers ordinary transmittance. Generalized transmittance changes the survival law itself, while unbiased ray marching keeps a march-like estimator and corrects its bias stochastically. Fast biased media methods trade convergence guarantees for grid, cache, shadow-map, temporal, or model approximations. Bias can appear as blur, banding, leaks, ghosting, truncation, clamping, or a wrong physical model; correlation changes variance and effective sample count, but not necessarily the mean. Neutron transport, shape calculus, and differentiable rendering reuse the same measure logic: histories, tallies, adjoints, moving domains, boundary terms, and Jacobians. Green, Stokes, divergence, and Cauchy contour theorems are all boundary versions of the same exterior-derivative story; high-dimensional Leibniz rules can be remembered as local change plus carry plus stretch, or more compactly as boundary flux. Tensor analysis is the coordinate-free bookkeeping layer: vector and covector components transform oppositely, rank-2 tensors are invariant through the bilinear maps or contractions they define, and functionals/operators are best understood by what functions they eat. Matrix methods are the finite-dimensional operator view: transport matrices, mass and stiffness matrices, Poisson solves, adjoints, spectra, and preconditioners all preserve or approximate that measure structure. Photon mapping, VCM, UPBP, and photon surfaces are correlated path-space estimators distinguished by support dimension, kernel, storage, and PDF accounting; photon points, disks, balls, beams, planes, spheres, cones, and cylinders are support choices, while BDPT, VCM, UPBP, path guiding, MLT, and PSSMLT are strategy, proposal, or correlation layers on top. A sampled photon primitive can be a compound sample whose contribution is the sum over all camera-ray intersections, unless a selected-root model is explicitly sampled and weighted. Simulation methods choose a representation first: grid, mesh, particle, rigid body, modal basis, or hybrid transfer. Wave optics and quantum optics add amplitudes: fields or probability amplitudes add before squaring, and fringes survive only through coherent off-diagonal terms. Quantum predictions are made by pairing states or density matrices with measurement operators; Wigner and mutual intensity descriptions look phase-space-like but are not ordinary nonnegative radiance densities in coherent regimes. Coherent-mode decomposition turns partial coherence into an incoherent mixture of coherent field propagations, which can itself be estimated by Monte Carlo without discarding within-mode interference. Etendue is both projected radiometric measure and an approximate optical mode budget through \(G/\lambda_0^2\); mode occupation then connects radiance to quantum statistics. Modern imaging papers can be read as changes to source, propagation, measurement, or inference, while \(g^{(2)}\) separates photon-arrival statistics from mean intensity and from numerical Monte Carlo noise. Holography makes optical phase visible by interfering an object wave with a reference wave; a quantum eraser has a similar coherence slot through the marker overlap, but it is a conditional quantum probability story, not an optical recording. ToF, gated, Doppler, epipolar, and transient cameras instead encode range, time, velocity, or path-family coordinates through modulation and filtering. Epipolar methods restrict path families by light-camera planes; transient methods add arrival time as another measurement coordinate. Microfacet theory repeats the same measure logic at the material scale: an NDF uses projected facet area, a VNDF adds visibility conditioning, and reflection adds a half-vector Jacobian. Adjoint transport reverses the operator question under a chosen pairing; physical reciprocity is a separate symmetry. Polarization enlarges radiance into Jones, coherency, or Stokes state, while inelastic spectral transport enlarges the kernel from one wavelength to an incident-output wavelength pair. Deterministic media solvers replace random error with angular, spatial, closure, and solver errors. In inverse transport, the Jacobian and Fisher information expose parameter null directions; only new measurements can make those directions observable. The geometric Jacobian still decides where each area-angle factor appears.
